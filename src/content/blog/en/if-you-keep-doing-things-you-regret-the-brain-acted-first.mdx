## 📌 Key takeaways

> **The short answer:** We repeat things we regret less because of weak willpower and more because **separate brain circuits respond to immediate rewards**, and hunger or anger tips us further toward them. Simply **putting your feeling into one word** reduced amygdala responses, and **"if X, then Y" plans** and **precommitment** that narrows your options in advance helped.

- 💰 **Immediate rewards are valued separately.** When people chose between money now and money weeks later, emotion and reward circuits responded more to the "now" option (**Science**, 2004).
- 🏷️ **Naming the feeling calmed the amygdala.** People who picked emotion words while viewing angry faces showed less amygdala response and more activity in part of the frontal cortex (**Psychological Science**, 2007).
- 🔒 **Tying your hands reduces procrastination.** Students who set their own deadlines got better grades than those with no deadlines (**Psychological Science**, 2002).
- 🧘 **Meditation and the amygdala are still debated.** A small study reported amygdala changes after eight weeks of meditation, but a randomized trial of about 200 people found no structural brain changes (**Science Advances**, 2022).

### 💬 "I knew better, and did it again"

Why do we make the same choice in the moment even when we know we'll regret it later? We order late-night food and regret it, regret words said in anger, and put things off until we're up all night before a deadline, and regret it again. When I first wrote this post, I was reading a book on how our thinking betrays us, and I framed this as "not a matter of willpower but of order."

Around then, after putting off meditation because it felt like a big undertaking, I tried just five minutes before bed with the lights off, focusing only on my breathing, and noticed my mood swings the next morning were smaller. This time, I checked the parts where my original post only said "research shows" against the original papers.

## Deep Dive: how do regrettable actions arise in the brain?

### 1. Why can't we see the regret coming in the moment?

In my original post I explained that the **amygdala**, which handles emotion, moves first and the **frontal lobe**, which handles reasoning, moves later, creating a time lag. That the amygdala has a shortcut for picking up threat signals very quickly is well known from animal research (LeDoux, **Annual Review of Neuroscience**, 2000). But the real brain doesn't split neatly into "emotion versus reason"; many regions work together.

More direct evidence comes from research on **reward**. A Princeton University team scanned people's brains while they chose between a small amount of money now and a larger amount weeks later (McClure et al., **Science**, 2004). When an **immediate** reward was on the table, limbic regions tied to emotion and reward responded more strongly, while the lateral prefrontal cortex was more active when people chose larger rewards further in the future. That's why late-night snacks and impulse buys pull so hard.

### 2. Why do we regret more when hungry or angry?

The "hot state" I mentioned in my original post is the **hot-cold empathy gap** studied by George Loewenstein of Carnegie Mellon University (Loewenstein, **Health Psychology**, 2005). In a "hot" state of hunger or anger, that feeling drives our judgment hard, and when we're calm, we fail to anticipate how we'll act in those moments.

A UK team asked office workers to choose in advance a snack they'd eat a week later, and those who chose **while hungry** picked unhealthy snacks more often than those who chose when full (Read & van Leeuwen, **Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes**, 1998). Their current state changed even a choice about the distant future.

### 3. Does naming a feeling really change anything?

I confirmed against the original study what I wrote in my first post, that "there's research showing that naming a feeling calms the amygdala." Matthew Lieberman's team at UCLA showed 30 people photos of angry or frightened faces, and had them pick an **emotion word** such as "angry" or "scared" on some trials and a person's name on others (Lieberman et al., **Psychological Science**, 2007). When they picked emotion words, **amygdala responses dropped**, and the right ventrolateral prefrontal cortex became more active instead.

**Cognitive reappraisal**, reinterpreting something that has happened from another angle, worked similarly. When people were asked to interpret sad photos differently, the prefrontal cortex became more active and amygdala responses fell (Ochsner et al., **Journal of Cognitive Neuroscience**, 2002).

### 4. Do waiting 10 minutes, planning, and tying your hands work?

Here's how the three habits from my original post hold up against research.

- **The 10-minute rule:** I couldn't find research directly supporting the explanation that "the amygdala's arousal settles within a few minutes." Still, delaying a decision a little buys time to leave a "hot" state, so it remains a useful practice.
- **Implementation intentions:** Plans that pair a situation with an action, such as "if I get angry, I'll go for a walk instead of texting," had a medium-to-large effect on reaching goals across 94 studies (Gollwitzer & Sheeran, **Advances in Experimental Social Psychology**, 2006).
- **Precommitment:** Dan Ariely's team at MIT let students set their own assignment deadlines, and those who set deadlines got better grades than those with none, though lower than those given evenly spaced deadlines (Ariely & Wertenbroch, **Psychological Science**, 2002). Like deleting a delivery app or leaving your card at home, it's about narrowing your options in advance instead of spending willpower in the moment.

### 5. Does meditation really calm the amygdala?

In my original post I wrote that "in studies by neuroscientists at Yale, Harvard, and MIT, the brain areas for greed and reactivity were less active in meditators." Looking up the original study, what Judson Brewer's team at Yale examined wasn't a "greed area" but the **default mode network**, which switches on when we zone out and our minds wander. In experienced meditators, this network was relatively less active (Brewer et al., **PNAS**, 2011).

On the amygdala, results are mixed. A small study found that among 26 stressed adults who did an eight-week meditation program, those whose stress dropped most showed reduced amygdala gray matter density (Hölzel et al., **Social Cognitive and Affective Neuroscience**, 2010). But a larger randomized trial of about 200 people found that eight weeks of meditation didn't change brain structure, including the amygdala (Kral et al., **Science Advances**, 2022).

> So I changed "meditation calms the amygdala" to "meditation was linked to activity in the mind-wandering network, and structural changes in the amygdala aren't yet certain." The change in mood swings I felt from five minutes of breathing before bed is my own experience; research hasn't yet shown everything about why.

## The fix: three steps to fewer regrets

**Step 1. Name the feeling**
The moment an urge rises, say your current feeling in one word: irritation, anxiety, hurt. Hunger and tiredness steer judgment as much as emotions do. You can practice right below.

<NameIt client:visible lang="en" />

**Step 2. Set an "if X, then Y" in advance**
Pick one situation you often regret and decide in one sentence what you'll do in that moment, such as "If I want to send an angry message, then I'll save it as a draft and walk for 10 minutes." For more detailed planning, try the WOOP plan in [three ways to achieve your goals](/en/blog/three-ways-to-achieve-your-goals/).

**Step 3. Narrow your options in advance**
Whatever you regret doing most often, change your environment to make it harder. If late-night food is the problem, remove the delivery app from your home screen; if it's impulse buying, don't save your payment details. If you've already done something you regret, instead of blaming yourself, jot down "what signals there were at the time." That becomes material for your next plan.

## Conclusion

We repeat things we regret less because of weak willpower and more because a brain that reacts strongly to immediate rewards meets a hungry or angry moment. Fortunately, there are ways to step into that gap. Naming the feeling calms the amygdala a little, and plans made in advance and options narrowed ahead of time reduce the need for willpower. This week, pick the one thing you regret most often and save the sentence you built above in a note on your phone.

### 🙋 Frequently asked questions

#### Can repeated speech habits and emotional reactions be changed too?

Repeated reactions harden like habits, but if you learn to notice the situations and cues that trigger them and repeatedly pair them with a different action, they change bit by bit. Why speech habits and emotional reactions harden is covered next in [repeated emotional reactions and speech habits](/en/blog/emotional-reactions-and-speech-habits-are-learned-reflex-circuits/).

#### How much meditation a day is enough?

Studies usually involved tens of minutes a day for eight weeks, but even short, steady sessions are an easy place to start. Noticing when your mind wanders from your breath and coming back is the practice itself. I covered breathing and the brain in [breath is your brain's switch](/en/blog/breath-is-your-brains-switch/).
