> Willpower, too, is made in a specific part of the brain

When a plan you made in the morning fizzles out within three days, it's easy to blame a weak-willed personality. But neuroscience increasingly explains this not as a personality problem but as a question of how active certain brain regions are. At the center of that discussion are two terms: <strong>BDNF</strong> and the <strong>aMCC</strong>.

<strong>BDNF</strong>, short for brain-derived neurotrophic factor, <strong>helps brain cells grow and connect with each other</strong>. The <strong>aMCC</strong> refers to the front-middle area of the **cingulate cortex**, and it's known to be deeply tied to <strong>the strength to push through things you don't want to do</strong>, what we usually call <strong>willpower</strong>. Each concept was famous on its own, but lately the discussion is converging on the idea that real change comes from stimulating both together.

> Getting smarter and steeling your resolve
> *start from the same place in the brain*

<strong>The problem is that neither region becomes active on its own while you sit still</strong>. Some say they actually shrink if you keep your body comfortable and rarely use your brain, so it's important to know exactly what to do and how.

## BDNF, the brain's "fertilizer": what is it?

<strong>BDNF</strong> is a protein that helps nerve cells grow and connect to one another through **synapses**. Just as muscles need protein to grow, brain cells need a steady supply of this substance to grow and be maintained. That's why neuroscience books abroad sometimes describe <strong>BDNF as fertilizer for the brain</strong>.

When BDNF levels drop, memory and learning ability tend to decline, and levels are known to fall naturally with age. Conversely, many sources repeat that aerobic exercise, enough sleep and learning new things increase BDNF release. That's why it's considered an essential element in keeping the brain young.

## The aMCC, the "willpower button": what is it?

<strong>The aMCC is the front-middle part of the cingulate cortex</strong>, known to become especially active <strong>when you endure hard, unpleasant tasks and push them through to the end</strong>. The concept started getting attention as several neuroscience research teams published findings on the relationship between this region and perseverance and self-control.

Interestingly, there are also observations that in people whose cognitive abilities stay remarkably sharp with age, often called **SuperAgers**, this aMCC area is more active than in their peers. <strong>On the other hand, it's also said that if you keep seeking only physical comfort and avoid challenges, this region gets used less and less and shrinks.</strong>

## Why intelligence and willpower start in the same place

If <strong>BDNF</strong> is the <strong>soil</strong> that grows brain cells, the <strong>aMCC</strong> is closer to <strong>the engine that keeps you repeating hard things on top of that soil</strong>. Learning something new means enduring difficult, boring stretches; the aMCC provides the staying power, and the brain cells stimulated in the process grow and connect with BDNF's help.

So even though a head for study and sheer grit look like completely different talents, in reality they often come together in people with similar habits of using the brain. That's also why, if you want to get smarter, you shouldn't just cling to workbooks but also make time for activities that use body and mind together.

## Ways to wake up BDNF and the aMCC together

The first is **aerobic exercise**. It's widely known that repeating exercise that gets your heart pounding, such as brisk walking or running, for **20 to 30 minutes** increases BDNF release, and <strong>the very process of trying to finish even when it's hard is seen as a way of stimulating the aMCC</strong>.

There are also methods you can try in daily life besides exercise:

✓ <strong>20–30 minutes of aerobic exercise</strong> at an intensity that leaves you slightly out of breath

✓ <strong>Finishing one tedious task you've been putting off</strong>; starting with the dishes or tidying up is fine

✓ <strong>Learning something unfamiliar</strong>, such as a foreign language or a musical instrument

✓ <strong>Practicing tolerating briefly uncomfortable stimuli</strong>, such as a cold shower

What these have in common is that they stimulate the brain by giving it a little discomfort.

## Changes that appear when you practice daily

Many people find that after keeping these habits for just a few weeks, they gradually stop giving up so easily on small things. It's also common to hear that they can now stay with work or study that requires focus for longer, without drifting off as they used to.

Still, the brain doesn't change overnight, so keep in mind that you need to keep repeating these for at least a few weeks to a few months before you feel a difference. If you hastily try for a day or two and quit because it doesn't seem to work, the habit itself tends to collapse.

## Cautions: doing it this way backfires

Pushing yourself hard no matter what isn't better. Some say BDNF levels actually drop under chronic stress or sleep deprivation, so continuing with excessive exercise or extreme self-denial can backfire.

One more thing to remember: you don't need to push yourself to the limit every day to build the aMCC. <strong>Simply stepping slightly outside a comfortable routine and regularly slipping in something uncomfortable is enough stimulation</strong>. Many sources emphasize in common that <strong>consistency matters more than intensity</strong>.

In the end, the wish to get smarter and the wish to become more strong-willed weren't different goals after all; they were a story that meets inside the brain. Rather than making grand plans, <strong>choosing one small discomfort today and acting on it</strong> can be the start.
