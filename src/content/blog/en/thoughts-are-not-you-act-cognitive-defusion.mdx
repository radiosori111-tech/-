## 📌 Key Takeaways

> **The short answer:** Anxious thoughts came back more often the harder people pushed them down. People told not to think of a white bear thought of it **more often** once they stopped trying. Rather than fighting to erase thoughts, **seeing thoughts as thoughts**, as in "I'm having the thought that…", is the approach that fits the research: cognitive defusion.

- 🐻‍❄️ **Suppressed thoughts came back.** People told not to think of a white bear for five minutes, once later allowed to think freely, brought up the white bear more often than people asked to think of it from the start (**Journal of Personality and Social Psychology**, 1987).
- 🔁 **Repeating a word fast made people believe it less.** People who repeated a self-critical word aloud quickly for 30 seconds believed the thought less and felt less uncomfortable (**Behaviour Research and Therapy**, 2004).
- 🧩 **Acceptance and commitment therapy worked.** In a meta-analysis of 39 randomized trials, **acceptance and commitment therapy** (ACT) reduced anxiety and depression more than waiting or usual care, and worked about as well as established cognitive behavioral therapy (**Psychotherapy and Psychosomatics**, 2015).
- 🏷️ **Naming emotions calmed the amygdala.** Labeling emotions while viewing angry faces reduced **amygdala** responses and increased activity in the **right ventrolateral prefrontal cortex** (**Psychological Science**, 2007).

### 💬 "Why does telling myself to think positively make me more anxious?"

"What if I botch the big presentation?" "What if people see me as incompetent?" When anxiety or self-blame surged, I'd try to push the thought down, telling myself "think positive." But the harder I pushed, the sharper the thought became.

Then, following **acceptance and commitment therapy** (ACT), I tried naming the thought: "Ah, my brain is sounding the alarm again." The thought didn't disappear, but that alone made the tightness in my chest ease a great deal.

The first version of this post included this experience, along with the explanation that "cognitive defusion activates the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex to suppress the amygdala" and three sources. Checking again, I couldn't find two of those papers, and the brain explanation differed somewhat from actual research. This time I rewrote it with research that can be verified.

## Deep Dive: Why do anxious thoughts grow stronger the more you suppress them?

### 1. Does resisting a thought really make it come up more?

It did. Psychologist Daniel Wegner's team at Trinity University in the US had participants say whatever came to mind for five minutes, telling one group "don't think of a white bear" and another "think of a white bear" (Wegner et al., **Journal of Personality and Social Psychology**, 1987).

- Even those told to resist the white bear thought of it more than once a minute.
- And once they stopped resisting and could think freely, they brought up the white bear **more often** than those who had thought of it from the start.

The interpretation is that while you suppress a thought, the brain has to keep checking whether the thought has appeared, and that checking process actually calls the thought up. The earlier version's "the more you suppress it, the sharper it gets" fits this study well.

### 2. What is cognitive defusion?

Steven Hayes's team at the University of Nevada, who developed acceptance and commitment therapy, called the state of believing thoughts as facts **cognitive fusion**, and stepping back to see thoughts as "words that showed up in your head" **cognitive defusion** (Hayes et al., **Behaviour Research and Therapy**, 2006). Instead of changing or erasing thoughts, it's a way of putting distance between you and your thoughts.

Hayes's team also tested this in experiments. They had participants choose a word that bothered them about themselves (like "fat" or "stupid"), and one group **repeated it aloud quickly for 30 seconds** (Masuda et al., **Behaviour Research and Therapy**, 2004).

- Those who repeated the word quickly came to **believe the thought less** and felt **less uncomfortable**.
- The effect was larger than in a group that tried to replace the thought with another or distract themselves.

As you keep repeating a word, its meaning blurs and only the sound remains, which is thought to help you see the thought as "just words." But it was a short experiment with college students.

### 3. Does acceptance and commitment therapy actually work?

A Dutch team pooled 39 randomized trials of acceptance and commitment therapy with 1,821 people (A-Tjak et al., **Psychotherapy and Psychosomatics**, 2015).

- Acceptance and commitment therapy reduced **anxiety**, **depression** and physical symptoms more than waiting or usual care.
- Compared with established **cognitive behavioral therapy**, the effects were similar.

So acceptance and commitment therapy is an evidence-based treatment. But the trials in the meta-analysis varied in quality, and if you have an anxiety disorder or depression, it's better to work with a professional than to practice alone.

### 4. What happens in the brain during defusion?

The first version said "cognitive defusion activates the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex to suppress runaway emotion in the amygdala," citing "Dimidjian et al. (2016)" and "Journal of Consulting and Clinical Psychology (2018)." I couldn't find either paper, so I removed them from the sources.

The brain research closest to defusion is the **affect labeling** experiment. A UCLA team had people label emotions like "angry" or "afraid" while viewing angry or fearful faces. **Amygdala** responses fell, and the **right ventrolateral prefrontal cortex** became more active (Lieberman et al., **Psychological Science**, 2007). It was the "ventrolateral," not the "dorsolateral," prefrontal cortex the first version mentioned. This study is covered in detail in [Why You Repeat Things You Regret](/en/blog/if-you-keep-doing-things-you-regret-the-brain-acted-first/).

Also, the original claim that "the amygdala can't fully tell verbal imagination from reality" is an oversimplification. It's true that imagining threats triggers the amygdala, but there's no basis for saying it "can't fully tell them apart."

### 5. Summary: What held up and what was corrected

| Original claim | What the research says | Type of evidence |

| Defusion puts distance between you and your thoughts | True. Repeating a word fast made people believe it less and feel less uncomfortable | Experiment (BRT 2004) |

## Solution: 3 Steps to Distance Yourself from Anxious Thoughts

**Step 1. Rephrase as "I'm having the thought that…"**
Instead of "I'm incompetent," try saying "My brain is having the thought that 'I'm incompetent.'" Don't try to erase the thought; just notice that it's a thought.

**Step 2. Repeat a distressing word fast for 30 seconds**
Pick one distressing word that keeps coming up and repeat it aloud quickly for 30 seconds. The word gradually loses its meaning and starts to sound like just a sound. Follow the routine below.

**Step 3. Do what you meant to do, thought and all**
The goal of acceptance and commitment therapy isn't to erase anxious thoughts but to move in the direction you want even with them present. Even if the thought remains, return to what you meant to do, like preparing a presentation or taking a walk.

## Conclusion

Anxious thoughts weren't an enemy that had to disappear. The harder they were pushed down, the more often they came back, like the white bear, while seeing thoughts as thoughts weakened their grip. A small experiment of repeating a distressing word for 30 seconds was enough to make people believe the thought less. Even with the uninvited guest of fear riding beside us, we can still steer toward where we want to go. Tonight, write down a thought that comes up and say once, "I'm having the thought that…"

### 🙋 Frequently Asked Questions

#### Does replacing thoughts with positive ones not work?

Not all ways of changing thoughts are ineffective. Cognitive behavioral therapy is also evidence-based, and it worked about as well as acceptance and commitment therapy. But forcibly pushing thoughts down or trying to erase them can make them come back more. If a hard-to-change thought keeps repeating, try adding defusion to create distance.

#### I have an anxiety disorder. Can I practice on my own?

Practicing on your own is fine for everyday worries, but if anxiety makes daily life hard, talk with a professional who offers acceptance and commitment therapy or cognitive behavioral therapy. Exercise is also an evidence-based way to reduce anxiety. That's covered in [Does Exercise Help Anxiety?](/en/blog/neuroscientist-anxiety-move-exercise-brain-science-guide/).
