For busy people today, "an hour of exercise a day" is like homework that's hard to get done.

But recent research in exercise physiology and brain science keeps finding that <strong>even short bursts of intense exercise play a decisive role in activating brain function</strong>. It means that <strong>"how high you push your heart rate in the moment"</strong> is a more powerful switch for the survival and reorganization of brain neurons than simply <strong>"moving for a long time."</strong>

Here is a careful summary of the scientific mechanisms by which <strong>short, high-intensity exercise such as a one-minute sprint</strong> (HIIE, high-intensity interval exercise) can match or even exceed the effects of a long walk, and the key evidence behind them.

## 1. The key to BDNF release: intensity, not time

The most notable effect of short, intense exercise on the brain is an explosive release of **BDNF** (brain-derived neurotrophic factor). <strong>BDNF acts as a kind of "brain fertilizer," helping brain cells grow, form strong connections and stay protected</strong>.

- <strong>Lactate signaling:</strong> sprinting at high intensity produces lactate in the muscles. Lactate was once seen only as a fatigue byproduct, but according to recent research, <strong><em>lactate from the muscles travels through the bloodstream, crosses the blood-brain barrier (BBB) and enters the brain, where it acts as a powerful signaling molecule that induces BDNF expression</em></strong>.
- <strong>Findings:</strong> BDNF concentrations in the blood and brain measure markedly higher after short, high-intensity exercise that pushes the heart rate to its maximum than after long, low-intensity exercise. According to researchers at the University of Otago in New Zealand, six minutes of high-intensity intervals raised BDNF protein levels up to four to five times more effectively than 60 minutes of light cycling.

## 2. Neuroplasticity and changes in hippocampal volume

The **hippocampus**, the brain's memory store and a key region for preventing dementia, <strong>loses about 1 to 2% of its volume each year with age</strong>. But <strong>high-intensity stimulation activates the neuroplasticity that reverses this trend</strong>.

- <strong>Neurogenesis:</strong> along with BDNF, **IGF-1** (insulin-like growth factor 1) and **VEGF** (vascular endothelial growth factor) released during high-intensity exercise promote the birth of new neurons in the dentate gyrus of the hippocampus.
- <strong>Cerebral blood flow and oxygen supply:</strong> intense exercise stimulates the endothelial cells of the brain's blood vessels, <strong>increasing nitric oxide (NO) release</strong> and sharply raising the elasticity and blood flow of the microvessels leading to the brain. This <strong>immediately supplies brain cells with plenty of oxygen and nutrients, helping prevent cognitive decline</strong>.

## 3. VIGOR and VILPA: the mortality benefit of just one minute in daily life

The benefits of short exercise have also been confirmed in large epidemiological studies.

- <strong>The VILPA (vigorous intermittent lifestyle physical activity) study:</strong> when a University of Sydney research team analyzed data from wearable devices, <strong>people who did VILPA, three or four short bursts a day of one to two minutes each that left them out of breath (climbing stairs, running for the bus and so on)</strong>, had <strong>about a 38 to 40% lower risk of death from all causes and up to a 49% lower risk of cardiovascular death</strong> than those who didn't.
- <strong>Efficiency for the time spent:</strong> <strong>if two hours of leisurely walking helps cleanse the cardiovascular system overall and lift your mood, a one-minute sprint works as a "biological shock therapy" that reorganizes the brain's neural circuits and stimulates hormone release</strong>.

## 4. Practicing safely: precautions

Despite the powerful effects of a one-minute sprint, a sudden all-out sprint isn't safe for everyone. To get the brain-stimulating benefits safely, you need a step-by-step approach.

1. <strong>Always warm up:</strong> if you don't usually exercise much, or you have cardiovascular or joint problems, sudden high-intensity stimulation can strain your blood vessels. Be sure to loosen up first with light marching in place or stretching.
2. <strong>Increase intensity gradually:</strong> it doesn't have to be a 100% sprint. The key is reaching 80 to 90% of your maximum heart rate (breathless enough that you can't hold a light conversation). You can substitute climbing a steep hill, going up stairs quickly or pedaling hard on a stationary bike.
3. <strong>Make it routine:</strong> using the low bar of "one minute a day" to build a habit tied naturally to everyday actions (your commute, a short spurt during a lunchtime walk) is the key to maintaining brain health over the long term.

## 5. Closing

<strong>If it's hard to find a long block of time, try splitting your running into short sessions like I do: before work, at lunch and on the way home</strong>. Even a few minutes of running is enough to feel your tired brain's fatigue wash away and fresh energy fill in.

<strong>You don't need a grand start, either.</strong> Five hundred meters is fine; just one minute is enough. Why not get up from where you are right now and run until you're out of breath for just one minute? <strong>That single minute of practice in your busy day can be the start of the most efficient brain health routine</strong>.
