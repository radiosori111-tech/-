## 📌 Key Takeaways

> **The short answer:** Gut health played a part in protecting the brain through immunity. **IgA antibody cells** trained by meeting gut bacteria settled in the membranes around the brain and blocked microbial invasion. But this is mostly from mouse experiments; in people, what's been confirmed is that eating more **fermented foods** diversified gut bacteria and lowered inflammatory markers.

- 🛡️ **Gut-trained IgA cells guarded the brain's membranes.** In mice and people, IgA-producing cells clustered beside large veins in the brain's membranes, and these cells came from the gut. Mice without gut bacteria had fewer of them (**Nature**, 2020).
- 🥬 **Fermented foods diversified gut bacteria.** When 36 people ate more fermented foods for 10 weeks, gut microbial diversity rose and 19 inflammatory markers fell. In a group that ate more fiber, diversity didn't rise (**Cell**, 2021).
- 🧪 **Gut serotonin was shaped by gut bacteria.** Most of the body's serotonin is made in the gut, regulated by gut bacteria. But gut serotonin doesn't enter the brain directly (**Cell**, 2015).
- 🐭 **Without gut bacteria, stress responses were bigger.** Germ-free mice had larger stress hormone responses, and giving certain gut bacteria early in life brought them closer to normal (**Journal of Physiology**, 2004).

### 💬 "If I want to protect my brain, should I really start with my gut?"

When we want a clear head and steady emotions, we usually focus only on the brain. This post began as a summary of the **gut-brain axis** story introduced in neurologist David Perlmutter's book *Brain Maker*, and of the link between the brain and **IgA antibodies**, which guard the gut lining. It was a long post, covering three diets and four lifestyle habits.

Looking again, its core point, "IgA cells trained in the gut guard the brain's membranes," was confirmed by an actual Nature paper. But there were also many claims not confirmed in people or without findable sources, like "leaky gut causes brain fog," "exercise raises beneficial bacteria by over 40%," and "4-6 breathing alone calms brain inflammation." This time I shortened it and rewrote it around confirmed research.

## Deep Dive: How are the gut and brain connected?

### 1. Do gut IgA antibodies really protect the brain?

A team from the University of Cambridge in the UK and the US National Institutes of Health (NIH) examined the membranes around the brain (the **meninges**) in mice and people (Fitzpatrick et al., **Nature**, 2020).

- Plasma cells making **IgA** clustered beside the large veins (venous sinuses) of the meninges.
- These cells were formed in the gut by meeting gut bacteria and then moved to the meninges. Germ-free mice without gut bacteria had far fewer of them.
- Without these cells, microbes carried in the blood (such as fungi) spread more easily into the meninges.

The original post's claim that "IgA cells trained in the gut guard the brain beside large vessels in the meninges" matches this study exactly. The researchers described it as a "firewall" between gut and brain. But it was mainly confirmed in mice; in people, the same cells were found in the meninges.

### 2. Do gut bacteria affect brain development?

In animal experiments, they did.

- A Kyushu University team in Japan showed that **germ-free mice** without gut bacteria had larger stress hormone responses than normal mice. Giving certain gut bacteria early in life brought the response closer to normal, but giving them after adulthood didn't (Sudo et al., **Journal of Physiology**, 2004).
- A University of Freiburg team in Germany reported that **microglia**, the brain's immune cells, were less mature in germ-free mice, and that feeding them **short-chain fatty acids** made by gut bacteria restored maturation (Erny et al., **Nature Neuroscience**, 2015).

The original post's lines that "without gut microbes, animals suffer anxiety disorders or memory decline" and "gut health determines precise brain circuit formation" stretch animal experiments too far to people. Whether the same happens in human brain development isn't yet clear.

### 3. Is 90% of serotonin made in the gut?

Yes. Most of the body's serotonin is made by enterochromaffin cells in the gut, and a Caltech team showed in mice that gut bacteria regulate this production (Yano et al., **Cell**, 2015).

But the original post's addition that "a worsening gut environment leads to anxiety, depression and attention deficits" doesn't follow directly. Serotonin made in the gut is used in the blood for things like gut movement and platelets, and barely crosses the **blood-brain barrier**. The brain's serotonin is made separately in the brain.

### 4. Do fermented foods work in people too?

This is where the evidence in people is clearest. A Stanford University team divided 36 healthy adults into two groups for 10 weeks. One group ate more **fermented foods** like yogurt, kimchi, sauerkraut and kombucha; the other ate more **fiber** (Wastyk et al., **Cell**, 2021).

- The fermented food group's **gut microbial diversity** rose, and 19 **inflammatory markers** in the blood fell.
- The fiber group's diversity didn't rise, and inflammatory markers varied by person.

The fermented foods the original post recommended, like kimchi, sauerkraut, natto, yogurt and doenjang, fit this result. But this study measured inflammatory markers; it didn't check whether brain function or IgA improved.

### 5. Does leaky gut cause brain fog?

The original post explained that "when IgA drops, cracks form in the gut wall, and endotoxins travel in the blood across the blood-brain barrier to cause brain inflammation and brain fog." Increased gut permeability and inflammation have been observed in animal studies and some diseases, but I couldn't find research confirming the sequence "leaky gut → brain inflammation → brain fog" in healthy people. "Leaky gut syndrome" isn't even an official diagnosis yet.

I also checked other claims from the original post.

- "Exercise raises short-chain-fatty-acid-producing beneficial bacteria by over 40%": I couldn't find the source.
- "5 minutes of 4-6 breathing a day calms inflammatory signals in the brain": I found no research measuring brain inflammation in people. Slow breathing may help reduce tension, though.
- Research showing that ketone bodies (BHB) suppress an inflammasome (Youm et al., **Nature Medicine**, 2015) is real, but it was in cells and mice.
- There's no human evidence that carnivore "completely blocks leaky gut." This is covered separately in [How Long Do Carnivore Side Effects Last?](/en/blog/carnivore-diet-for-better-brain-function-inspired-by-you-quiz/).

**Step 1. Add a little fermented food every day**
Start adding familiar fermented foods like yogurt, kimchi, doenjang or natto once a day. In the Stanford study, people also increased gradually over several weeks. Go easy on salty fermented foods.

**Step 2. Eat vegetables, beans and whole grains consistently**
Fiber is the raw material gut bacteria use to make short-chain fatty acids. In the study, diversity didn't rise within 10 weeks, but consistently feeding your gut bacteria is a basic of gut health. Soak and cook beans thoroughly if you're concerned about lectins.

**Step 3. Mind sleep and exercise together**
For both gut and brain, sleep more than 7 hours and move about 30 minutes three times a week. Minding sleep and exercise alongside diet is closer to the evidence than trying to solve everything with food alone.

## Conclusion

The gut and brain were linked not only through nerves but through immunity too. The discovery that IgA cells trained by gut bacteria block microbes in the membranes around the brain supports the core of the original post. But the ideas that the gut alone unlocks the brain's potential, or that leaky gut causes brain fog, haven't been confirmed in people yet. What you can do today is simple: add one fermented food, eat your vegetables, and sleep and move enough.

### 🙋 Frequently Asked Questions

#### Are probiotic supplements good for the brain too?

Human studies on whether probiotics improve mood or cognition have mixed results. Effects vary by strain and person, and the evidence isn't yet strong enough to recommend a specific product for the brain. Start by eating fermented foods and vegetables, and talk with a doctor or pharmacist about supplements if needed.

#### Should I get tested for leaky gut?

"Leaky gut syndrome" isn't an official diagnosis yet, and there's no standard test for the general public. If symptoms like stomach pain, diarrhea, weight loss or bloody stools continue, seeing a gastroenterologist is the accurate route.
