## 📌 Key Takeaways

> **The short answer:** Tracking your time is likely to change your habits. People **estimated their real phone time at about half**, and the more consistently people recorded, the better they reached their goals. First log your day to see the drifting time as numbers, then repeat a new habit to fill that time **at the same moment** each day.

- 📱 **People used their phones twice as much as they thought.** When 23 people's phone use was recorded with an app, they averaged about 5 hours and 85 uses a day, but estimated about half that (**PLoS ONE**, 2015).
- 📝 **Consistent recording brought people closer to their goals.** In a systematic review of 22 studies, the more consistently people recorded what they ate and their exercise, the more weight they lost (**Journal of the American Dietetic Association**, 2011).
- 🔁 **Habits took 66 days on average to settle in.** Repeating the same action at the same time each day took a median of 66 days to become automatic, and missing a day didn't matter much (**European Journal of Social Psychology**, 2010).
- 🏃 **Exercise spread to people nearby.** In an analysis of 1.1 million people's running records, on days a friend ran more, that person ran more too (**Nature Communications**, 2017).

### 💬 "Am I actually looking at my time, or just drifting?"

Honestly, for a while I was letting my days slip by. Fifteen minutes opening and closing a delivery app, another twenty browsing a shopping site with nothing to buy. One day I wrote down my daily routine and found far more of that time than I'd thought. That's when I first realized that what I'd vaguely called "no time" was really **no direction**.

Once I started logging my days, I opened the delivery app less, and other choices took its place. Now I'm someone who walks or runs during the short break after meals. Even when I think "maybe I'll just rest today," once I put my shoes on and head out, I find myself running. Some time later, coworkers who had been watching told me, "Seeing you move every day after lunch, I started walking early in the morning too."

In the original post I explained this experience by comparing it to the quantum **observer effect**, **superposition** and **quantum entanglement**. Looking again, the right move was to keep the experience but correct the physics comparisons and explain it with research that actually supports it.

## Deep Dive: Why does logging your day make a difference?

### 1. How accurately do we know our own time?

Less than we think. A UK team installed usage-logging apps on 23 people's phones to record actual use, then compared it with their own estimates (Andrews et al., **PLoS ONE**, 2015).

- Participants used their phones an average of about **5 hours** and **85 times** a day. More than half of those were quick checks lasting under 30 seconds.
- But their own estimates of time and frequency were **about half** of the real figures.

It's a small study, but it shows there can be a big gap between a vague sense of "no time" and actual time. That's probably why I was surprised when I wrote down my day. Estimate your own day with the calculator below.

### 2. Can recording alone change behavior?

A University of Pittsburgh team reviewed 22 studies on the effects of self-recording food, exercise and weight in weight-loss programs (Burke et al., **Journal of the American Dietetic Association**, 2011).

- In nearly every study, **the more consistently people recorded**, the more weight they lost.
- As missed recording days increased, the weight-loss effect shrank.

Most of these studies compared people who recorded with those who didn't, so people with more willpower to begin with may have recorded better. Still, the finding that records put your actual behavior in front of you, and that this changes your next choice, pointed the same way across many studies. My experience that "habits faded once I started observing" is more accurately explained by these studies than by physics.

### 3. Can the observer effect and quantum entanglement describe a day?

The original post said "the moment you observe, the superposition settles into one state" and "people are connected like quantum entanglement." It's a striking comparison, but it differs from what the physics actually means.

- In the **observer effect**, "observation" doesn't mean a person seeing something, but a measuring device **physically interacting** with a particle. It doesn't mean human consciousness or attention decides a particle's state.
- **Quantum entanglement** is a phenomenon where two particles' measurement results are correlated, but it can't be used to send information or influence instantly. It's a different phenomenon from influence between people.
- The **Orch-OR** theory introduced in the original post (the hypothesis that consciousness arises from quantum processes in the brain's microtubules) is a hypothesis published in a journal (Hameroff & Penrose, **Physics of Life Reviews**, 2014), but it hasn't been accepted by mainstream science. A leading objection is the calculation that in a warm, wet environment like the brain, quantum states collapse far faster than neurons take to send signals, in less than a trillionth of a second (Tegmark, **Physical Review E**, 2000).

So this post doesn't use quantum mechanics as evidence, and makes clear that the original post's comparisons were only "metaphors."

### 4. Does the reticular activating system filter only what I focus on?

The original post called the **reticular activating system** (RAS) "a filter that picks out information related to what I focus on." This explanation is widely used in self-help, but it differs from the original research.

The reticular activating system is a network of neurons in the brainstem. In cat experiments, stimulating this area changed sleep-like brain waves into waking brain waves (Moruzzi & Magoun, **Electroencephalography and Clinical Neurophysiology**, 1949). In other words, the RAS mainly regulates **sleep, waking and arousal level**. Choosing what to pay attention to is mainly handled by attention networks in the frontal and parietal lobes. The experience that "once you set a goal, related information stands out" is common, but explaining it with the RAS alone is an oversimplification.

### 5. Do repeated actions really change the brain?

**Neuroplasticity** is a confirmed phenomenon. A University of Regensburg team in Germany had people who had never juggled practice juggling for three months. Those who practiced had more **gray matter** in a brain area involved in seeing motion, and it shrank again after they stopped (Draganski et al., **Nature**, 2004).

But the original post's statement that "the brain changes in the direction you pay attention to" is a bit broader than this result. What the research showed was that areas related to **a skill practiced repeatedly** change. There's no evidence that logging your day changed brain structure, but when you repeat a new habit that came from logging for a long time, that action gets easier. How the brain changes as habits build up is covered in detail in [5 Minutes a Day: Why the Brain Really Changes](/en/blog/five-minutes-a-day-why-the-brain-really-changes-neuroplasticity/).

### 6. Why did running after meals become a habit?

A UCL team in the UK had 96 people each choose a new action to do in the same situation every day, like "drink a bottle of water after lunch," and followed them for 12 weeks (Lally et al., **European Journal of Social Psychology**, 2010).

- The time for an action to become automatic had a median of **66 days**, ranging from 18 to 254 days depending on the person and the action.
- Missing a day had little effect on the habit settling in.

My putting on running shoes every day at the same moment, "after meals," was exactly this approach. The set situation became a cue that drew out the action.

### 7. Can my habit spread to coworkers?

The original post explained my coworkers starting to walk with **mirror neurons**. Mirror neurons are nerve cells, actually discovered, that respond both when a monkey acts and when it watches others act (Rizzolatti & Sinigaglia, **Nature Reviews Neuroscience**, 2010). But explaining changes in human habits with mirror neurons still lacks evidence.

Instead, there's more direct evidence that exercise spreads between people. An MIT team analyzed running data recorded over five years by about 1.1 million people worldwide. Comparing situations like weather that happened to increase a friend's running, **on days a friend ran more, that person ran more too** (Aral & Nicolaides, **Nature Communications**, 2017). My coworkers starting to walk is more accurately seen as an example of this "exercise contagion."

## Solution: 3 Steps of Time Tracking to Change Your Day

**Step 1. Log your day for just three days**
For three days, write down your waking hours in 30-minute blocks. Check your phone's screen-time record too. Just seeing how different your estimate is from reality changes your next choice.

**Step 2. Attach one new action to one stretch of drifting time**
Pick the "purposeless time" that shows up most often in your log, and decide on a new action for that time. The key is setting it as **the same situation, the same action**, like "after lunch, I walk for 10 minutes." Missing a day is fine, so keep it going with 66 days as your target.

**Step 3. Keep a record and share it with people around you**
Briefly check off whether you did the new habit, and do it with a coworker or friend if you can. Exercise spread among friends, and your record could be someone else's start. Ways to keep records are also covered in [Can Exercise Stop Late-Night Snack Cravings?](/en/blog/break-bad-habits-reset-the-brains-reward-circuit-with-sweat/).

## Conclusion

What changed when I started observing my day wasn't a law of physics, but the fact that I saw my own time accurately for the first time. People estimated their time far lower than reality, and logging put that gap in front of them. A new action repeated at the same moment became a habit, and that habit spread to my coworkers. Three days of logging today is a surer starting point than an elegant quantum metaphor.

### 🙋 Frequently Asked Questions

#### Do I have to use an app to track my time?

No. Writing in a paper notebook in 30-minute blocks is enough. But since self-estimates of phone time tend to come out lower than reality, it's a good idea to check the screen-time record built into your phone as well.

#### If I miss a few days of logging, do I have to start over?

No. In the habit research too, missing a day didn't have much effect on the habit settling in. Skip the missed days and pick it up again the next day. Memory changes more easily than you'd think, so writing even briefly on the day is more accurate than recalling it later.
