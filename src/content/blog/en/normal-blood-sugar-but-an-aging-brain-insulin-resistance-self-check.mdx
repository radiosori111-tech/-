## 📌 Key takeaways

> **The short answer:** Even in people without diabetes, **higher average blood sugar went with a higher risk of dementia**. **Insulin resistance** rarely causes obvious symptoms, so numbers are more reliable than how you feel. A **triglyceride/HDL ratio** of 3.0 or more, fasting glucose of 100 or more, and waist size on your checkup report are good clues, and doing aerobic and strength training together is the best-supported response.

- 🧠 **Blood sugar mattered for the brain even without diabetes.** Among older adults without diabetes, those whose average blood sugar was 115 mg/dL had an **18% higher** risk of dementia than those at 100 mg/dL (**NEJM**, 2013).
- 🔬 **Brains with Alzheimer's responded less to insulin.** Insulin signaling was blunted even in the brain tissue of Alzheimer's patients who didn't have diabetes (**Journal of Clinical Investigation**, 2012).
- 📊 **Numbers are more accurate.** In overweight adults, a triglyceride/HDL ratio of 3.0 or more made insulin resistance likely (**Annals of Internal Medicine**, 2003).
- 🏋️ **Exercise worked best when combined.** Of 262 people with type 2 diabetes, only the group that did aerobic and strength training **together** saw a meaningful drop in HbA1c (**JAMA**, 2010).

### 💬 "My checkup says my blood sugar is normal. Can I relax?"

**Insulin resistance** shows up a lot in health articles these days. It sounds like it's about diabetes, but it's often mentioned alongside dementia, which can feel unfamiliar. When I first wrote this post, I drew on several articles to explain that "even if your blood sugar is normal, you can't be complacent."

This time I tracked down the original papers those articles relied on. Fortunately, the core message held up, but some of the self-check signs and the claim that "cutting sugar in childhood lowers dementia risk" needed correcting.

## Deep Dive: how much is confirmed about insulin resistance and the brain?

### 1. Can "normal" blood sugar still affect the brain?

A University of Washington team followed the blood sugar records of 2,067 people aged 65 and over without dementia for an average of 6.8 years (Crane et al., **NEJM**, 2013). Even among those **without** diabetes, people whose average blood sugar over the previous five years was 115 mg/dL had an 18% higher risk of dementia than those at 100 mg/dL. Among people with diabetes, an average of 190 mg/dL carried a 40% higher risk than 160 mg/dL.

As my original title put it, "even in the normal range, blood sugar is linked to the brain," and this study backs that up. But it's an observational study, so we can't conclude that blood sugar directly causes dementia.

### 2. Does the brain use insulin? Is "type 3 diabetes" accurate?

There are insulin receptors throughout the brain, including the **hippocampus**, which handles memory. A University of Pennsylvania team added insulin to hippocampal tissue from people who had died with Alzheimer's and found that insulin signaling was blunted **even in patients who hadn't had diabetes**, and the more blunted it was, the lower their cognitive function had been in life (Talbot et al., **Journal of Clinical Investigation**, 2012).

Because of findings like these, some researchers call Alzheimer's disease **"type 3 diabetes"** (de la Monte & Wands, **Journal of Diabetes Science and Technology**, 2008). As my original post noted, this isn't an official disease category but a term used by researchers. In 2024, the Lancet Commission on dementia concluded that **14 modifiable risk factors**, including diabetes, obesity, physical inactivity, and high LDL cholesterol, are linked to about 45% of dementia (Livingston et al., **Lancet**, 2024).

### 3. What signs point to insulin resistance?

In my original post, I listed "severe drowsiness and fatigue right after meals" as the first sign. Post-meal sleepiness has many causes, including portion size, sleep, and the body clock, so I couldn't find evidence that it alone points to insulin resistance. I removed it and replaced it with **numeric thresholds** used in research.

- **Triglyceride/HDL ratio:** A Stanford University team reported that among 258 overweight adults, triglycerides of 130 mg/dL or more, or a triglyceride/HDL ratio of **3.0 or more**, did a good job of identifying insulin resistance (McLaughlin et al., **Annals of Internal Medicine**, 2003).
- **Waist size:** The Korean cutoff for abdominal obesity is 90 cm for men and 85 cm for women (Lee et al., **Diabetes Research and Clinical Practice**, 2007). Cutoffs differ by population.
- **Fasting glucose:** 100 to 125 mg/dL is impaired fasting glucose, and 126 mg/dL or more may meet the threshold for diabetes.
- Clinics sometimes estimate insulin resistance by calculating **HOMA-IR** from fasting insulin and glucose (Matthews et al., **Diabetologia**, 1985).

### 4. Can medication protect the brain?

Efforts to restore insulin signaling to protect the brain continue, but there are no definitive results yet. In a randomized trial in which 289 people with mild cognitive impairment or early Alzheimer's used nasal-spray insulin for a year, **no improvement in cognition appeared** (Craft et al., **JAMA Neurology**, 2020). As my original post noted, trials of diabetes-drug classes have also produced mixed results between biomarkers and actual symptoms, so taking them to protect the brain is hard to recommend. If you already take diabetes medication, don't change it without talking to your doctor.

### 5. Does cutting sugar really protect the brain?

In my original post I wrote that "people whose sugar intake was cut in childhood had a lower risk of dementia as adults." The study most often cited for this analyzed sugar rationing in the UK: people whose sugar intake was restricted during their first 1,000 days of life later had about a **35% lower risk of type 2 diabetes and about a 20% lower risk of high blood pressure** (Gracner et al., **Science**, 2024). What this paper directly showed was diabetes and blood pressure, and I couldn't find a peer-reviewed paper confirming dementia risk as well, so I corrected the wording. Still, since diabetes is a risk factor for dementia, cutting sugary drinks and sugar remains worthwhile.

## The fix: three steps to check and lower insulin resistance

**Step 1. Check by the numbers on your checkup report**
Find your triglycerides, HDL, fasting glucose, and waist size on your checkup results and enter them below. If any item crosses its threshold, go over it with your doctor at your next visit.

**Step 2. Combine aerobic and strength training**
In the HART-D trial at the Pennington Biomedical Research Center in the US, 262 people with type 2 diabetes were split into groups for nine months, and only the group that did **both** saw a meaningful drop in HbA1c, compared with groups that did aerobic or strength training alone (Church et al., **JAMA**, 2010). Walk briskly three times a week and add strength exercises such as squats twice a week. A 10-minute walk after meals helps too.

**Step 3. Start by cutting sugary drinks**
Cut back on sugary drinks and refined carbs, and eat vegetables and protein first. I graphed how meal order and walking after meals affect blood sugar in [what insulin does](/en/blog/what-does-insulin-do-and-how-to-use-it-wisely/).

## Conclusion

"My blood sugar is normal, so I'm fine" is only half right. Even among people without diabetes, higher average blood sugar went with higher dementia risk, and the brains of people with Alzheimer's were responding less to insulin. Since insulin resistance is hard to feel, try entering the numbers from this year's checkup. And this week, add two strength sessions to your walks.

### 🙋 Frequently asked questions

#### Can insulin resistance be reversed?

In many cases it can improve with lifestyle changes. When you lose weight and gain muscle, the same insulin lowers blood sugar more effectively, because muscle receives most of the glucose insulin sends. I covered how this works in [what insulin does](/en/blog/what-does-insulin-do-and-how-to-use-it-wisely/).

#### Does intermittent fasting help with insulin resistance?

A small study found insulin sensitivity improved when people ate only early in the day, but weight loss was ultimately decided by how much people ate. If you take diabetes medication, there's a risk of low blood sugar, so be sure to talk to your doctor first. I summarized the research in [intermittent fasting results](/en/blog/intermittent-fasting-a-secret-for-those-who-gained-weight-over-the-holidays/).
