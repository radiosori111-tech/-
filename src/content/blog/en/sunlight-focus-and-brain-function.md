When your focus slips, there's something to check before reaching for coffee: whether you got proper sunlight during the day. According to a University of Manchester study, <strong>people exposed to bright daylight had reaction times 7 to 10% faster on average.</strong> After I switched my routine to morning runs, an unexplained lethargy gradually eased, and I began to wonder whether that might not be a coincidence.

## The days I lived without enough light

A few years ago, there was a period when unexplained stomach aches and digestive discomfort came almost every day. The hospital found nothing in particular, and I put it down to my sensitive nature and pushed through. Then, after I moved my running from evenings to mornings, at some point I began to feel my body getting a little lighter.

At first I assumed it was because my workout intensity or sleep had changed. But there was exactly one thing that was different: I had started moving at a time of day when I was fully in the sun. Wondering <strong>"Could light have changed something?"</strong>, I began looking into the research.

It's generally <strong>thought that focus and cognitive function are governed by sleep and caffeine</strong>, but in my experience I sensed something far more fundamental at work. The clue lay in the **circadian rhythm**, the body's internal biological clock that repeats on a roughly 24-hour cycle and governs almost every bodily function, from sleep and wakefulness to hormone release and body temperature. And research has repeatedly confirmed that <strong>the external signal that most powerfully sets this rhythm is none other than light</strong> ([Source: Health Chosun](https://health.chosun.com/site/data/html_dir/2026/01/13/2026011302989.html)).

<strong>In short:</strong> light is the key signal that sets the circadian rhythm, and morning sunlight alone can change how your body feels.

## Cells inside the eye wake up the brain

Researchers at the University of Manchester followed 58 adults for seven days, measuring their focus and reaction times with a wrist-worn light-exposure sensor and a smartphone app called Brightertime. Because the study took place in real daily life rather than in a lab, I find the results fairly trustworthy.

The results were clear. The more bright, consistent light people were exposed to during the day, the longer they sustained attention and the faster they reacted. In numbers, reaction times improved by 7 to 10% on average. That's not just a matter of mood; it's a measurable change. Honestly, this surprised me. At first it was hard to grasp that light could affect reaction speed so directly.

The researchers <strong>explain that this effect begins with intrinsically photosensitive retinal ganglion cells (ipRGCs)</strong>, special neurons in the retina that, rather than helping us see objects, <strong>handle non-visual functions such as setting the circadian rhythm, the pupil reflex, and mood and cognition</strong>. These cells contain a protein called **melanopsin**, which <strong>responds especially to blue-green wavelengths of light and sends signals that set the state of alertness across the brain and body.</strong>

There was another interesting finding: <strong>the clearer the contrast between daytime and nighttime light, the better people's cognitive function.</strong> In other words, focus rose the more consistently the environment stayed bright by day and dark by night. Reading this, I thought, <strong>"Working late and staring at a bright monitor wasn't just disturbing my sleep; it was eating away at my brain function itself."</strong>

- Bright light during the day → reaction times 7 to 10% faster on average
- The greater the contrast between light and dark, the bigger the boost to cognition
- People who habitually go to bed early show a clearer alertness response to changes in light
- Light turned out to be a more important variable than the time of day or how long people had been awake

I don't think this study can be summed up as simply "take a walk in the morning." <strong>The key is how consistently you maintain a bright environment during the day.</strong> <strong>Even indoors, simply sitting near a window or keeping the lighting bright may stimulate the ipRGCs.</strong>

<strong>In short:</strong> ipRGCs and melanopsin in the eye receive light signals and set the brain's alertness and cognition, and the greater the day-night contrast in light, the larger the boost to focus.

## When I actually built it into my routine, things changed

In my case, the only change was switching from evening runs to morning runs, and that alone gradually eased my lethargy and digestive discomfort. Of course, I can't say for sure whether this is causation or correlation. But it's interesting that the Manchester findings and my experience overlap.

Dr. Altug Didikoglu, who led the study, said that <strong>"light is a key environmental factor that regulates the human body clock, sleep, and cognitive function."</strong> It's already known that artificial light at night disrupts sleep, but this study <strong>deserves attention because it showed, in real life, how important bright daytime light is for maintaining brain function</strong>.

Here is <strong>the light-exposure routine</strong> I put together as I felt the changes myself: get at least 30 minutes of natural light in the morning, choose a seat by the window when working indoors, and dim the lights from one to two hours before bed. If all three are hard to manage at once, I think morning sunlight alone is the most effective place to start ([Source: Health Chosun](https://health.chosun.com/site/data/html_dir/2026/01/13/2026011302989.html)).

I think it's right to approach light exposure not as a "mood boost" but as a matter of setting up your environment for cognitive function. If a cup of coffee artificially pushes up **dopamine**, <strong>morning sunlight is closer to aligning the circadian rhythm itself to the right phase.</strong> <strong>Phase describes where the body clock is, and in what state, at a given point in the day</strong>; if the timing of light exposure is off, the whole circadian rhythm can be thrown out of joint.

<strong>In short:</strong> three routines, natural light in the morning, working by a window during the day, and dimming the lights before bed, are enough to correct the phase of your circadian rhythm and protect cognitive function.

## Frequently asked questions

### Does morning sunlight still help on cloudy days?

The stimulus is weaker than on a perfectly clear day, but outdoor light on a cloudy day is still far brighter than indoor lighting. Because ipRGCs respond not only to absolute brightness but also to relative changes in light, going outside on a cloudy day is likely to be a more meaningful stimulus than staying indoors.

### Is exercising at night really bad for focus?

I think the bigger problem is not the exercise itself but "being exposed to bright light at night." Strong artificial lighting during evening workouts can stimulate melanopsin cells and push the phase of your circadian rhythm later. That can lead to a vicious cycle of later bedtimes and lower alertness the next day.

### I work indoors and don't have a window seat. What can I do?

In that case, using a high-intensity LED light that includes blue-green wavelengths, or a light therapy lamp, for about 30 minutes in the morning is often mentioned as one option. That said, I haven't tried a light therapy lamp myself yet, and I think we need to wait and see whether it can fully replace natural light. Realistically, the most accessible approach is to use your lunch break to step outside for even 10 to 15 minutes.

### If I don't go to bed early, does morning sunlight not work?

The study found that people who habitually went to bed early tended to respond more sensitively to changes in light. That doesn't mean it doesn't work for people who stay up late. It seems more appropriate to read it as meaning that light works better when the phase of your circadian rhythm is already aligned. Gradually moving your bedtime earlier while also getting morning sunlight is likely to be more effective.

## Conclusion

When we want to improve focus, <strong>we usually look for something to add: supplements, caffeine, more sleep.</strong> But the direction this study and my experience both point to was a little different. Rather than adding something, the first step is <strong>properly setting up the environment itself: bright by day, dark by night.</strong>

Having experienced it myself, the change isn't dramatic. A 30-minute outdoor run in the morning, a seat by the window during the day, and dimmer lights in the evening were enough for my circadian rhythm to start responding. If you want to sort out your light-exposure habits, stepping outside for just 10 minutes at lunch today could be the smallest first step.
