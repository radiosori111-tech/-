## 📌 Key Takeaways

> **The short answer:** Games can sharpen some kinds of attention, but playing longer doesn't make your brain better. LCK pro gamers made **less than half as many errors** as non-gamers at moments when they shouldn't respond, but because the study compared people at a single point in time, we can't tell whether gaming training caused this or whether people who were already like that became pro players. Whether in games or study, practice with **goals, feedback, and review** mattered more than hours.

- 🎮 **Pro gamers made less than half the errors.** Comparing 23 LCK players with 20 non-gamers, the players responded wrongly when they shouldn't have an average of 2.6 times, less than half the non-gamers' 5.7 (**Scientific Reports**, 2026).
- 🧠 **Their attention-catching circuit was more strongly connected.** The players had stronger connections between the **insula**, which notices unexpected stimuli, and the **anterior cingulate cortex**, which coordinates action, and the stronger the connection, the fewer the errors.
- 🔬 **Short game training raised some attention skills.** When non-gamers played an action game for 10 days, their visual attention improved (**Nature**, 2003), and an analysis pooling many experiments confirmed small-to-medium effects (**Psychological Bulletin**, 2018).
- ⏱️ **Practice hours alone didn't explain skill.** In an analysis of 88 studies, the amount of deliberate practice explained about 26% of the differences in game performance (**Psychological Science**, 2014). The rest came from other factors such as practice quality, innate ability, and starting age.

### 💬 "I thought seeing more would make me better"

When I first heard about this study, it surprised me. I had vaguely believed that to be good at games you obviously had to take in more, faster. But the players' brains differed less in "seeing more" than in "filtering better."

My own experience was similar. Back when I checked my form in the gym mirror on every set, my focus actually scattered. When I kept only the one sensation I needed to feel in that movement, it got much better. Before runs, I spent about 30 seconds picturing the pace I'd hold and the feel of my feet hitting the ground, and my pace stayed steady the whole way. When studying, instead of skimming text with my eyes, I sketched the skeleton of the key concepts on blank paper, and I got less tired while remembering more.

The original version of this post, titled "Why Pro Gamers' Brains Got Sharper Despite 10 Hours of Play a Day," explained that training had changed pro gamers' brains. Looking at the research again, the results themselves held up, but the parts that asserted a cause needed correcting.

## Deep Dive: How, and how far, do games change the brain?

### 1. How did pro gamers' brains differ from everyone else's?

A joint team from Chung-Ang University's Department of Psychiatry and Gachon University's Department of Biomedical Engineering compared brain MRIs and attention test results between 23 LCK (League of Legends Champions Korea) pro gamers and 20 people who don't play games (**Scientific Reports**, 2026; as reported by Science Times).

- **Errors**: The number of wrong responses to signals that called for no response averaged **2.6** for players and **5.7** for non-gamers.
- **Stronger connection**: The connection between the **insula**, which notices unexpected stimuli and shifts attention, and the **anterior cingulate cortex**, which takes that signal and coordinates action, was stronger. The stronger the connection, the fewer the errors.
- **Weaker connection**: The connection between the **thalamus**, which relays sensory information, and the anterior cingulate cortex was weaker, and the weaker this connection, the higher the working memory scores.
- **Cortical thickness**: The cortex of the insula and anterior cingulate was thicker than in non-gamers.

The team interpreted the weaker thalamic connection as "the brain reallocating resources." The first version of this post asserted this meant "energy for emotional processing is redirected to cognitive processing," but that is the team's interpretation, not something they measured directly.

### 2. So did gaming training change their brains?

This study alone can't tell us. Because it **compared players and non-gamers at a single point in time**, it can't distinguish whether training changed their brains or whether people with already-strong attention circuits were selected as players. There were also only 43 participants. To say, as the original title did, that "the brain got sharper despite gaming," you need experiments that compare the same people before and after training.

There are a few such experiments.

- Daphne Bavelier's team at the University of Rochester had non-gamers play an action game for one hour a day for 10 days. Their **visual attention** improved more than that of people who played a puzzle game (Tetris) (Green & Bavelier, **Nature**, 2003).
- A team at Germany's Max Planck Institute had adults play a 3D game (Super Mario 64) for at least 30 minutes a day for two months, and their gray matter in the **hippocampus** and **dorsolateral prefrontal cortex** increased compared with people who didn't play (Kühn et al., **Molecular Psychiatry**, 2014).
- In an analysis pooling many studies, the difference between action gamers and non-gamers was medium-sized (0.55), but smaller in training experiments (0.34) (Bediou et al., **Psychological Bulletin**, 2018). This suggests that some of the difference seen in comparison studies may arise because people who already have these traits enjoy such games.

In short, games can raise **some kinds of attention**, but the effect is smaller than comparison studies suggest, and it varies by genre and how you play.

### 3. What sets skill apart: hours, or the structure of practice?

The first version wrote that "what changes the brain is not the amount of time but the structure of training." That direction fits the research. Korean pro gamers train 10–14 hours a day, but that time includes not just repeated play but also **strategy meetings, match analysis, and team meetings**.

Swedish psychologist Anders Ericsson called skill-building practice **deliberate practice**: clear goals, immediate feedback, and targeted repetition of weaknesses matter more than simply doing a lot (Ericsson et al., **Psychological Review**, 1993).

But practice wasn't everything. When a Princeton University team analyzed 88 studies, the amount of deliberate practice explained **about 26%** of differences in game performance, 21% in music, and 18% in sports (Macnamara et al., **Psychological Science**, 2014). Practice matters, but most of the rest came from other factors such as practice quality, starting age, and innate ability.

### 4. Do mental rehearsal and "output" study work?

- **Mental rehearsal**: In an analysis of 35 studies, **mental practice**, rehearsing a movement in your head, improved performance, but less than physical practice did (Driskell et al., **Journal of Applied Psychology**, 1994). The earlier version's phrase "it warms up the brain's motor areas in advance" went too far. It's better seen as something to add to real practice rather than a replacement for it.
- **Output study**: Students who practiced recalling material rather than rereading it remembered more a week later (Roediger & Karpicke, **Psychological Science**, 2006). That's why my method of sketching the skeleton of concepts on blank paper worked. I covered this in more detail in the post on [five minutes of study a day](/en/blog/five-minutes-a-day-why-the-brain-really-changes-neuroplasticity/).

### 5. Is gaming good for teenagers' brain development?

It depends. When an Oxford University team analyzed 120,000 adolescents in England, moderate daily gaming was not harmful to mental well-being, and only very long play showed a small negative association (Przybylski & Weinstein, **Psychological Science**, 2017). It's an observational study, so cause and effect can't be established, but neither "games are always harmful" nor "the longer the better" fits the research.

The World Esports Summit details in the first version (Busan, July 2025, "WES 2026") had years that didn't match each other and couldn't be verified, so I removed them in this revision.

## Solution: 3 steps to change the structure of practice instead of the hours

Choose something you want to get better at right now. You'll see a practice structure and the evidence behind it.

**Step 1. Set just one goal before you start**
Whether gaming, exercising, or studying, set a goal of "just this one thing today" before you begin. The key is to narrow where you focus rather than to take in more.

**Step 2. Review just one scene afterward**
Rewatch or recall one scene that didn't go well, and write one line about what you'll change next time. It's a pro team's match analysis scaled down to personal size.

**Step 3. Recall rather than reread**
Instead of rereading what you learned, sketch it on blank paper or explain it out loud. The places where you get stuck become the goal of your next practice.

## Conclusion

What the pro gamer brain study showed was not that "playing games for a long time makes your brain better." The players' brains differed less in seeing more than in filtering better, and their days held not just play but goals, feedback, and review. We still don't know whether that difference is thanks to training. What is clear is that, in gaming, exercise, or study alike, a **structure of deciding what to focus on and looking back when you're done** changes practice more than adding hours. Pick one thing you do today and spend the 30 seconds before you start choosing a single goal.

### 🙋 FAQ

#### Does playing games for a long time make your brain better?

Playing longer doesn't make it better. There are experiments where short action-game training raised visual attention, but the effects were small to medium and varied by game type. Among teens who played very long hours, a small negative association also appeared. Whether your sleep and daily life are protected comes before gaming time.

#### What changes when the insula–anterior cingulate connection is stronger?

This connection is involved in noticing unexpected stimuli and shifting attention. In the study, people with a stronger connection made fewer errors at moments when they shouldn't respond. However, no method has been shown to deliberately strengthen this connection, so we can't say any particular training grows this circuit.
