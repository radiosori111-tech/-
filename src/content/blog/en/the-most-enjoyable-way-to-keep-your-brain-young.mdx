## 📌 Key takeaways

> **The short answer:** New, slightly challenging hobbies can improve some cognitive skills even later in life. Older adults who learned **piano** for six months improved on an attention-switching test. But older adults who took **language classes** for 11 weeks showed no change in memory. For lowering dementia risk, overall lifestyle, including **social connection**, **hearing**, and **exercise**, mattered more than any single hobby.

- 🎹 **Six months of piano improved attention switching.** Among 31 adults aged 60–85, those who took individual piano lessons for six months improved more on tests of attention switching and processing speed than those who didn't (**Aging & Mental Health**, 2007). It was a small study.
- 🗣️ **Eleven weeks of language classes didn't change memory.** When 160 Swedish adults aged 65–75 took Italian classes for 11 weeks, they did no better on memory and intelligence tests than those who took relaxation classes (**Psychology and Aging**, 2020).
- 🌍 **Lifelong bilinguals showed dementia symptoms later.** In a study of 184 people with dementia, those who had used two languages all their lives developed symptoms about 4 years later (**Neuropsychologia**, 2007). But it was observational, so language can't be singled out as the cause.
- 🤝 **About 45% of dementia was linked to modifiable factors.** In 2024 the Lancet Commission named 14 factors, including social isolation, hearing loss, physical inactivity, and depression (**Lancet**, 2024).

### 💬 "Can a hobby bring back the words on the tip of my tongue?"

I often had the experience of turning around and finding the words I'd meant to say had vanished from the tip of my tongue. At first I put it down to tiredness, but as it happened more often, it started to bother me.

So I took out the ukulele I'd put away long ago. The results were dismal, but I found the throbbing-head feeling of the first few days interesting. I translated the lyrics of pop songs I liked, and spent 10 minutes a day repeating foreign-language words with a phone app. I also went to a small group of people with similar interests for a month, and on my way home I sometimes took side streets I'd never walked before.

When I looked up the original research, what had been confirmed was mixed with things that hadn't lived up to expectations. ## Deep Dive: which hobbies help an aging brain?

### 1. Does learning an instrument later in life help the brain?

It helped somewhat. A University of Florida team split 31 adults aged 60–85 who had never learned an instrument, and gave only one group individual piano lessons for six months (Bugos et al., **Aging & Mental Health**, 2007).

- The piano group improved more on an **attention-switching** test, alternately connecting numbers and letters, and on a **processing speed** test.
- The group without lessons showed no such change.

There's also an observational study in which older adults who had played instruments for many years did better on tests of memory and executive function than those who hadn't (Hanna-Pladdy & MacKay, **Neuropsychology**, 2011). Both studies involved only a few dozen participants, though.

I found no study directly supporting the original post's explanation that "multisensory integration engages the left and right brain at once and raises synaptic density." Playing an instrument does use several senses together, but there's no evidence that it raises synaptic density.

### 2. Can learning a foreign language delay dementia?

There are two kinds of results here.

- **People who used two languages all their lives**: When a team in Toronto, Canada, studied 184 people with dementia, those who had used two languages throughout life developed symptoms about **4 years** later than those who used one (Bialystok et al., **Neuropsychologia**, 2007). But because it was observational, differences other than language may have played a role.
- **People who took a language class later in life**: A Swedish team had 160 adults aged 65–75 take 11 weeks of either Italian classes or relaxation classes. The language group's memory and intelligence scores didn't rise any more (Berggren et al., **Psychology and Aging**, 2020).

In short, learning a foreign language is enjoyable and meaningful, but the experimental evidence that a few months of study improves memory is still weak. The original post's description of language learning as "the most accessible high-intensity brain training" was an exaggeration.

### 3. What about brain-training apps and games?

The original post said "puzzle and strategy games that make you repeatedly judge and choose are good for cognitive training." A broad review of brain-training research concluded that people get better at the tasks they train on, but there is little evidence that **untrained everyday abilities** improve too (Simons et al., **Psychological Science in the Public Interest**, 2016). Enjoying games is fine, but it's hard to expect them alone to slow brain aging.

### 4. What matters more than hobbies?

Your whole lifestyle. In 2024 the Lancet Commission pooled research from around the world and concluded that about **45%** of dementia cases are linked to 14 modifiable factors (Livingston et al., **Lancet**, 2024).

- These included less education in childhood, hearing loss, high LDL cholesterol, depression, head injury, physical inactivity, diabetes, smoking, high blood pressure, obesity, heavy drinking, **social isolation**, air pollution, and untreated vision loss.
- It means that **everyday factors** such as joining a group, using a hearing aid, and walking are a bigger picture than any one hobby.

I found no experiment directly showing the original post's claim that "walking new side streets stimulates the hippocampus and helps preserve memory." But walking outdoors does help reduce one risk factor, physical inactivity. I covered sleep's role in memory in [how the brain sorts memories while you sleep](/en/articles/sleep-memory/).

**Step 1. Keep up one new, slightly challenging hobby for six months**
Choose a hobby, like an instrument, that's awkward enough that you have to think, and keep it up for 15 minutes a day for several months. The piano study also lasted six months. Consistency matters more than polish.

**Step 2. Turn a solo hobby into one you share**
Try joining a group where people learn a language or an instrument. Adding social contact to learning also reduces social isolation, one of the dementia risk factors.

**Step 3. Check the basics: walking, hearing, and sleep**
Walk 150 minutes a week, and if speech is hard to hear, get a hearing test. At night, get enough sleep to give your brain time to sort what you learned.

## Conclusion

When I took the ukulele back out, I was hoping for a dramatic change that would "switch my brain back on." The answer the research gave was a little calmer. New, challenging learning like piano improved some cognitive skills, but a few months of language classes didn't improve memory. And for lowering dementia risk, overall lifestyle, including social contact, walking, and hearing, played a bigger role than any one hobby. So pick one enjoyable hobby, and start it not alone but with someone.

### 🙋 FAQ

#### Is it too late to take up an instrument at my age?

It's not too late. The participants in the piano study were also 60–85 and had never learned an instrument. But the effects were seen only on some tests, so it's better to choose a hobby you can enjoy for a long time than to treat it as a "prescription" for brain health.

#### Should I see a doctor if I'm forgetting things more often?

Forgetfulness that doesn't interfere with daily life is common as we age. But if you repeat the same questions, get lost on familiar routes, or memory problems last several weeks, see a neurologist or psychiatrist. There may be treatable causes such as depression, thyroid disease, or hearing loss.
