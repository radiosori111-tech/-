## 📌 Key takeaways

- 🔋 Magnesium does its work by binding to **ATP**, the cell's energy currency, to form **Mg-ATP**. **More than 600 enzymes** depend on it (**Physiological Reviews**, 2015). It is the cell factory's **main switch**, and **Nature** (2016) showed that magnesium inside cells rises and falls on a daily cycle, tuning how fast cells spend energy.
- 🚢 The same magnesium is absorbed very differently **depending on the molecule it's paired with**. In one study, **magnesium oxide** had a fractional absorption of only about <strong>4%</strong> (**Magnesium Research**, 2001), while **magnesium citrate** was the best absorbed of the forms compared (**Magnesium Research**, 2003).
- 🧠 **Magnesium L-threonate** raised brain magnesium in rats, increased synapses in the **hippocampus**, and improved learning and memory (**Neuron**, 2010). Human studies, however, are still small.
- ⚖️ Magnesium supplementation tended to improve markers of **insulin sensitivity** (a 2016 meta-analysis of randomised controlled trials), and magnesium is also an essential helper for the enzymes that make **glutathione**, which is linked to skin tone.
- 😴 Magnesium acts as a **brake for sleep**, locking excitatory signals and helping calming ones. One analysis found it shortened the time to fall asleep by about 17 minutes on average in older adults with insomnia, though the evidence is still low quality (**BMC Complementary Medicine and Therapies**, 2021).

### 💬 Does this sound familiar?

One day, after months of saying you're always tired, someone suggests: "Try magnesium." At the pharmacy shelf there's a whole line of names: oxide, citrate, glycinate, even L-threonate. You pick the cheapest one with the highest dose and take it for a month. The fatigue stays; you just visit the bathroom more often. And you think: *maybe magnesium just doesn't work for me.*

In fact, the problem was quite possibly not magnesium but its **form**. This post is about where each of those labels goes once it's inside you.

## 🔬 Deep Dive: how magnesium reaches your cells

### 1. The factory's main switch: Mg-ATP

Every cell in the body works on an energy currency called **ATP**. But ATP on its own can barely be used. Only when a magnesium ion binds to it, forming the **Mg-ATP complex**, do enzymes recognise it and draw energy out. Magnesium is less the electricity itself than the **main switch** that connects the electricity to the machines.

**More than 600 enzymes** need this switch (de Baaij et al., **Physiological Reviews**, 2015). Muscle contraction and relaxation, nerve signalling, copying DNA and building proteins all pass through it.

A 2016 study in **Nature** went a step further. In both human cells and single-celled algae, magnesium levels inside the cell **rose and fell on a daily cycle**, and with them the rate at which cells spent energy and built proteins (Feeney et al., 2016). Magnesium turned out to be not just a switch but also a **dimmer** that sets the rhythm of the day.

### 2. The partner decides the fate: inorganic salts, organic salts and chelates

Magnesium can't go into a tablet on its own, so it always comes paired with another molecule. That partner decides what happens in the gut. Picture magnesium as cargo, and the partner as the **ship** carrying it: the type of ship determines how it reaches the harbour, the cells lining your intestine.

**🪨 Inorganic salts (magnesium oxide and others): a freighter anchored outside the harbour**
Magnesium oxide packs a lot of magnesium into each tablet, but it dissolves poorly in water. Undissolved magnesium can't cross the gut wall; it stays in the intestine and pulls in water by **osmosis**. That softens stools, which is why it has long been used as a laxative. In a comparison of US commercial products, magnesium oxide's fractional absorption was only about <strong>4%</strong> (Firoz & Graber, 2001). It falls short for topping up your body's stores, but it does its job of getting the gut moving.

**🍋 Organic salts (magnesium citrate, gluconate, malate and others): ships that dissolve and sail into port**
Magnesium paired with organic acids such as citric or malic acid dissolves readily in water. Magnesium has to dissolve into ions before it can pass through channels in the gut lining, so solubility is the first gate to absorption. In a randomised, double-blind study, **magnesium citrate** raised blood and urine magnesium more than magnesium oxide or an amino acid chelate (Walker et al., **Magnesium Research**, 2003).

**Magnesium gluconate** is another leading member of this group. Paired with gluconic acid, which is made from glucose, it dissolves very readily in water, much like citrate, and is relatively less likely to irritate the stomach or cause a laxative effect such as diarrhoea, which is why it is often used in liquid and powder products. In a stable-isotope study comparing **ten** organic and inorganic salts in magnesium-depleted rats, magnesium gluconate showed the **highest** bioavailability (Coudray et al., **Magnesium Research**, 2005). Think of it as a fast boat that carries a light load and slips quickly into port. Its elemental magnesium content by weight is low, though, at **about 5–6%**, so you need more of it to get the same amount, and taking a lot at once can still loosen stools like other forms.

**Magnesium malate** pairs magnesium with malic acid, an ingredient in the cycle cells use to make ATP (the TCA cycle). That's why it is often promoted as "magnesium for energy," but evidence that it reduces fatigue in people is still lacking.

**🧬 Amino acid chelates (glycinate, L-threonate and others): ships that may carry a pass**
In a chelate, amino acids grip magnesium like tongs. Commonly cited advantages are that it breaks apart less in the stomach and is gentler on the gut, causing less diarrhoea. Some have **proposed** that these complexes can use a separate entrance, amino acid and peptide transporters such as **PEPT1**, but how much this route actually contributes in humans hasn't been well established. Honestly, it's a ship that *might* be carrying a pass.

### 3. L-threonate and the checkpoint in front of the brain

The brain is surrounded by a strict checkpoint called the <strong>blood–brain barrier (BBB)</strong>, so a rise in blood magnesium doesn't automatically raise magnesium in the brain.

In a 2010 study in **Neuron**, researchers compared several forms of magnesium and developed a new one, <strong>magnesium L-threonate (MgT)</strong>. Rats given it had higher magnesium in their cerebrospinal fluid, greater **synapse density** in the **hippocampus**, the brain's memory hub, and performed better on learning and on short- and long-term memory tasks (Slutsky et al., 2010). The researchers pointed to stronger signalling through hippocampal **NMDA receptors** as the key. NMDA receptors are **gatekeepers** that decide whether to strengthen connections between neurons. Magnesium normally acts as a **safety lock** on this gate, blocking background noise so that important signals come through more clearly.

In people, a 12-week study of **44** adults aged 50 to 70 reported improved cognitive scores (Liu et al., **Journal of Alzheimer's Disease**, 2016). It was small, though, and funded by the manufacturer, so larger independent studies are needed.

### 4. Glutathione and skin tone: magnesium lends a hand

**Glutathione** is one of the main antioxidants that cells make for themselves. The body links three amino acids together in two enzyme steps, and both enzymes run on **Mg-ATP**. When magnesium runs low, the switch on this assembly line loosens.

The link to skin lies in **melanin**. Melanin is made along two branches: dark brown **eumelanin** and light, yellowish **pheomelanin**. When a cell has plenty of glutathione and cysteine, production tilts towards the <strong>lighter branch (pheomelanin)</strong>, like a railway **switch** turning the train onto a different track. A small clinical study found that people who took glutathione for four weeks had a slightly lower skin melanin index (Arjinpathana & Asawanonda, 2012).

One thing has to be said clearly: <strong>there is no clinical study yet showing that magnesium supplements lighten skin.</strong> Magnesium is an essential helper in the glutathione factory, not a whitening agent. For someone who is low, topping up may help the antioxidant system work properly, and that is as far as the science currently goes.

### 5. Insulin's keyhole and body fat

When **insulin** knocks on a cell's door, the **insulin receptor** on the cell surface activates an enzyme inside called a **tyrosine kinase**, sending the signal "let glucose in." This kinase also runs on Mg-ATP. When magnesium is short, insulin can put its key in but **the lock turns stiffly**, and blood sugar that isn't used tends to end up stored as fat.

A meta-analysis of **18** randomised controlled trials found that magnesium supplementation lowered fasting blood glucose and, in people at risk of diabetes, improved markers of insulin sensitivity (Simental-Mendía et al., **Pharmacological Research**, 2016). That doesn't mean magnesium makes you lose weight. **Putting a missing switch back in place so metabolism can run normally** is the honest share magnesium can contribute to weight management.

### 6. 😴 A hand on the brakes of sleep: magnesium, sleep and a calm nervous system

Some nights you lie down at the end of the day and your mind just won't switch off. Your body is tired, but your thoughts keep running and your legs feel oddly tense. To fall asleep, the brain and body have to lift their foot off the **accelerator** and press the **brake**, and magnesium has a hand on several points along the way.

#### 🧠 The brake pedal: helping GABA receptors

Inside the brain, **glutamate**, which excites neurons, and **GABA**, which calms them, always sit on opposite sides of a scale. As we drift towards sleep, that scale needs to tip towards GABA.

Magnesium moves the scale from both sides. On one side, it sits in the gate of the **NMDA receptor**, where glutamate comes and goes, like a **safety lock** that keeps excitatory signals from leaking through. On the other, it helps **GABA-A receptors** respond better to GABA, so neurons can quietly close their doors. Laboratory studies have reported that magnesium ions enhance the response of GABA-A receptors (Möykkynen et al., **NeuroReport**, 2001). Locking the accelerator while making the brake easier to press: this is the first way magnesium steadies the nervous system.

#### 🚨 The alarm volume: cortisol and the stress axis

Rats fed a magnesium-deficient diet showed more anxious behaviour, and the axis that releases stress hormones of the **cortisol** family (hypothalamus–pituitary–adrenal) became overactive (Sartori et al., **Neuropharmacology**, 2012). In the other direction, stress makes the body lose more magnesium in urine, which can set up a vicious circle: stress, then low magnesium, then even greater sensitivity.

In people, one study found that **264** adults with low blood magnesium and high stress had lower stress scores after taking magnesium for 8 weeks (including a group that also took vitamin B6) (Pouteau et al., **PLoS One**, 2018). The study had no placebo group, though, which limits how far it can be read.

#### 🌙 The night-time traffic light: melatonin

**Melatonin** is the hormone released by the **pineal gland**, in the middle of the brain, when it gets dark; it switches on the traffic light that says "time to sleep." In rats fed a magnesium-deficient diet, blood melatonin fell (Billyard et al., **Magnesium Research**, 2006), and in the insomnia study of older adults described below, melatonin rose after magnesium supplementation. Melatonin is made from serotonin through several enzyme steps, and magnesium is thought to be involved in this process and in signalling within pineal cells. Exactly which step it contributes to, and how much, is still being studied.

The **Nature** (2016) finding fits in here too. Magnesium inside cells itself rises and falls on a daily cycle, working like a **metronome** that keeps the cell's clock in time. Magnesium is a brake for sleep, and also a hand that keeps the beat of the daily rhythm.

#### 💪 The muscle switch: calcium turns it on, magnesium turns it off

When a muscle contracts, **calcium** is the switch that turns it on; when it relaxes again, **magnesium** is the switch that turns it off. In nerve and muscle cells, magnesium competes with calcium and calms calcium channels, and the calcium pumps a contracted muscle needs in order to relax again also run on Mg-ATP. That is why low magnesium can leave nerves and muscles easily excited, leading to twitches or cramps. When the body's tension eases, the brain can cross the threshold into sleep more easily.

So can magnesium prevent night-time leg cramps? Honestly, **the results are mixed**. A Cochrane systematic review of randomised controlled trials concluded that magnesium is **unlikely to provide meaningful prevention** for older adults with cramps of unknown cause, and for leg cramps during pregnancy the findings differed between studies (Garrison et al., **Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews**, 2020). It may help people who are genuinely low in magnesium, but if you get cramps often, it's worth looking into other causes too, such as dehydration, medications or circulation problems.

#### 📈 How much did it help in people?

In a randomised, double-blind trial, **46** older adults with insomnia took **500 mg** of magnesium daily for 8 weeks (as magnesium oxide in this study). Compared with placebo, their insomnia scores, sleep efficiency and time to fall asleep improved, blood **melatonin** rose and **cortisol** fell (Abbasi et al., **Journal of Research in Medical Sciences**, 2012). A systematic review pooling three such trials found that older adults taking magnesium fell asleep **about 17 minutes** faster on average than with placebo (Mah & Pitre, **BMC Complementary Medicine and Therapies**, 2021). The authors rated the evidence as low quality, though, and 500 mg was a study dose used under supervision that exceeds the usual supplement upper limit (350 mg).

#### 🌿 Why bisglycinate is called "the sleep magnesium"

**Magnesium bisglycinate** is a form in which two molecules of the amino acid **glycine** wrap around a single magnesium. There are three reasons it is so often recommended for the evening.

1. **It keeps the gut quiet.** It places little burden on the gut, such as diarrhoea, so you're less likely to be up in the night for the bathroom after taking it before bed. Magnesium oxide in the evening, by contrast, can keep your gut busy.
2. **Its partner, glycine, also helps sleep.** Glycine is an inhibitory neurotransmitter that calms nerves in the brainstem and spinal cord. People who took **3 g** of glycine before bed reported better subjective sleep quality and less fatigue the next day (Yamadera et al., **Sleep and Biological Rhythms**, 2007). Animal research has shown that glycine acts on the **suprachiasmatic nucleus**, the hub of the body clock, widening blood vessels in the hands and feet and **lowering core body temperature**, which prepares the body for sleep (Kawai et al., **Neuropsychopharmacology**, 2015).
3. **It works on sensitive nerves in two ways.** Magnesium supports the GABA brake, while glycine switches on another calming circuit. It's a combination that suits nights when stress keeps you from falling asleep.

That said, a bisglycinate supplement providing 200 mg of elemental magnesium contains **about 1.2 g** of glycine, less than the 3 g used in research. It's more accurate to treat the glycine effect as a bonus and to see the biggest advantage as a form that's gentle on the gut and easy to take in the evening. Few human studies have directly compared its effect on sleep with other forms of magnesium.

Magnesium is not a sleeping pill that forces sleep down on you; it's closer to **topping up brake fluid that had run low**. If sleepless nights go on for more than three months, a sleep specialist should come before a supplement.

## ✅ The fix: three steps to using magnesium well

1. <strong>🥬 Step 1: Fill up from your plate first.</strong> Recommended intake for adults is roughly **360–420 mg a day for men and 280–320 mg for women** (exact figures vary by country). Leafy greens such as spinach, nuts and seeds like pumpkin seeds and almonds, beans, brown rice and high-cocoa dark chocolate are good sources. Magnesium from food carries almost no risk of excess.
2. <strong>🎯 Step 2: Choose the form for your purpose.</strong> If constipation is the concern, **magnesium oxide**; to top up efficiently, **magnesium citrate**; to absorb well while going easier on your stomach, **magnesium gluconate**; if your gut is sensitive or your goal is better sleep and less tension, **glycinate**; if memory is your interest, **L-threonate**, which is still being studied. Always check the label for the amount of **elemental magnesium**.
3. <strong>⏰ Step 3: Split the dose and respect the upper limit.</strong> For adults, the upper limit for magnesium from supplements is **350 mg a day**. Splitting it into two doses rather than taking it all at once improves absorption and reduces diarrhoea. Keep it **at least 2 hours** apart from calcium or iron supplements and some antibiotics. If sleep is your goal, move one of those two doses to the evening.

> 🌙 **If better sleep is your goal**
>
> - **When:** Any time from after dinner to 1–2 hours before bed works well. Taking it at the same time every day turns it into a "bedtime cue."
> - **Which:** **Glycinate**, which is gentle on the gut, is the easiest choice. Take magnesium oxide in the morning rather than the evening.
> - **How much:** Start low, at around 100–200 mg of elemental magnesium a day, and give it 2–4 weeks. Keep your total from supplements under 350 mg a day.
> - **Alongside:** Dim the lights an hour before bed, put screens away, and keep caffeine to the early afternoon. Magnesium is one piece of a sleep routine; it can't replace the whole routine.

## 🙋 Frequently asked questions

### Q1. When is the best time to take magnesium?

Absorption isn't known to change much with the time of day, so taking it **consistently at the same time every day** matters most. If it upsets your stomach when empty, take it after a meal. Many people take gentler forms such as glycinate in the evening or before bed; attaching it to your wind-down routine makes it easier to remember. If sleep is your goal, 1–2 hours before bed works well, as in the 🌙 tips above.

### Q2. Isn't more better? Are there side effects?

Magnesium is not a nutrient where more is better. Going over the supplement upper limit (**350 mg a day**) easily causes diarrhoea, stomach cramps and nausea. People with **reduced kidney function** in particular may not clear magnesium properly, and blood levels can rise dangerously, so always talk to your doctor first. If you take blood pressure medication, diuretics, osteoporosis drugs or antibiotics, ask a pharmacist to check for interactions.
