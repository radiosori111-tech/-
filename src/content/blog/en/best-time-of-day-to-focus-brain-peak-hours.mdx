## 📌 Key takeaways

> **The short answer:** It varies, but focus is usually best **a few hours after waking, when body temperature rises**, not right after you get up. Morning types remembered better in the morning and evening types in the afternoon. Interestingly, at their **least alert hours** people solved more **insight problems**, the kind that need a flash of inspiration rather than analysis. So put analytical work at your peak and idea work outside it.

- ⏰ **Right after waking isn't the peak.** The stress hormone **cortisol** is highest 30–45 minutes after waking (**Life Sciences**, 1997), but performance on memory and calculation was better during the day as body temperature rose (**American Journal of Physiology**, 2002).
- 🧓 **Peak times differ between people.** Older adults, mostly morning types, did better on memory tests in the morning; university students, mostly evening types, did better in the afternoon (**Psychological Science**, 1993).
- 💡 **Half-awake hours were better for creative problems.** 428 university students solved more insight problems at their non-optimal time of day, while analytical problems showed no difference by time (**Thinking & Reasoning**, 2011).
- 📉 **The same test scored lower later in the day.** In about 2 million Danish school tests, scores fell the later in the day the test was taken, and students who had a 20–30 minute break just before scored higher (**PNAS**, 2016).
- 🌙 **Bright light at night delays the sleep signal.** People in ordinary room light before bed had melatonin onset later and for a shorter time (**Journal of Clinical Endocrinology & Metabolism**, 2011).

### 💬 "They say do important work in the morning. So why won't my brain work then?"

You often hear "get the important work done first thing in the morning." But when you sit down to write a report right after arriving at work, your head may not cooperate, and on some days ideas come late in the afternoon instead. So is there a fixed time when the brain works best, or does it differ from person to person?

This post answers that question based on research into the 24-hour **body clock**, or **circadian rhythm**. It walks through how cortisol and melatonin rise and fall over the day, and how that rhythm affects focus, memory, and creativity.

## Deep Dive: when in the day does the brain work best?

### 1. Is focus best when cortisol is highest?

No. Cortisol is the hormone that wakes the body up in the morning. In measurements by a team at the University of Trier in Germany, salivary cortisol peaked **30–45 minutes** after waking, and this rise repeated similarly each day (Pruessner et al., **Life Sciences**, 1997). This is called the **cortisol awakening response**.

But that is a signal to get the body moving, not the peak of focus. Right after waking, the brain is still in **sleep inertia**, so judgment and reactions are dull for a while. I covered just how dull the brain is right after waking in [why your head feels foggy as soon as you wake up](/en/blog/the-early-bird-the-first-30-minutes-and-sleep-inertia/).

### 2. Then when does focus improve?

When body temperature rises. A Harvard team measured body temperature and performance together while controlling participants' schedules. The higher the body temperature, the better cognitive performance such as memory, calculation, and reaction speed, and performance was worst in the early morning, when body temperature was lowest (Wright et al., **American Journal of Physiology**, 2002).

For someone on a typical schedule, body temperature starts rising in the late morning and stays high from the afternoon into the early evening. But the exact timing depends on when you wake up and on whether you are a **morning or evening type**, discussed below. That's why no single clock time, such as "9 a.m.", fits everyone.

### 3. Do morning and evening types peak at different times?

They do. A team at Duke University gave memory tests to older adults and university students in the morning or afternoon. Most of the older adults were morning types and most of the students were evening types. The older adults remembered better in the morning and the students in the afternoon, and the gap between the two groups was largest in the afternoon tests (May et al., **Psychological Science**, 1993).

A review of many studies also concluded that effortful tasks such as attention and memory are performed better at times that match one's **chronotype**, or morning–evening tendency (Schmidt et al., **Cognitive Neuropsychology**, 2007). In other words, your peak is set by your body's rhythm, not by the clock.

### 4. Should you do nothing during your less alert hours?

They turned out to be useful. A team at Albion College in the U.S. assessed 428 university students' morning–evening tendencies, then had them solve two kinds of problems at their optimal and non-optimal times (Wieth & Zacks, **Thinking & Reasoning**, 2011).

- **Insight problems**, which need a flash of inspiration, were solved more often at the **non-optimal** time.
- **Analytical problems**, which need step-by-step reasoning, showed no difference by time of day.

The researchers suggested that at less alert times, the ability to hold attention narrowly weakens, so unexpected connections come to mind more easily. Off-peak hours, then, may not be wasted time but a good fit for idea work such as brainstorming and planning.

### 5. Does the same person do worse later in the day?

The day wears you down. A team from Harvard and Denmark analyzed about **2 million** national tests taken by Danish students (Sievertsen et al., **PNAS**, 2016).

- Scores fell a little for **each hour later** the test was taken.
- Students who had a **20–30 minute break** just before the test scored higher.

The researchers attributed this to mental fatigue that builds up over the day. A drop in afternoon performance may not be a lack of willpower but the result of a day without breaks. ### 6. What slows the brain's clock at night?

Bright light. **Melatonin** is the hormone released when it gets dark, signaling that it's time to sleep. When a Harvard team compared people who spent the 8 hours before bed in ordinary room light (about 200 lux) with people in dim light, melatonin onset came later in the room-light group and its duration was about 90 minutes shorter (Gooley et al., **Journal of Clinical Endocrinology & Metabolism**, 2011).

Staying in bright light late at night pushes the body clock later, and the next morning's peak may shift later with it. Your daily timetable, in effect, starts the night before.

**Step 1. Find your peak hours**
For a week, rate how well you can focus in the morning, afternoon, and evening on a scale of 1 to 5. The highest-scoring time is your peak. Right after waking usually isn't the peak, so use that time to warm up in the sunlight.

**Step 2. Match types of work to times of day**
Put analysis, writing, and hard decisions at your peak, and brainstorming and planning outside it. At less alert times, people solved more insight problems.

**Step 3. Protect your rhythm with breaks and dim evening light**
Take 20–30 minute breaks between focus blocks, and dim lights and screens 1–2 hours before bed. That keeps the next day's peak where it should be.

## Conclusion

"Do the important work in the morning" turned out to be only half right. Cortisol is highest in the morning, but the best time to focus came a few hours later as body temperature rose, and that time differed between morning and evening types. And the less alert hours weren't wasted time; they were when ideas came more easily. Starting today, rate your focus three times a day for a week and find your own brain timetable.

### 🙋 FAQ

#### I'm an evening type. Do I have to force myself to become a morning type?

Not necessarily. In the research, memory and focus were better at times that matched one's chronotype. But if you have fixed hours for work or school, simply getting morning sunlight and dimming the lights at night can help shift your body clock a little earlier.

#### My focus drops every afternoon. Is it weak willpower?

It's more likely mental fatigue that has built up over the day, plus the post-lunch rhythm. In the test study, too, scores fell later in the day but rose again after a 20–30 minute break. Try putting breaks on your afternoon schedule first.
