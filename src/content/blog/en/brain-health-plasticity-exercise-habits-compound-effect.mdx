## 📌 Key Takeaways

> **The short answer:** Even a late-start exercise habit changed the brain. When inactive people **in their 60s and 70s** did aerobic exercise like walking for six months, **brain volume** increased, and people around 60 gained **gray matter** by practicing a new skill. Starting early is good, but it's not too late to start now.

- 🧠 **People in their 60s and 70s grew brain volume in six months.** When inactive older adults did aerobic exercise three times a week for six months, unlike a stretching-only group, their **gray matter** and **white matter** volume increased (**Journal of Gerontology: Medical Sciences**, 2006).
- 🤹 **People around 60 gained gray matter with practice.** When people with an average age of 60 practiced juggling for three months, gray matter grew in a brain area that processes motion. The change was smaller than in younger people (**Journal of Neuroscience**, 2008).
- 🩻 **Silent brain infarcts were common.** In a review pooling many studies, small brain infarcts without symptoms were found on MRI in 8–28% of older adults, and they raised the risk of stroke and dementia (**Lancet Neurology**, 2007).
- 🛋️ **Moving less is close to instinct.** Even the Hadza hunter-gatherers spent about 10 hours a day in resting postures, but many of those postures, like squatting, kept muscles working (**PNAS**, 2020).

### 💬 "A 50-year-old brain looks like an 80-year-old's on MRI?"

When I first heard that a 50-year-old's brain can look like an 80-year-old's on MRI, honestly, a chill ran down my spine. The brain records the way you've lived, and that record isn't made all at once. That fact is the real reason I lace up my running shoes every morning.

I started running casually in my 20s. Looking back, that choice cut a path in my body. Once the habit settled in, my body actually feels stiff and something feels missing on days I skip. After work it's hard to get myself moving, so I switched to a short run of about **2 km, 12 minutes** before work. The sense of achievement from overcoming the urge not to do it changed the temperature at which my day starts.

This post originally summarized that experience along with brain plasticity, "silent strokes" and the compound effect of habits. This time, instead of the YouTube video and general links I'd cited, I looked up and checked the original papers.

## Deep Dive: How does an exercise habit build up in the brain?

### 1. Does the brain really harden with age?

The original post said "brain plasticity declines with age." That's half right. But even with age, the brain responded to practice.

A team at University Medical Center Hamburg-Eppendorf in Germany had people with an average age of 60 practice three-ball juggling for three months (Boyke et al., **Journal of Neuroscience**, 2008).

- Those who practiced gained **gray matter** in a brain area involved in seeing moving objects, and changes appeared in other areas including the hippocampus.
- The change was smaller than in the same experiment with younger people, and juggling skill improved less too.

In short, with age it takes more repetition to get the same change, but change still happens. The original post's statement that "change is still possible in adulthood but needs more repetition" fits this result well.

### 2. Can exercise started late grow the brain?

A University of Illinois team divided 59 inactive adults aged 60–79 into two groups for six months. One group did **aerobic exercise** like brisk walking for an hour three times a week; the other did stretching and toning (Colcombe et al., **Journal of Gerontology: Medical Sciences**, 2006).

- The aerobic group gained **gray matter** volume in frontal and temporal areas, and **white matter** volume connecting the left and right brain.
- The stretching group showed no such changes.

In another trial by the same team, older adults who did walking exercise for a year saw their **hippocampus** grow by about 2%. That study is covered in detail in [How Often Should You Do 1-Minute Workouts?](/en/blog/one-minute-high-intensity-exercise-hiie-brain-health-science/). Even if you didn't start in your 20s, the exercise you start now leaves its mark on the brain.

### 3. Are "silent strokes" really common?

What the original post called a silent stroke is medically called a **silent brain infarct**. It's a state where a small blood vessel in the brain is blocked and some brain tissue is damaged, but with no clear symptoms, so it's only found with MRI.

According to a review by a Dutch team pooling many studies, silent brain infarcts were found in **8–28%** of the general older population, more often with age. People with silent brain infarcts later had higher risks of **stroke** and **dementia** (Vermeer et al., **Lancet Neurology**, 2007). The most important risk factor was **high blood pressure**.

But I couldn't confirm the source of the original post's line that "a 50-year-old's MRI looks like a 70- or 80-year-old's." It's true that silent brain infarcts are common and affected by lifestyle and vascular health, but I removed the specific age comparison.

### 4. Is disliking exercise really instinct?

The original post said "even hunter-gatherers sat for 10 hours a day." Checking the original study, that was right, and there was one more point.

A University of Southern California team put activity trackers on the **Hadza**, hunter-gatherers in Tanzania (Raichlen et al., **PNAS**, 2020).

- The Hadza also spent about **10 hours** a day in resting postures, similar to modern people.
- But much of that time was spent **squatting or kneeling** rather than sitting in chairs, and in these postures the leg muscles kept working a little.
- They also spent several hours a day walking and working at moderate or higher intensity.

Wanting to rest is instinct, but our ancestors used their muscles a little even while resting, and moved quite a lot each day. That makes the original post's conclusion, "design your environment and routine instead of relying on willpower," even more important.

### 5. Is there evidence for the compound effect of habits?

"Compound interest" is a metaphor, but research confirms that habits get easier over time. In a UCL study following 96 people for 12 weeks, a new action done every day in the same situation took a **median of 66 days** to become automatic (Lally et al., **European Journal of Social Psychology**, 2010). The original post's note that it's "much longer than the commonly cited 21 days" is correct. My experience of attaching a new habit while tracking my time is covered in [Does Tracking Your Time Change Habits?](/en/blog/what-changed-when-i-started-observing-my-day-quantum-entanglement/).

### 6. Does half of mild cognitive impairment turn into dementia?

The original post said "about half of people with mild cognitive impairment progress to dementia." A meta-analysis of 41 studies found that in the community, the rate at which people with mild cognitive impairment progressed to dementia was **around 5% a year**, and many didn't progress even over long follow-up (Mitchell & Shiri-Feshki, **Acta Psychiatrica Scandinavica**, 2009). The rate was higher among patients seen at clinics. So the "half" figure is corrected.

And a substantial share of dementia risk was linked to changeable factors. The Lancet Commission on dementia estimated that 12 modifiable factors, including high blood pressure, physical inactivity, smoking, heavy drinking and diabetes, are linked to about **40%** of all dementia (Livingston et al., **Lancet**, 2020).

**Step 1. Start by checking your blood pressure**
The biggest risk factor for silent brain infarcts was high blood pressure. Check your blood pressure before you start exercising, and talk with a doctor if it's high. Exercise also helps manage blood pressure.

**Step 2. Make one short, set workout, like 2 km in the morning**
Like my 2 km, 12-minute run before work, repeat one short, set workout at the same time each day. The first few weeks take conscious effort, but after about 66 days, skipping it feels stranger than doing it. If you're older, starting with brisk walking is enough.

**Step 3. Break up your sitting time**
If you spend most of the day sitting, get up every 30 minutes to walk or squat for 2–3 minutes. Our ancestors used their muscles a little even while resting.

## Conclusion

The brain records your life. The step you took today and the step you skipped today both stay in the brain. Fortunately, that record could be rewritten even with age. People in their 60s and 70s gained brain volume after six months of exercise, and people around 60 gained gray matter with practice. Just as the running I started in my 20s did for me, one short workout you start today builds up in your brain five and ten years from now. Check your blood pressure and start with 2 km in the morning.

### 🙋 Frequently Asked Questions

#### I work full time and truly have no time to exercise.

My after-work exercise attempts kept failing too. Switching to a 12-minute run before work is what has lasted until now. Research also suggests that after a day of mental work, evenings tip toward easy choices. That's covered in [Does Mental Fatigue Make You Give Up?](/en/blog/why-we-give-up-diets-and-tasks-the-brain-uses-20-percent-of-energy/).

#### Can exercise prevent dementia?

Exercise can't be said to definitely prevent dementia, but physical inactivity is one of the modifiable risk factors named by the Lancet Commission on dementia. Along with exercise, it's good to take care of other factors too, like blood pressure, quitting smoking, moderate drinking and hearing.
