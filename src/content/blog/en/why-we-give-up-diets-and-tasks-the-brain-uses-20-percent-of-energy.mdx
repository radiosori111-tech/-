## 📌 Key Takeaways

> **The short answer:** After more than six hours of hard mental work, **glutamate** built up in the front of the brain, and people chose **immediate rewards** over larger ones that required waiting. **Mental fatigue** like this, more than willpower, may also play a part in giving up on diets and put-off tasks in the evening. Making hard decisions in the morning and setting evening choices in advance is the approach that fits the research.

- 🧠 **The brain is 2% of body weight but uses about 20% of the body's energy at rest.** This figure is correct (**PNAS**, 2002).
- 🔋 **But focusing barely increases the brain's energy use.** The extra energy used during a hard task was **less than 5%** of what the brain uses at rest (**Annual Review of Neuroscience**, 2006). "Giving up because the fuel ran out" doesn't hold.
- 🌙 **A full day of mental work changed choices.** People who did hard tasks for over six hours had more glutamate in the **lateral prefrontal cortex** and chose more low-effort, immediate rewards (**Current Biology**, 2022).
- 😴 **Short sleep pulled people toward high-calorie food.** After a night without sleep, responses in brain regions that help weigh choices fell, **amygdala** responses rose, and people wanted more high-calorie food (**Nature Communications**, 2013).

### 💬 "Why do I always fall apart in the evening?"

You're on a diet but end up ordering a late-night snack again, and the task you put off until tomorrow is still sitting there today. This post began as notes on *I Read Brain Science Because I Want to Live as Myself*, summarizing the idea that "it's not weak willpower but a brain trying to save energy."

Looking up the original papers this time, the direction was right but the reason was a bit different. ## Deep Dive: How does mental fatigue change our choices?

### 1. Is it true that the brain uses 20% of the body's energy?

Yes. The brain is about 2% of body weight but uses about 20% of the energy the body spends at rest (Raichle & Gusnard, **PNAS**, 2002). Neurons constantly exchange signals, and keeping the ion balance inside and outside cells ready for those signals takes continuous work.

### 2. Does heavy thinking drain the brain's fuel?

Here comes the unexpected part. Most of the brain's energy goes to a baseline cost it pays even at rest. The extra energy used during a hard task was **less than 5%** of that baseline (Raichle & Mintun, **Annual Review of Neuroscience**, 2006).

So the explanation that "hard decisions use up brain fuel and break your willpower" doesn't hold. The once-famous finding that "eating glucose restores willpower" was shaken by later experiments, and when 23 labs replicated the idea together, the effect that "willpower shrinks with use" barely appeared (Hagger et al., **Perspectives on Psychological Science**, 2016). This is covered in detail in [Why Resolutions Last Three Days, and It Isn't Willpower](/en/blog/why-diets-and-savings-plans-last-three-days-identity-based-motivation/).

### 3. Then why does mental effort feel so unappealing?

Even with enough fuel, the brain **treats mental effort itself as a cost**. A Princeton team let people freely choose between two decks of cards. In one, the rules switched often and demanded more thinking; the other demanded less. The rewards were the same, yet most people chose the **less demanding deck** (Kool et al., **Journal of Experimental Psychology: General**, 2010).

The earlier version's line that "the brain weighs effort against reward" fits this result. But that calculation is less about saving calories and more about putting a "cost" on mental effort.

### 4. What happens in the brain after focusing all day?

A team at the Paris Brain Institute had 40 people spend over six hours on tasks requiring memory and switching. One group did a hard version, the other an easy version (Wiehler et al., **Current Biology**, 2022).

- The hard-task group had more **glutamate** built up in the **lateral prefrontal cortex**, measured with magnetic resonance spectroscopy (MRS).
- By the end of the day, this group chose "less now" over "more later," and lower-effort options, more often. Their pupil responses also shrank, a sign of less effort.
- In the easy-task group, these changes were not clear.

The researchers interpreted this as glutamate buildup raising the cost of continuing to use this region, so the brain leans toward easier choices that need less control. It overlaps with ordering a late-night snack and putting off a task yet again. But with only 40 participants, it didn't directly test everyday dieting.

### 5. Why do late-night snacks call when you're short on sleep?

A UC Berkeley team had 23 people view food pictures and rate how much they wanted each, once after a good night's sleep and once after a sleepless night, while their brains were scanned (Greer et al., **Nature Communications**, 2013).

- After a sleepless night, responses fell in cortical regions involved in weighing the value of food, such as the **anterior cingulate cortex** and **insula**.
- Instead, responses rose in the **amygdala**, which is involved in emotional reactions, and people wanted more **high-calorie food**.

When short sleep piles on top of the day's mental fatigue, evening choices tip even more easily. The earlier version's advice to keep "enough sleep and regular meals" matched the research.

### 6. Do counting down five seconds and smiling actually work?

The first version introduced two more methods, and checking the research showed parts to correct.

- **The 5-second rule**: I wrote that "the nucleus accumbens doesn't switch on from thinking alone and only releases dopamine once the body moves," but I couldn't find research stating it this way. The 5-second rule comes from a self-help book, and I couldn't find an experiment testing it directly. Still, making the first action very small works on the same principle as the "if-then" plan below, so it's worth trying.
- **Smiling**: In a large experiment with 3,878 people in 19 countries, deliberately making a smiling face raised happiness **slightly** (Coles et al., **Nature Human Behaviour**, 2022). The effect was small, and I found no basis for the "hold it at least 15 seconds" rule.

## Solution: 3 Steps to Fewer Evening Give-Ups

**Step 1. Make hard decisions in the morning**
Decisions that take thinking, like planning meals or choosing the first step of a put-off task, are best made in the morning before mental fatigue builds. In the evening, you just follow what you decided.

**Step 2. Set evening choices in advance**
Write them in **if-then** form: "If I'm hungry at 9 p.m., I'll eat the boiled egg in the fridge," "When I get home, I'll open my laptop and work for just 5 minutes." In a meta-analysis of 94 studies, plans like these clearly helped people reach their goals (Gollwitzer & Sheeran, **Advances in Experimental Social Psychology**, 2006). Try the routine below as is.

**Step 3. Protect your sleep first**
On short-sleep days, mental fatigue combines with sleepiness to make high-calorie food more tempting. When planning your day, set your bedtime first. ## Conclusion

There was no need to blame yourself for weak willpower when you fall apart every evening. But "the brain tricks you to save calories" wasn't accurate either. Mental work all day builds fatigue in the brain, and that fatigue tips evening choices toward the easy option. So instead of fighting it, change the order. Hard decisions in the morning, evening choices in advance, and sleep protected first.

### 🙋 Frequently Asked Questions

#### Does eating something sweet relieve mental fatigue?

No. The brain's energy use doesn't rise much with focus, and the finding that "glucose restores willpower" didn't replicate. When you feel mentally tired, getting up for a short break or walk beats sweets. How blood sugar affects the brain is covered in [How to Use a Glucose Meter at Home](/en/blog/glucose-meter-at-home-brain-health-signals-from-one-drop-of-blood/).

#### What if my job requires thinking hard all day?

Try adjusting the order of your work. Put the hardest work in the morning and routine tasks in the afternoon, and cut down on new decisions in the evening. In the study, the easy-task group's choices didn't change much even after working the same hours. If you've done hard work for a long time, follow your pre-set plan at least that evening.
