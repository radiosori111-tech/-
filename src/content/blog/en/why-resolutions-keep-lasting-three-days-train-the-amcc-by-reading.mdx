## 📌 Key takeaways

> **The short answer:** **Reading focus** grows less from never letting your mind wander and more from **practicing noticing it and returning to the page**, because that's when attention-related brain networks get to work. Turn off notifications, read informational books on paper, and start with 10 minutes a day.

- 🔁 **Your brain works when you come back.** When meditators **noticed** their minds had wandered, a "salience network" including the area around the aMCC lit up, and when they **brought their focus back**, the prefrontal cortex became active (**NeuroImage**, 2012).
- 📱 **Just a notification can break focus.** Merely **receiving** a phone notification, without looking at the phone, sharply increased errors on an attention task (**Journal of Experimental Psychology: Human Perception and Performance**, 2015).
- 📖 **Paper won for informational text.** In a meta-analysis of 54 studies and more than 170,000 people, expository texts were understood better on paper (**Educational Research Review**, 2018).
- 🧓 **A reading habit protects the brain.** Older adults who read and wrote often throughout life had slower cognitive decline regardless of how much brain pathology they had (**Neurology**, 2013).

### 💬 "I open a book, read three lines, and grab my phone"

Reading is one of the habits most likely to end in a three-day resolution. When I first wrote this post, I presented reading as a way to build the **aMCC** and wrote that "the aMCC grows the more you use it, like a muscle, and shrinks when you don't," and that "how long you keep reading matters more than whether it's paper or e-book."

I still believe that the core is repeatedly returning to the book after your mind wanders. Looking up the original papers this time, I found research that backs that idea, along with parts that needed correcting.

## Deep Dive: what happens in the brain when you read?

### 1. What do the frontal lobe and the aMCC do?

The **frontal lobe** is like the brain's control tower, planning, judging, and holding back impulses. The **aMCC** lies deep inside it, in the **front-middle part** of the cingulate cortex. In the first version of this post I called the aMCC the "anterior cingulate cortex," but that name refers to a region further forward (the ACC), so I corrected it to "anterior midcingulate cortex."

The aMCC is thought to be a hub that decides how much energy to spend on hard tasks (Touroutoglou et al., **Cortex**, 2020), and there's an observation that stimulating it electrically produced a feeling of "I'll push through even if it's hard" (Parvizi et al., **Neuron**, 2013). But "it grows with use like a muscle" is still a hypothesis not confirmed in human experiments, which I corrected in detail in [resolutions and the aMCC](/en/blog/three-day-resolutions-use-bdnf-and-the-amcc-before-willpower/).

### 2. Is coming back from mind-wandering really training?

The heart of the first version was that "the process of drifting off and coming back to the book is itself the training." There's a study that broke this process down very finely.

A team at Emory University asked 14 experienced meditators to focus on their breath and press a button whenever they noticed their minds had wandered, then looked at brain activity before and after with fMRI (Hasenkamp et al., **NeuroImage**, 2012).

- During mind-wandering, the **default mode network**, which switches on when we zone out, was active.
- At the **moment of noticing**, the **salience network**, including the area around the aMCC and the insula, switched on.
- When they **shifted attention back** to the breath, the **dorsolateral prefrontal cortex** became active.

In other words, mind-wandering itself wasn't a failure; each time they noticed it and came back, attention-related brain regions went to work in turn. You can use the same principle in reading, but keep in mind that this was a meditation study.

### 3. Phones and paper books: what decides focus?

**Phone notifications** are a bigger distraction than you might think. A team at Florida State University secretly sent call or text notifications to college students doing an attention task (Stothart et al., **Journal of Experimental Psychology: Human Perception and Performance**, 2015). Even though they only **received** the notifications without picking up their phones, their errors rose sharply, to a level similar to those who actually took a call or sent a text.

On **paper versus e-books**, I had to correct the first version. I first wrote that "how long you keep reading matters more than paper versus e-book," but in a meta-analysis of 54 studies since 2000 with more than 170,000 people, **informational texts such as expository writing** were **understood better on paper** than on screens. The gap was larger under time limits and wasn't clear for narrative texts such as novels (Delgado et al., **Educational Research Review**, 2018).

### 4. Does a reading habit leave a lasting mark on the brain?

A team at Rush University surveyed 294 older adults every year until death about cognitive activities such as reading and writing, then examined their brains after death (Wilson et al., **Neurology**, 2013). Those who did these activities often throughout life had **slower cognitive decline**, regardless of how much pathology had built up in their brains. As an observational study, it can't prove that reading caused this, but it suggests a steady reading habit may protect the brain's reserve.

## The fix: three steps to build reading focus

**Step 1. Notifications off, start with 10 minutes**
Switch your phone to silent and put it in another room or a bag. Ten minutes a day is plenty to start. Try the timer below right away. When you notice your mind has wandered while reading, press the button and return to the book.

<FocusTimer client:visible lang="en" />

**Step 2. Informational books on paper, same time and place**
For texts you need to understand, such as books for study or work, read on paper when you can. Sitting in the same spot at the same time each day turns the situation itself into a habit cue. Habits took a median of 66 days to settle in (Lally et al., **European Journal of Social Psychology**, 2010).

**Step 3. When you finish, one line in your own words**
Rather than copying out what you read, try **summarizing it in a line or two in your own words**. The "generation effect," in which information you produce yourself is remembered better than information you simply read, was confirmed in a meta-analysis of 86 studies (Bertsch et al., **Memory & Cognition**, 2007).

## Conclusion

Your mind wandering while you read isn't a sign of weak will; it happens to everyone. What matters is **noticing that moment and coming back**, and research shows attention-related brain regions go to work each time you do. Today, turn off notifications and read for just 10 minutes. Count how many times you came back from mind-wandering; that number is how many times you practiced.

### 🙋 Frequently asked questions

#### Do audiobooks help with reading focus?

Many studies find little difference in comprehension between listening and reading for stories, but for difficult informational texts it helps to read with your eyes so you can go back and check. Try using audiobooks for walks or commutes and reading books that need focus on paper. To keep it going, use the "if X, then Y" plans from [three ways to achieve your goals](/en/blog/three-ways-to-achieve-your-goals/).

#### I finish books but can't remember them. What can I do?

Memories get stronger when you recall them. Write a one-line summary right after reading, then try recalling it a few days later without looking at the book. I covered why memories change and how to record them in [memory is less true, so write it down](/en/blog/memory-is-less-true-so-write-it-down/).
