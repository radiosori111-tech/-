## 📌 Key Takeaways

> **The short answer:** Focusing on a muscle while training can make that muscle grow more. People who focused on the muscle's contraction for 8 weeks grew their **arm muscles nearly twice as much**. But when chasing **performance**, like lifting heavy or jumping high, focusing outside the body (the floor, a target) worked better than focusing on the muscle. Switch where you focus depending on your goal.

- 💪 **Focusing on the muscle grew the arms more.** Thirty untrained men did the same exercises for 8 weeks, one group focusing on "squeeze the muscle" and the other on "lift the weight." The muscle-focus group's arm muscles grew about 12%, nearly twice the other group's (about 7%) (**European Journal of Sport Science**, 2018).
- 🏋️ **The focus effect disappeared at heavy loads.** Focusing on the chest during the bench press raised chest muscle activity up to 60% of max, but made no difference at 80% (**European Journal of Applied Physiology**, 2016).
- 🎯 **Focusing outside was better for performance.** A review of many studies found that putting attention outside the body (a target, the floor, a ball) improved performance like jumping, throwing and balance (**International Review of Sport and Exercise Psychology**, 2013).
- 🏛️ **Pinning words to places more than doubled memory.** Six weeks of "memory palace" training, placing words along a familiar route, raised words remembered from an average of 26 to 62 (**Neuron**, 2017).

### 💬 "Chasing the numbers, why did I get hurt somewhere unexpected?"

When I started at the gym, my eyes were fixed only on the numbers on the plates and my reflection in the mirror. The day I set a goal of a **60 kg hip thrust**, all I could think was "push the bar up." In the end my knees did the work instead of my glutes, and in my third month I went to the hospital with pelvic pain.

After that I changed my approach. Before each set, I lightly tapped the target area with my hands to wake up the sensation, and focused on the feeling of the muscle stretching and contracting rather than the weight. For squats, instead of "lift the bar," I thought "push the floor away with my feet." My glute engagement in hip thrusts changed noticeably, and the knee pain went away. When studying, reading concepts aloud while writing them by hand also helped me remember better the next day.

This post originally explained this experience through two spatial maps in the brain: an "allocentric" and an "egocentric" frame of reference. Looking up the original research this time, my experience was explained more accurately by sports science research on **attentional focus**, and a few points needed correcting in the opposite direction.

## Deep Dive: Does where you focus change your body and memory?

### 1. Does focusing on the muscle really make it grow more?

It could. Brad Schoenfeld's team at Lehman College in the US had 30 untrained men do arm (biceps curl) and leg (leg extension) exercises three times a week for 8 weeks (Schoenfeld et al., **European Journal of Sport Science**, 2018).

- One group used an **internal focus** on the working muscle, like "squeeze the muscle"; the other used an **external focus** outside the body, like "lift the weight."
- Arm muscles grew about **12.4%** in the internal-focus group and about **6.9%** in the external-focus group, nearly a twofold difference.
- Leg muscles grew similarly in both groups.

The original post's **mind-muscle connection**, deliberately focusing on the working muscle, is supported as a way to help build muscle. But keep in mind the small number of participants, and that there was no difference in the legs.

### 2. Does focusing on the muscle work with heavy weights too?

It didn't. A University of Valencia team in Spain measured muscle activity while people did the bench press focusing on either the chest or the back of the arm (Calatayud et al., **European Journal of Applied Physiology**, 2016).

- At **20–60%** of max, activity in the focused muscle increased.
- But at **80%** of max, focusing made no difference.

The interpretation is that with heavy weights the body has to recruit every muscle it can, leaving no room to channel signals to one muscle. My benefit from lowering the weight and focusing on sensation fits this result.

### 3. What kind of focus is "pushing the floor away"?

The original post called thinking "push the earth away with my feet" instead of "lift the bar" an "egocentric" focus. But in sports science, this actually counts as an **external focus**, putting attention outside the body (on the floor).

According to a review by Professor Gabriele Wulf of the University of Nevada, Las Vegas, in movements aimed at **performance or accuracy**, like jumping, throwing, golf and balance, putting attention on an **external target** rather than your own body improved performance and made movement more efficient (Wulf, **International Review of Sport and Exercise Psychology**, 2013). So the "push the floor away" cue steadying my squat was likely thanks to an external focus.

In short, the research supports focusing on **the muscle (internal focus) when building muscle**, and on **something outside (external focus) when lifting heavy or chasing performance**. I couldn't find research directly confirming the original claim that "tapping the muscle before a set stamps an address in the somatosensory cortex," but it may help you become aware of which muscle to focus on.

### 4. If you walk while memorizing, do the hippocampus's grid cells tie memories together?

The original post said "walking around a room repeating concepts makes the hippocampus's grid cells store spatial coordinates and memories together." Two corrections.

- **Grid cells** were found not in the hippocampus but in the neighboring **entorhinal cortex**. A Norwegian team found cells that fire in a grid-like pattern across the floor as rats move through space (Hafting et al., **Nature**, 2005), a discovery that won the 2014 Nobel Prize.
- I couldn't find an experiment showing that walking while memorizing makes memories last longer through grid cells.

Instead, the effect of memory methods that use "space" is clearly confirmed. A Max Planck Institute team in Germany compared memory champions with ordinary people, then trained ordinary people for six weeks in the **memory palace** method (placing items to remember one by one in places along a familiar route or around your home). Words remembered out of 72 rose from an average of **26 to 62**, and the effect remained four months later (Dresler et al., **Neuron**, 2017). People didn't need to actually walk; mentally following the route was enough.

### 5. Do you really learn better by explaining to someone else?

The original post said "switching your position from student to teacher and explaining reconstructs knowledge." In an experiment by a UC Santa Barbara team, students who **prepared to teach or actually explained** what they'd learned to someone else did better on a test a week later than students who simply restudied (Fiorella & Mayer, **Contemporary Educational Psychology**, 2013). The effect lasted longer for students who actually explained. Why reading aloud helps memory is covered in the production effect section of [Self-Talk Effects](/en/blog/one-word-rewires-the-brain-neuroplasticity-and-speech-habits/).

## Solution: 3 Steps to Choosing Where to Focus for Your Goal

First, pick what you're trying to do right now. You'll see the matching focus method and the evidence behind it.

**Step 1. To build muscle, go light and focus on the muscle**
Lower the weight to 60% of max or less, and repeat slowly while focusing on the feeling of the working muscle stretching and contracting. Touching that muscle with your hand before a set can help you become aware of where to focus.

**Step 2. To lift heavy, focus outside**
When going for heavy weights or records, focus on a target outside your body, like "push the floor away" or "send the bar to the ceiling." That way you use every muscle you can, efficiently.

**Step 3. For studying, pin it to places and explain it to someone**
If you have a lot to memorize, try the memory palace, placing items one by one in familiar spots. And when you finish studying, say what you learned aloud as if explaining it to someone.

## Conclusion

Looking back, the day I hurt my pelvis at the gym was actually a blessing. Without that experience, I'd have kept chasing numbers and using the wrong muscles. The answer research gave wasn't "always focus on the muscle." Focus on the muscle when building it, on something outside when lifting heavy, and on places when memorizing. Choosing where to focus for your goal was what changed both body and memory.

### 🙋 Frequently Asked Questions

#### Is it bad to work out while watching the mirror?

A mirror helps early on while you learn form. But relying only on your reflection makes it easy to miss the feeling in your muscles. Once your form is fairly solid, add sets where you focus on the muscle's feeling without a mirror. Why warming up before exercise matters is covered in [The 5-Minute Warm-Up Before and After Running](/en/blog/skip-the-5-minute-warm-up-and-cool-down-and-you-halve-runnings-brain-benefits/).

#### Does walking around while memorizing work?

I couldn't find an experiment showing that walking itself boosts memory through grid cells. What's been confirmed is the memory palace, placing items one by one in familiar spots. It worked just by following the route in your mind. Still, short walks between study sessions can help restore mood and focus.
