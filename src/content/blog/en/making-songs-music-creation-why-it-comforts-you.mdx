## 📌 Key takeaways

> **The short answer:** Making something yourself is recovery in three layers: **it takes your mind off work, gives you the feeling that "I did this," and rewards you again through the result.** With music, there is one more layer: dopamine from the reward circuit in the moment you get chills.

- 📓 **The day after creating:** In diaries kept by 658 people for 13 days, people felt more energized and felt they were doing well the day after they created more than usual. The reverse did not hold: feeling good did not lead to more creating the next day (Conner et al., Journal of Positive Psychology, 2018).
- 🎨 **Whatever your skill:** About 75% of people who spent 45 minutes freely making something had lower levels of the stress hormone cortisol, and the results were similar with or without art experience (Kaimal et al., Art Therapy, 2016).
- 🧠 **Chills and dopamine:** Dopamine was released in different parts of the brain while people anticipated the peak of music they loved and at the peak itself (Salimpoor et al., Nature Neuroscience, 2011).
- 🛠 **Why what you make seems better:** People valued things they assembled themselves more highly than the same things made by someone else (Norton et al., Journal of Consumer Psychology, 2012).
- ⚠️ **To be honest:** Almost no research has looked specifically at creating with AI tools. The evidence in this post comes from studies of activities people do by hand.

### 💬 The day of an unfair complaint, I made a song

One evening not long ago, work was unusually hard. After an unfair customer complaint on top of it, my spirits sank for a long while. Thinking that music might at least change my mood, I put a song I had written the lyrics for into Suno AI.

I chose the style I wanted to hear. **Indie pop, a male voice, a first verse that starts in a relaxed falsetto, and a second verse that turns the flow around.** I filled the lyrics with the words I most wanted to hear that day.

> And you?<br />Were you happy to have met me?
>
> [trembling breath]<br />Even if I gave you everything I am<br />it would not be too much
>
> I was so, so happy

Making something was a joy in itself. And when the finished song fit my style exactly, **I no longer thought about how tired work had made me, and I felt comforted.**

Why was that? Why did the fatigue fade, and why did a song I made, not a song by someone else, bring comfort? When I followed the research, I found a reason for each thing I had done that day.

Making songs like this has become my recovery after work. The song in this post is still in progress, and an earlier song of mine, 「간지러운 바람」 ("Ticklish Breeze"), comes out on music streaming sites on November 6 under the name month12000.

## 🔬 Deep Dive: How does a brain that makes things recover?

### Does your mood really change the day after creating?

A team at the University of Otago in New Zealand asked 658 university students to keep a diary every day for 13 days, noting how much creative activity they did that day and how they felt. Creativity here did not mean grand art. The common examples the team named were everyday activities such as **writing songs**, writing poems or short stories, knitting, trying new recipes, painting and playing music.

The result ran in one direction. On the **day after** they created more than usual, people were more energized and enthusiastic, and felt they were doing well. The opposite direction, more creating the day after a good mood, did not appear (Conner, DeYoung & Silvia, Journal of Positive Psychology, 2018). It suggests that people probably felt better because they created, not that they created because they felt good. The study did not sort results by type of creative activity, though, so it does not show an effect of songwriting alone.

### Does it work if you have no skill?

A team at Drexel University in the US had 39 adults aged 18 to 59 freely make something with markers, clay and collage materials for 45 minutes. There was no set task and no grading. When the team measured the stress hormone cortisol in saliva before and after, **cortisol fell in about 75% of participants** and rose in the other 25% or so. There was no difference between people with art experience and people with little (Kaimal, Ray & Muniz, Art Therapy, 2016).

**Still, this was a small study of 39 people with no comparison group.** It also used art materials, not music. Even so, it offers a hint that you can let go of the idea that it only works if you make something good.

### Why did the fatigue of work fade while I was making it?

Researchers who study recovery after work divide the experience of recovering from work into four types (Sonnentag & Fritz, Journal of Occupational Health Psychology, 2007).

| Recovery experience | Meaning | When making a song |
|---|---|---|
| **Psychological detachment** | Getting away from thoughts about work | While you are absorbed in lyrics and structure, the complaint gets pushed out of your head. |
| Relaxation | Lowering tension in body and mind | Time to rest while listening to music you love |
| **Mastery** | The experience of learning and achieving something new | The sense of achievement from building the structure you wanted |
| **Control** | The experience of deciding and doing things your way | You decide the genre, the voice and the flow of both verses. |

An unfair complaint is something you cannot control. Time spent making a song, by contrast, was **time I decided on** from beginning to end. It is a way of regaining elsewhere the sense of control you lost at work.

Other research has looked at creative hobbies and work. In a survey of 341 employees, those who did creative activities more often outside work solved problems more creatively at work and helped colleagues more. A second survey of 92 Air Force officers, rated by coworkers and subordinates, found something similar (Eschleman et al., Journal of Occupational and Organizational Psychology, 2014). **As these were surveys, though, they did not establish that creating is what improved people's work.**

### What happens in the brain in the moment of creating?

A team from the US National Institutes of Health (NIH) and Johns Hopkins University scanned the brains of professional jazz pianists while they improvised inside an MRI. Compared with playing a memorized piece, improvising brought **less activity in the lateral prefrontal cortex, which monitors and controls the self**, and more activity in the medial prefrontal cortex, which is linked to self-expression (Limb & Braun, PLoS ONE, 2008).

The team read this as a state in which the voice checking "Am I doing well?" goes quiet for a while, and what comes from within simply comes out. It lets us guess why time spent making what you like feels like rest after a day of being judged by others. Still, this study covered only 6 professional musicians, so we cannot say it applies to all creative work.

### Why does a song I made sound better to me?

A research team including Harvard Business School and Duke University had people either assemble an IKEA box themselves or look at the same box already assembled, then asked how much they would pay. **People who assembled it themselves were willing to pay more for the same box.** People who folded origami themselves put a value on their work similar to that of experts' work, even though others valued the same pieces at about a fifth of what the makers did. But people who were stopped with two assembly steps left bid less than half of what those who finished did, and the effect also disappeared when people built something and then took it apart themselves (Norton, Mochon & Ariely, Journal of Consumer Psychology, 2012). The team called this the "IKEA effect."

What has been finished by your own hands feels more precious. If it also holds the words you wanted to hear and sounds to your taste, that song becomes the one fitted to you more closely than any other song in the world.

### What is the brain doing when you get chills?

A team at McGill University in Canada played participants music that gave them chills and took scans (PET) that measure whether dopamine is actually released. In the moment of chills, the **nucleus accumbens** deep in the brain was more active, and earlier, during **about 15 seconds of anticipating the peak, the caudate** was more active. Dopamine was released in the striatum at both moments (Salimpoor et al., Nature Neuroscience, 2011). **The PET study had 8 participants.**

Later work also tested whether dopamine really is the cause. A Spanish team gave 27 people a drug that boosts dopamine, a drug that blocks it and a placebo in turn. Music was more enjoyable when dopamine was boosted and less enjoyable when it was blocked (Ferreri et al., PNAS, 2019). These drugs were used only in an experiment to test the principle. They are not something to take to enjoy music more.

Looking back, placing **[trembling breath]** right before "I was so, so happy" was a device for creating this "anticipation." In the research the anticipation window was about 15 seconds before the peak, so one breath is not all of it. Still, the moment the sound empties and a breath is drawn can be seen as a signal to the listener that "here it comes," raising that anticipation right up to the peak.

### Why is it enjoyable when the second verse turns the flow around?

A team from the Max Planck Institute in Germany and the University of Bergen in Norway took about 80,000 chords from 745 US Billboard hits, calculated how hard each chord was to predict and how surprising it was, and played them to people. When people **felt sure of the next chord, they enjoyed a surprising one**, and when they **had no idea where it would go, they enjoyed an expected one**. This enjoyment was reflected in activity in the amygdala, hippocampus and auditory cortex (Cheung et al., Current Biology, 2019).

When the first verse moves along familiarly in a relaxed falsetto, the listener grows sure that "this song is going to go like this." If the second verse turns the flow around once that confidence has formed, the change can be received with more enjoyment. **This study analyzed chords, so we cannot say it applies directly to the structure of a whole song**, but the principle of balance between prediction and surprise is the same.

### Is doing it different from only listening?

A team at the University of Oxford compared people who made music themselves, by singing, dancing or drumming, with people who only listened. Those who made music themselves **tolerated pain longer afterwards and were in a better mood.** No such change appeared in those who only listened (Dunbar et al., Evolutionary Psychology, 2012). The team read this as a sign that the body had released endorphins.

| What I did that day | What research showed | Strength of evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Making a song | People were more energized the day after creating more. | **658 people, 13-day diary study** |
| Making something whatever my skill | Cortisol fell in about 75% after 45 minutes of making. | 39 people, no comparison group, art materials |
| Forgetting about work | Psychological detachment, mastery and control are core recovery experiences. | Questionnaire development study, 930 people |
| Finishing in my own style | People valued what they completed themselves more highly. | 4 experiments. Effect only when completed |

## ⚠️ Setting the record straight: "brainwave music" and the "Mozart effect"

**"Listening to Mozart makes you smarter":** This began with a short report in Nature in 1993, but an analysis pooling about 40 studies and more than 3,000 participants found no evidence that Mozart's music specifically improves ability (Pietschnig et al., Intelligence, 2010).

**"Binaural beats tune your brainwaves":** Of 14 studies that directly measured brainwave changes, 5 had results in line with the claim, 8 had results against it and 1 was mixed (Ingendoh et al., PLOS ONE, 2023).

**"Creating with AI works just as well":** Almost no research has looked at this question on its own yet. What was tied to the effects in the studies above, however, was **absorption, the experience of deciding for yourself and the satisfaction of finishing**, and writing your own lyrics and choosing your own structure involve all of these.

## ✅ The fix: Three small steps for making something on a tiring day

### Step 1: Start small, at a size you can finish

The IKEA effect appeared only when people finished. Aiming for a masterpiece from the start can leave you unable to finish and even more worn out.

- Today, make only **four lines of lyrics** or one short chorus.
- With a tool like Suno AI, you can get to a finished song without knowing an instrument. You decide the lyrics and the style.
- **Set up your environment**: create a "lyrics note" in your phone's notes app, and jot down each line as it comes to you.

### Step 2: In words you want to hear, to your own taste

The sense of control and self-expression were at the heart of recovery. Decide by your own standards, not someone else's.

- Lyrics: write down **what you most wanted to hear from someone today**.
- Style: choose the genre, voice and mood you like.
- Set judgment aside for a while. Whether you finished comes before whether it is good.

### Step 3: Design the "anticipation" and the "turn"

Chills grew at the peak after anticipation, and enjoyment grew with the surprising change after confidence.

- Place **a breath or a short silence** before your most important line.
- Keep the first verse familiar and simple, then change the flow in the second verse or the last chorus. Changing just one of voice, instruments or mood is enough.
- Note where you get chills as you listen to your finished song. It becomes your own formula for the next one.

| Step | Key action | Evidence | Environmental setup |
|---|---|---|---|
| Step 1 Start small | Four lines of lyrics, one chorus | Value grew when people finished (Norton 2012). | Lyrics note on your phone |
| Step 2 Your own taste | Words you want to hear, a style you like | Control and detachment are core recovery experiences (Sonnentag 2007). | Finish without judging |
| Step 3 Anticipation and the turn | Silence before the peak, a change of flow in verse two | Dopamine of anticipation, surprising change after confidence (Salimpoor 2011, Cheung 2019) | Note where the chills come |

### 🎧 On days with no time to make anything, how to listen

- **When reading and memorizing:** Reading comprehension scores while listening to songs with lyrics were much lower than in quiet (Perham & Currie, 2014). In the graph from this 30-person experiment, the proportion correct was about 61% in quiet, about 55% with music without lyrics and about 37% with music with lyrics. Even without lyrics, **fast, loud music** disrupted reading comprehension (Thompson et al., 2012). Silence or soft instrumental music is better.
- **When exercising:** An analysis of 139 studies found that music lifted mood and reduced how hard exercise felt, with larger performance effects at **fast tempos** (Terry et al., Psychological Bulletin, 2020).

## Conclusion

On the day I heard those unfair words, what comforted me was not someone's advice but a song I had made. Following the research, I found there was a reason for that comfort. While I was making it, my mind came away from work. The sense that I had decided and I had finished filled the control I had lost. The song, finished to my taste, was fitted to me more closely than any other. And at the line that bursts out after the breath, my brain gave joy as if it had been waiting.

Creating was not only for the talented. The creativity in the research was everyday activity such as writing lyrics, knitting and trying a new recipe, and the day after doing more of it, people were more energized.

If today was a tiring day, try writing down **four lines you wanted to hear**. Whether or not they become a song, while you write those few lines, today's fatigue will step back for a while.

## 🙋 FAQ

**Q1. Can I make a song if I know nothing about music?**

Yes. In the 45-minute making experiment, changes in the stress hormone were similar with or without experience. These days you can finish a song with an AI tool just by choosing lyrics and style. What matters more than skill is the experience of deciding for yourself and finishing.

**Q2. If AI made the song for me, does it still count as creating?**

Almost no research has looked specifically at creating with AI tools yet. Still, the absorption, control and finishing that research tied to recovery are also part of writing lyrics and choosing a structure. The more your words and your taste go in, the more it feels like "a song I made."

**Q3. What if music doesn't give me chills?**

There is nothing strange about that. Research has confirmed that some healthy people get little pleasure from music (Martínez-Molina et al., PNAS, 2016). Even without chills, you can still fully enjoy the pleasure of making something.
