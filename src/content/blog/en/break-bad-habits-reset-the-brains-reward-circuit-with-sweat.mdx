## 📌 Key Takeaways

> **The short answer:** When late-night snacks or sweets call, **even a 15-minute brisk walk** reduced the urge to eat. People who walked wanted sugary snacks less and ate about half as much chocolate. Rather than resisting the urge, giving yourself a different reward, exercise, at that same moment and recording it is the approach that fits the research.

- 🚶 **A 15-minute walk reduced sugar cravings.** When overweight people who often ate sugary snacks walked briskly for 15 minutes, their **sugary snack cravings** were lower than when they sat still (**PLoS ONE**, 2015).
- 🍫 **After walking, people ate about half the chocolate.** Regular chocolate eaters ate about half as much chocolate while working after a 15-minute walk (**Appetite**, 2012).
- 🏃 **Runner's high wasn't just about endorphins.** Even with a drug blocking endorphin action, the good feeling after running remained, and **endocannabinoids** rose (**Psychoneuroendocrinology**, 2021).
- 📝 **Recording brought people closer to their goals.** In a meta-analysis of 138 studies, people who **wrote down or shared** their progress reached their goals more often (**Psychological Bulletin**, 2016).

### 💬 "I'm not hungry, so why do I think of snacks every night?"

I get off work at 10 p.m. and get home at 11. I'm not really hungry, but I want something to eat, and eating every night made me gain weight and left my stomach uncomfortable the next morning. So when the urge to eat came, I started pulling out the snack puzzle piece and fitting in a **running** piece instead.

Surprisingly, on days I went running, the snack thoughts were completely gone. I wanted to eat because I was tired, and I ran even harder, yet my body felt refreshed instead. In the original post I explained this as "exercise restored my dopamine receptors and reset my reward circuit." Looking up the original papers this time, my experience fit the research well, but the parts explaining why needed some correcting.

## Deep Dive: How does exercise reduce late-night cravings?

### 1. Does exercise really reduce the urge to eat?

It did, and with less exercise than you might think.

- A University of Innsbruck team in Austria had 47 overweight people who often ate sugary snacks abstain from them for two days, then walk briskly for 15 minutes on one occasion and sit still on another. After walking, their **sugary snack cravings** were lower, and cravings rose less during a stressful task or with a snack in front of them (Ledochowski et al., **PLoS ONE**, 2015).
- A University of Exeter team in the UK had 78 regular chocolate eaters work in an office-like setting with chocolate nearby. Those who walked briskly for 15 minutes before working ate **about half as much** chocolate as those who only rested (Oh & Taylor, **Appetite**, 2012).
- A meta-analysis of 19 studies on people abstaining from smoking also found that a single bout of exercise reduced **cigarette cravings** (Haasova et al., **Addiction**, 2013).

The original post's practice, "when snacks call, don't resist; head out for 15 minutes right away," matches these experiments almost down to the minute. My experience that "the snack thoughts disappear after a run" is backed by research.

### 2. Do short videos and late-night snacks reduce dopamine receptors?

The original post said "repeated short videos, sweets and late-night snacks make the brain reduce **dopamine D2 receptors**, building tolerance," along with an image comparing a normal brain and an "addicted brain."

![An image comparing a normal brain and a brain with fewer dopamine receptors](/images/blog/c5a1245523/01.webp)
*An image comparing a normal brain and a brain with fewer dopamine receptors. Differences like this were observed in studies of drug addiction and severe obesity.*

Images like this come mainly from **drug addiction** research. For food, a team at Brookhaven National Laboratory in the US scanned the brains of 10 people with severe obesity (BMI over 40) using PET, and found fewer D2 receptors in the **striatum** than in normal-weight people, with fewer receptors at higher BMI (Wang et al., **Lancet**, 2001).

But I couldn't find research showing that watching short videos or reels, or occasional late-night snacking, reduces D2 receptors. The obesity study also couldn't tell whether fewer receptors led to overeating or overeating reduced receptors. So the part explaining everyday habits with the same image as an "addicted brain" is corrected.

### 3. Does exercise restore dopamine receptors?

A UCLA team divided methamphetamine-dependent patients in residential treatment into an exercise group and an education group for eight weeks. The exercise group had more **striatal D2/D3 receptors**; the education group didn't (Robertson et al., **Neuropsychopharmacology**, 2016).

The original post's claim that "exercise restores lowered dopamine receptors" points the same way as this study. But since it was a small study in people with drug addiction, it's too early to say the same happens in the brains of ordinary people with a snacking habit. And the original claim that "BDNF cuts the neural connections hardened by bad habits" isn't an explanation confirmed by research.

### 4. Is runner's high really from endorphins?

The original post said running releases **endorphins** and **endocannabinoids** that lift mood. One of them turned out to matter more.

A University of Hamburg team in Germany gave 63 recreational runners either a drug that blocks the action of **opioids** like endorphins (naloxone) or a placebo, then had them run for 45 minutes (Siebers et al., **Psychoneuroendocrinology**, 2021).

- Even with opioids blocked, the **good mood** and **reduced anxiety** after running still appeared.
- Instead, blood **endocannabinoids** (such as anandamide) rose.

Runner's high seems more related to endocannabinoids than the long-told "endorphin effect." But this study doesn't fully settle the cause either.

### 5. Does recording really help?

I post my workouts to Instagram every day and check my total and monthly distance in the Runday app. This habit is backed by research too.

A University of Sheffield team in the UK analyzed 138 studies with about 20,000 people on monitoring progress toward goals (Harkin et al., **Psychological Bulletin**, 2016).

- People who monitored their progress more often reached their goals more often.
- The effect was larger when progress was **written down** or **shared with others**.

The original post's claim that "a boost in self-esteem works as a secondary dopamine reward" isn't a confirmed explanation, but recording and sharing itself has solid evidence.

### 6. Summary: What held up and what was corrected

| Original claim | What the research says | Type of evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Exercising 15 minutes when an urge hits reduces it | True. A 15-minute walk reduced sugar cravings and chocolate eating | Experiments (PLoS ONE 2015, Appetite 2012) |
| Short videos and snacks reduce dopamine D2 receptors | Seen only in drug addiction and severe obesity; no evidence for everyday habits | PET study (Lancet 2001) |
| Exercise restores dopamine receptors | They increased in methamphetamine patients, but there's no evidence yet for ordinary people | Randomized trial (2016) |
| BDNF cuts the neural connections of bad habits | I couldn't find research confirming this | Insufficient evidence |
| Runner's high comes from endorphins and endocannabinoids | The good mood remained with opioids blocked, and endocannabinoids rose | Experiment (2021) |
| Recording workouts motivates you | True. Writing down or sharing progress helped people reach goals | Meta-analysis (Psych Bull 2016) |

## Solution: 3 Steps to Change Late-Night Cravings

First, rate your current urge with the tool below. Rate again after a 15-minute walk to see the change for yourself.

**Step 1. When the urge hits, don't resist; put your shoes on**
If you hold out by telling yourself "I mustn't eat," you keep thinking about it. Instead, put on your shoes right away and walk briskly or jog lightly for 15 minutes. In the experiments, 15 minutes was enough. If going out late is hard, stair climbing or marching in place at home works too.

**Step 2. On days with more time, run 20–30 minutes until breathing hard**
If you have a bit more time, run for 20–30 minutes at a pace where you can talk but not sing. The good feeling after running becomes a reward that makes you choose exercise again the next day.

**Step 3. Write down your workout and tell someone**
Right after exercising, briefly note today's distance and time, and the urge you got past. Logging it in an app or on social media, or telling family, makes it more effective. If you wonder why you fall apart every evening, also read [Does Mental Fatigue Make You Give Up?](/en/blog/why-we-give-up-diets-and-tasks-the-brain-uses-20-percent-of-energy/).

## Conclusion

My experience of snack thoughts disappearing on days I ran instead was confirmed by research. Even a 15-minute walk reduced sugar cravings. But explaining it as "short videos and snacks reduce dopamine receptors and exercise restores them" stretched drug addiction and severe obesity research too far into everyday life. Even without knowing the exact reason, the method is clear. When the urge comes, put on your shoes, walk for 15 minutes, and record it.

### 🙋 Frequently Asked Questions

#### Won't exercising late at night ruin my sleep?

In a meta-analysis of 23 studies, evening exercise within four hours of bedtime generally didn't disrupt sleep. But very hard exercise within an hour of bedtime could delay falling asleep (Stutz et al., **Sports Medicine**, 2019). At 11 p.m., a 15-minute brisk walk is better than a hard run.

#### Doesn't exercise make me hungrier?

It varies by person, but in the experiments, sugary snack cravings and chocolate eating actually fell after a 15-minute walk. If you're truly hungry after exercise, choose a light food with protein, like eggs or milk, over sugary snacks. The effects of exercise when you're anxious are covered in [Does Exercise Help Anxiety?](/en/blog/neuroscientist-anxiety-move-exercise-brain-science-guide/).
