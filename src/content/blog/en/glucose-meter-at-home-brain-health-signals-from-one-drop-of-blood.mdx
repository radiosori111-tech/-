## 📌 Key takeaways

> **The short answer:** To use a **glucose meter**, wash your hands with soap, dry them completely, and touch a drop of blood from the side of a fingertip to the test strip. Because blood sugar responses to the same food varied widely between people, it's worth measuring how your own body reacts. But home readings aren't used to diagnose diabetes.

- 🍞 **Same bread, different blood sugar.** An analysis of more than 46,000 meals eaten by 800 people found that blood sugar responses to the same food varied widely between individuals (**Cell**, 2015).
- 🥣 **Blood sugar spiked even without diabetes.** When 57 adults without diabetes wore continuous glucose monitors, many saw their blood sugar rise into the prediabetic range after cereal and milk (**PLOS Biology**, 2018).
- 🖐️ **Washing your hands really matters.** Testing without washing after peeling fruit gave "falsely high" readings well above the true value (**Diabetes Care**, 2011).
- 🧠 **The brain is an energy hog.** It's 2% of body weight but uses about 20% of the body's energy (**PNAS**, 2002).

### 💬 "Can people without diabetes use a glucose meter too?"

More and more people are reportedly looking for glucose meters at pharmacies and online. It's easy to think they're only for people diagnosed with diabetes, but as the link between blood sugar and brain health has become better known, many people pick one up for general health. In my original post, I walked through everything from choosing a meter to normal ranges.

Most of the usage steps and thresholds were right. This time, I added research showing why it's worth measuring at home, along with the accuracy details my first post left out.

## Deep Dive: what can home blood sugar readings tell you?

### 1. Is blood sugar really linked to the brain?

The brain is only about 2% of body weight, but even at rest it uses about **20%** of the body's energy, mainly from glucose (Raichle & Gusnard, **PNAS**, 2002). My original statement that "the brain is one of the organs that uses the most glucose" was right.

But the claim that "big swings in blood sugar hurt concentration" needs more caution. A meta-analysis of 31 studies found that within an hour of eating sugar, people actually felt **less alert and more tired** (Mantantzis et al., **Neuroscience & Biobehavioral Reviews**, 2019), but there aren't many studies yet that directly link the size of blood sugar swings to concentration. In the long run, even among people without diabetes, higher average blood sugar went with a higher risk of dementia (Crane et al., **NEJM**, 2013).

### 2. Why bother measuring your own blood sugar?

A team at Israel's Weizmann Institute fitted 800 people with continuous glucose monitors and analyzed the blood sugar responses to more than 46,000 meals eaten over a week (Zeevi et al., **Cell**, 2015). Blood sugar responses to the same food varied widely between people; some reacted more strongly to bananas than to bread, and others the other way around.

A Stanford University team continuously measured blood sugar in 57 adults without diabetes, and after a common breakfast such as cereal and milk, many participants' blood sugar rose into the prediabetic range (Hall et al., **PLOS Biology**, 2018). That's why measuring **how your own body actually responds** can tell you more than a generic "good foods" list.

### 3. What do you need to do to measure accurately?

**Hand washing**, which my original post stressed, matters even more than you might think. A Japanese team measured fingertip blood sugar after people peeled fruit such as tangerines, kiwis, and grapes without washing their hands, and fruit sugar on the skin produced readings **far higher** than true blood sugar (Hirose et al., **Diabetes Care**, 2011). After rinsing with water, readings went back to normal. Leftover water can dilute the blood the other way, so dry your hands completely before testing.

Home meters also aren't perfect. The international standard (ISO 15197:2013) passes a meter if 95% of its readings fall within ±15% of the true value (±15 mg/dL below 100 mg/dL). So rather than reacting to a single number, look at the trend across several readings taken under the same conditions.

### 4. What counts as normal, and where is diabetes diagnosed?

The thresholds in my original post match those of the American Diabetes Association (American Diabetes Association, **Diabetes Care**, 2024).

- **Fasting blood sugar:** 70–99 mg/dL is normal, 100–125 mg/dL is impaired fasting glucose, and 126 mg/dL or higher may meet the threshold for diabetes.
- **After eating:** The diagnostic standard is a value measured at a clinic two hours after drinking a glucose solution: below 140 mg/dL is normal and 200 mg/dL or higher meets the diabetes threshold. Treat a reading you take at home two hours after starting a meal only as a rough comparison.

> In my original post I wrote only that "if readings of 126 mg/dL or higher keep coming up, you might suspect diabetes." Diabetes is diagnosed not with a home fingertip meter but with **venous blood tests** at a clinic (fasting glucose, HbA1c, or a glucose tolerance test), so I added that a high reading at home should be confirmed with a clinic test.

## The fix: three steps to using a glucose meter well

**Step 1. Measure accurately**
Wash your hands with soap and dry them completely. Pricking the **side** of a fingertip rather than the center hurts less. Touch a drop of blood to the test strip, then throw away the used lancet and strip right away. Check the strips' expiration date too.

**Step 2. Pair fasting and post-meal readings**
If you don't have diabetes, you don't need to test every day. Two or three times a week, measure **first thing in the morning while fasting** and **two hours after starting a meal**. Enter your reading below to see which range it falls into.

**Step 3. Change high-reading meals and test again**
If a meal gives you a high post-meal reading, next time **eat vegetables and protein first** or add a **10-minute walk after eating**, and test again under the same conditions. A 10-minute walk after each meal lowered post-meal blood sugar more than one 30-minute walk a day (Reynolds et al., **Diabetologia**, 2016). One study also found that short sleep reduced the body's ability to handle glucose (Spiegel et al., **Lancet**, 1999), so comparing days after good and poor sleep can be revealing.

## Conclusion

A glucose meter isn't a diagnostic tool; it's **a tool for learning how your body responds**. Because blood sugar responses to the same food varied between people, your own numbers can tell you more than someone else's meal plan. Wash and dry your hands and measure accurately, then change the order of high-reading meals or walk afterward and test again. If high readings keep coming up, don't forget to confirm them with a clinic test.

### 🙋 Frequently asked questions

#### Without diabetes, is it worth wearing a continuous glucose monitor (CGM)?

There isn't yet enough evidence that CGMs improve health in people without diabetes. But wearing one for about two weeks can show which foods and habits you react to most, so it may be worth trying once if the cost isn't a burden. Just be careful not to let the numbers make you overly anxious. I showed meal order and walking after meals on a graph in [what insulin does](/en/blog/what-does-insulin-do-and-how-to-use-it-wisely/).

#### My fasting blood sugar was just over 100. Do I have diabetes?

No. 100–125 mg/dL is impaired fasting glucose, a prediabetes stage. Confirm it with clinic tests such as HbA1c, and in many cases it can be reversed through exercise and diet. I covered how to add strength training in [insulin resistance self-check](/en/blog/normal-blood-sugar-but-an-aging-brain-insulin-resistance-self-check/).
