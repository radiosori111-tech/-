Have you ever heard a sharp remark in a meeting and had a cutting reply fly out before you had time to think? Looking back later, you often wonder why you reacted that way.

> That reaction wasn't really a thought;
> *it was a reflex of the brain.*

We usually treat it as a matter of personality or emotional control, but brain science explains these <strong>immediate reactions with the concept of reflexes</strong>. A reflex is <strong><em>a phenomenon in which the body or emotions move first, before the brain thinks deeply, when a stimulus comes in</em></strong>.

What's interesting is that reflexes don't stop at physical responses like your leg jerking when your knee is tapped. The idea being put forward is that <strong><em>repeated emotional reactions, speech habits and even habitual attitudes are ultimately a kind of reflex circuit the brain has learned</em></strong>. Today let's unpack, one by one, how these reflexes form and why they even shape our attitudes.

## Reflexes: the moment the spinal cord moves before the brain

<strong>When your hand touches a hot pot, you've already pulled it away before you even feel the pain</strong>. That speed is possible because the signal isn't sent up to the brain and back down as a command; it's handled right away at the level of the spinal cord. In fact, <strong>these reflexes happen in around 0.1 seconds</strong>, a speed conscious judgment can never match.

You've probably had the test at a clinic where a rubber hammer taps just below your knee and your leg kicks up on its own. That's the patellar reflex, a classic reflex response, used as the most basic way to check whether neural circuits are working normally. In this way, <strong>the brain</strong> doesn't judge every stimulus one by one; <strong>it's structured to handle signals tied to survival first, through a shortcut.</strong>

## Why did Pavlov's dogs drool at the sound of a bell?

Not all reflexes are innate. When the physiologist Pavlov repeatedly rang a bell every time he fed his dogs, they eventually began to drool at the sound of the bell alone, with no food. A body that originally responded only to food had learned, through repeated experience, to respond the same way to a completely unrelated bell.

This is called a <strong>conditioned reflex</strong>, and people aren't much different. When your phone vibrates, your hand heads to your pocket on its own mid-conversation; even hearing a notification sound makes your heart tighten a little. These too are close to learned conditioned reflexes.

In the end, this means <strong><em>repeated experience can build brand-new automatic response circuits in the brain that weren't there before</em></strong>.

## Speech habits and other habits were conditioned reflexes built by repetition

The same tone of voice popping out every time you're irritated, or biting your nails every time you're stressed, is an extension of these conditioned reflexes. Even a reaction you once chose consciously, when repeated the same way in the same situation many times, gets polished by the brain into a faster, more efficient circuit.

<strong>Brain science explains that repeated behavior patterns like these are stored mainly in a region called the basal ganglia</strong>. It's the same principle as learning to ride a bicycle: at first it took all your concentration, but now you can pedal without a thought. Once speech habits and emotional reactions harden this way, they become much harder to stop consciously later on.

## Attitude isn't innate personality but accumulated reflexes

We often say someone "is just like that," but from a brain-science perspective, much of that <strong>attitude</strong> is closer to the result of <strong>reflex responses repeated over a long time</strong>. The person who raises their voice first when angry and the person who laughs off even hard situations each have response patterns they've repeated countless times.

<!-- related-link -->
If emotional reactions are reflex circuits the brain has learned, there is also a way to practice stepping back from them: [**acceptance and commitment therapy (ACT) and cognitive defusion**](/en/blog/thoughts-are-not-you-act-cognitive-defusion/).

That's also why changing your attitude is so hard. It's not simply a matter of pulling yourself together; you have to fight neural circuits that have already hardened. Put the other way, <strong>the very fact that the circuits were built by repetition is also hope that they can be changed again through repetition</strong>.

## The key to changing automatic responses: a 0.1-second gap before you react

You can't erase a hardened reflex circuit all at once, but change begins simply by creating <strong>a very short *gap* between stimulus and response</strong>.

<strong>The moment anger surges, simply taking one deep breath, or naming the emotion you're feeling before you react, can buy time for the frontal lobe to step in</strong>.

This short gap matters because in that moment the frontal lobe gets <strong><em>a chance to consider other options</em></strong> <strong>instead of the old automatic response</strong>. Even if it feels awkward and unnatural at first, if you keep practicing creating this gap consciously, you'll feel the speed of your reactions themselves gradually change.

## New reflexes start with small repetitions

In the end, <strong>the surest way to erase an old reflex is to fill its place with a new one</strong>. Just as Pavlov's dogs learned to respond to a bell, if we deliberately repeat the responses we want, those responses can become more and more automatic too.

- The first step is to write down in advance the situations where your emotions run high and identify your own patterns.
- If you decide ahead of time on one new sentence to use in those situations, you'll gain the breathing room to catch the words that used to fly out reflexively.
- If you practice the new response at the same time every day, even briefly, a few weeks later it will feel as natural as a reflex.

Attitudes aren't formed overnight, and they don't change overnight. But simply realizing that your reactions so far were reflexes built by repetition may give you room to make a slightly different choice the next time that moment comes.
