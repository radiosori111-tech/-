## 📌 Key takeaways

> **The short answer:** Imagining "I've already done it" isn't enough to reach a goal. What research supports is picturing a good outcome **together with the obstacles that will get in your way**, deciding your actions in advance as "**if X happens, then I will do Y**," and **tracking** your progress. A habit of moving your body supports that follow-through.

- 💭 **Picturing only good outcomes drains energy.** People who imagined an idealized future **felt less energized** than those who didn't (**Journal of Experimental Social Psychology**, 2011), and people who started a weight-loss program with purely positive fantasies **lost less weight** a year later (**Cognitive Therapy and Research**, 1991).
- 🧱 **Picturing obstacles gets you moving.** High school students who pictured their wish together with their obstacles and made "if X, then Y" plans **completed more practice questions** for a test (**Educational Psychology**, 2011).
- 📝 **"If X" plans work well.** In a meta-analysis of 94 studies, these **implementation intentions** had a medium-to-large effect on reaching goals (**Advances in Experimental Social Psychology**, 2006).
- 📊 **Writing it down brings you closer.** In an analysis of 138 studies, people who **recorded and checked** their progress were more likely to reach their goals, especially when they wrote it down or told others (**Psychological Bulletin**, 2016).

### 💬 "I bought a 35,000-won planner, so why haven't my plans moved?"

At the start of 2023, determined to make it a year of growth, I was drawn in by the slogan "a planner that changes your life" and splurged on a 35,000-won planner. But about three weeks into January, most of my plans were moving far more slowly than I'd expected. So I asked myself: "What am I doing wrong? And if I'm not doing anything wrong, what should I do, and how?"

At the time I came up with three answers: **change your thinking, use neuroplasticity, and move your body.** This post checks how far those three answers hold up against research, and what needs fixing. The photo below is the to-do list I wrote around then to work toward my goals.

## Deep Dive: how do goals actually get achieved?

### 1. If you imagine you've already achieved it, will it happen?

In my original post I introduced the Korean author Kim Sae-hae's book on "money secrets" and wrote that if your goal is "earning a million won a month on YouTube," you should think "**I already earn a million won a month.**" The reasoning was that focusing on what you lack makes you anxious, while focusing on what you have relaxes your shoulders.

I still think letting go of anxiousness is right. But imagining "I've already done it" turned out to be the opposite of what research shows.

- Gabriele Oettingen's team at New York University had people imagine their coming week **going as well as it possibly could** (Kappes & Oettingen, **Journal of Experimental Social Psychology**, 2011). Those who imagined an idealized future showed **lower energy**, measured by blood pressure, than those who didn't, and accomplished less that week. It was as if the brain relaxed as though the goal were already reached.
- Among women starting a weight-loss program, those who **fantasized only positively** about the results lost less weight a year later (Oettingen & Wadden, **Cognitive Therapy and Research**, 1991).
- Interestingly, **"I can do it" expectations based on past experience** actually helped people succeed (Oettingen & Mayer, **Journal of Personality and Social Psychology**, 2002). Self-confidence like "I can do this" helps, but daydreaming "I've already done it" saps your strength.

### 2. So how should you think about it?

The method Oettingen's team proposes is **mental contrasting**. After vividly picturing the outcome you want, you immediately picture **the obstacle inside you that will get in the way**. To this you add **implementation intentions**, studied by Peter Gollwitzer at New York University: a plan of the form "**if [obstacle] happens, then I will do [action].**" The combination is called **WOOP**, from the first letters of Wish, Outcome, Obstacle, and Plan.

- When high school students preparing for a college entrance practice test used this method, they **completed more practice questions** over the summer than students who only wrote positive thoughts (Duckworth et al., **Educational Psychology**, 2011).
- In a meta-analysis pooling 94 studies and more than 8,000 people, "if X, then Y" **implementation intentions** had a **medium-to-large effect** on reaching goals (Gollwitzer & Sheeran, **Advances in Experimental Social Psychology**, 2006).

Implementation intentions work because they lay down a path in the brain in advance, so that when the situation arrives the action **springs out automatically** without deliberation. Try building one below.

### 3. Does the brain really change with use?

In my original post I introduced Anders Hansen's *The Attention Fix* (Swedish title *Skärmhjärnan*) and the story of London taxi drivers whose **hippocampus** grows as they learn the streets. Looking up the original research, that story was true.

- When 79 trainee taxi drivers preparing for the exam on London's complex streets were followed for nearly four years, only those who **passed the exam** gained gray matter in the back of the hippocampus. Those who failed or quit did not (Woollett & Maguire, **Current Biology**, 2011).
- When people followed navigation directions, unlike when they found their own way, the hippocampus and prefrontal cortex didn't respond to the branching of streets (Javadi et al., **Nature Communications**, 2017). As Hansen says, the brain doesn't spend energy on functions it isn't using.

> In my original post I wrote, "We have 20,000 genes but 200 trillion neural connections, a difference of 100 million times." It's true that humans have about 20,000 genes, but estimates of synapse numbers vary widely between studies, and the "100 million times" figure doesn't add up, so I removed it. The research above shows "the brain changes with use" well enough without that number.

### 4. Does writing things down really bring you closer to your goals?

Buying a planner wasn't the wrong choice. What mattered was **how I used it**. A team at the University of Sheffield analyzed 138 experiments with about 20,000 people (Harkin et al., **Psychological Bulletin**, 2016). People who were prompted to **monitor their own progress** were more likely to reach their goals, and the effect was larger when they **wrote it down on paper or reported it to others**. The key is not stopping at writing your plans, but writing **how much you actually did**.

### 5. Does moving your body help with goals?

In the Korean drama *Misaeng*, the main character Jang Geu-rae hears this from his childhood Go teacher: "**If you want to win, first build a body that can bear your struggles. Without the protection of stamina, willpower is just a slogan.**"

Research backs this up. According to a review by a team at the University of Illinois, regular aerobic exercise supports **executive function**, our ability to plan, focus, and resist impulses, along with the frontal lobes and hippocampus that handle it (Hillman et al., **Nature Reviews Neuroscience**, 2008). Taking a walk or doing light exercise when you feel stuck is more than a change of mood; it builds **the brain's foundation** for moving toward your goals again.

## The plan: 3 steps to achieve your goals

**Step 1. Thinking: picture the outcome and the obstacle together (WOOP)**
First, picture what you want and the best thing about achieving it. Then honestly write down, "What is the obstacle **inside me** that gets in the way?" The more specific, the better: being busy, being tired, reaching for your phone first. You can use the WOOP builder above as is.

**Step 2. Neuroplasticity: repeat "if X, then I will Y" every day**
Decide in one sentence what you'll do the moment the obstacle appears, such as "If I feel like sitting on the couch after work, then I will put on my sneakers first." The more you repeat the same action in the same situation, the more solid that path becomes in your brain. How long it takes for a habit to run automatically varies, but the median was 66 days.

**Step 3. Body and records: move, and write down what you did**
A few times a week, walk or exercise until you're slightly out of breath. In your planner, write not only plans but **what you actually did today**, and look back once a week. Telling someone you trust about your progress also helps.

| Step | What | How much | Evidence | What the evidence showed |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Thinking | Write your wish, outcome, and obstacle | About five minutes per goal | J Exp Soc Psychol (2011), Educational Psychology (2011) | Picturing only good outcomes lowered energy; picturing obstacles too led to more action |
| 2. Implementation intentions | Decide "if X, then I will Y" | Repeat in the same situation every day | Adv Exp Soc Psychol (2006) | A medium-to-large effect across 94 studies |
| 3. Body and records | Exercise, and write down what you did | Exercise a few times a week; review weekly | Psychological Bulletin (2016), Nat Rev Neurosci (2008) | People who tracked achieved more, and exercise supported executive function |

## Conclusion

What it takes to reach a goal isn't imagining "I've already done it," but **a plan that moves you even knowing the obstacles**. Picture only good outcomes and the brain relaxes as if the goal were already reached; picture the obstacles too and decide "if X, then Y," and action follows on its own. Add writing down what you've done and moving your body to strengthen your brain's foundation, and a 35,000-won planner really can become a planner that changes your life. ### 🙋 Frequently asked questions

#### So should I stop thinking positively?

Not at all. What helped in the research was **"I can do it" expectations based on past experience**; what drained energy was **daydreaming** "I've already done it." Picturing positive outcomes is also the first two steps of WOOP. The key is not to stop there, but to carry on to the obstacle and the plan. Why affirmations alone don't change things is covered in more detail in [Why affirmations fail even after 1,000 times](/en/blog/why-affirmations-fail-even-after-1000-times-brain-science/).

#### What if I have several goals?

Make a separate WOOP for each goal, but it's better to focus on **one or two** at first, because implementation intentions work best when you pair one situation with one action. Why new things feel tiresome and make you sleepy is covered in [Why do new things make you sleepy?](/en/blog/the-brain-is-changing/).
