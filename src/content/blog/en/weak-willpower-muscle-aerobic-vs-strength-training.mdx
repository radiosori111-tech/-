## 📌 Key Takeaways

> **The short answer:** Strength training helped the brain too. When older adults with mild cognitive impairment did strength training for six months, their cognitive scores rose, and **those whose strength rose most** improved most. But strength training wasn't uniquely special: aerobic exercise also improved cognition, so doing **both** is closest to the evidence.

- 🏋️ **Cognitive scores rose as strength rose.** In a trial of 100 older adults with mild cognitive impairment, six months of strength training improved global cognitive scores, and those whose strength rose most improved most (**Journal of the American Geriatrics Society**, 2017).
- 🔄 **Both strength and aerobic training worked.** In a meta-analysis of 39 studies in people over 50, both strength and aerobic training improved cognition (**British Journal of Sports Medicine**, 2018).
- 😌 **Strength training also eased depressive symptoms.** In a meta-analysis of 33 randomized trials with 1,877 people, strength training clearly reduced depressive symptoms (**JAMA Psychiatry**, 2018).
- ✊ **Strength was a marker of overall health.** In a study measuring grip strength in 140,000 people across 17 countries, every 5 kg lower grip strength meant 16% higher risk of death (**Lancet**, 2015).

### 💬 "I ran every day, so why didn't my 'staying power' change?"

When I first heard that lifting heavy barbells is good for the brain, honestly, I was skeptical. I'd vaguely believed exercise was for the body and reading was for the mind. I ran for 30 minutes every morning and thought that was enough.

But after nearly a year of running, my ability to resist impulses and push plans through to the end didn't seem to change much. So I added **weight training** two or three times a week, 40–50 minutes per session. After about four to six weeks I noticed my focus changing, and above all I felt I'd gained "the strength not to give up."

This post originally explained this experience as "strength training trains the prefrontal cortex through a different route than aerobic exercise, building willpower," citing a YouTube video and general links. Looking up the original papers this time, the overall direction that strength training is good for the brain held up, but the idea that "only strength training builds executive function" needed correcting.

## Deep Dive: How does strength training change the brain?

### 1. Does strength training really improve cognition?

It did. A University of Sydney team assigned 100 adults over 55 with mild cognitive impairment, for six months, with one group doing **progressive strength training** two or three times a week (the SMART trial; Fiatarone Singh et al., **JAMDA**, 2014).

- The strength training group's global cognitive scores improved, and the effect lasted more than a year after training ended.
- In a further analysis by the same team, **the more someone's strength rose**, the more their cognitive scores rose (Mavros et al., **Journal of the American Geriatrics Society**, 2017).

The key point of this research is that building strength itself moved together with cognitive improvement. Another study found older women's judgment test scores rose after a year of strength training; that's covered in [Does Exercise Help Anxiety?](/en/blog/neuroscientist-anxiety-move-exercise-brain-science-guide/).

### 2. Does only strength training build "willpower"?

The first version of this post said "it's strength training, not aerobic exercise, that intensively boosts executive function." The research didn't quite show that.

When a University of Canberra team pooled 39 randomized trials in people over 50 (Northey et al., **British Journal of Sports Medicine**, 2018):

- Both **strength training** and **aerobic exercise** improved cognition, with similar effect sizes.
- Combined training and tai chi also worked.
- Sessions of 45–60 minutes at moderate or higher intensity had larger effects.

So it's hard to split things as neatly as the original's "aerobic for memory, strength for willpower." Aerobic exercise also improved **executive function**, the planning and judgment skills, and strength training also helped memory. That makes the earlier version's conclusion, "do both," the more accurate answer. Research showing aerobic exercise like walking grew the hippocampus is covered in [Can a Late-Start Exercise Habit Change Your Brain?](/en/blog/brain-health-plasticity-exercise-habits-compound-effect/).

### 3. What signals does muscle send to the brain?

Muscle isn't just tissue for moving the body; it's also an organ that releases various signaling molecules when it contracts. Bente Pedersen's team at the University of Copenhagen in Denmark called these molecules **myokines** and reviewed research showing they affect other organs, including fat tissue, the liver and the brain (Pedersen & Febbraio, **Nature Reviews Endocrinology**, 2012).

The **IGF-1** introduced in the first version is also one of the molecules strength training can raise. But what myokines and IGF-1 do exactly in the human brain has mostly been studied in animals, and nothing like "a repair crew filling cracks in the brain" has been confirmed. The exact route by which strength training improves cognition is still being studied.

### 4. Does strength training help with low mood?

It did. When a University of Limerick team in Ireland pooled 33 randomized trials of strength training with 1,877 people, strength training clearly reduced depressive symptoms (Gordon et al., **JAMA Psychiatry**, 2018). It worked whether training volume was high or low, and whether or not strength actually increased.

But the earlier version's statement that "academia is reclassifying depression as a brain inflammation disease" goes too far. Research linking inflammation to some depression is growing, but depression hasn't been reclassified as an inflammatory disease. Why strength training eases low mood also isn't settled into a single explanation yet.

### 5. Do muscle mass and grip strength really predict health?

They did. The PURE study, led by McMaster University in Canada, measured grip strength in about 140,000 people across 17 countries and followed them for four years. Every **5 kg lower** grip strength meant **16%** higher all-cause mortality, and higher cardiovascular mortality too (Leong et al., **Lancet**, 2015). The earlier version's line that "grip strength is used as a marker that predicts mortality" fits this study. But it's an observational study, so it doesn't mean building grip strength lowers death risk by that much.

## Solution: 3 Steps to Combining Strength and Aerobic Training

**Step 1. Add strength training 2–3 times a week**
If you only do aerobic exercise now, add strength training two or three times a week. Start with moves that use big muscles, like squats, push-ups and lunges, and the key is gradually increasing the weight you can handle. In the research, effects were larger the more strength rose.

**Step 2. Keep your aerobic exercise**
Adding strength training doesn't mean cutting aerobic exercise. Both improved cognition. Split your week like this.

**Step 3. Gradually add weight and keep going for 4+ weeks**
Start strength training with light weights and add gradually. I only noticed changes after four to six weeks. If you're older, focus on learning good form for the first few weeks.

## Conclusion

If you want to keep using your brain well for a long time, aerobic exercise alone fell a little short. Strength training raised cognitive scores in step with strength gains, and eased depressive symptoms too. But strength training wasn't a uniquely special key to willpower. Both aerobic and strength training helped the brain, and doing both was the answer. The "strength not to give up" I felt after adding weights was probably a change that running and strength training made together.

### 🙋 Frequently Asked Questions

#### How much strength training does it take to affect the brain?

In the research, effects appeared after two or three sessions a week for several months or more, with 45–60 minutes at moderate or higher intensity working well. I noticed changes after about four to six weeks of two or three 40–50 minute sessions a week. Consistency and gradual weight increases matter more than intensity.

#### I'm older. Is it OK to start strength training?

Yes. Most of the studies above included people over 50, even older adults with mild cognitive impairment. But start with light weights and slow movements so you don't strain your joints, and talk with a doctor first if you have heart or joint problems.
