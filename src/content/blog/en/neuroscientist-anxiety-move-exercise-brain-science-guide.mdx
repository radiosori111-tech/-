## 📌 Key Takeaways

> **The short answer:** Exercise is likely to help when you're anxious. It lowered anxiety symptoms by a **medium amount**, and people who repeatedly ran hard enough to breathe heavily became less afraid of a **pounding heart and breathlessness**. Start with about 20 minutes of movement that gets you breathing hard, a few times a week.

- 🏃 **Exercise reduced anxiety.** A large overview pooling 1,039 trials and about 128,000 people found exercise lowered **anxiety** and **depression** symptoms by a medium amount, with bigger effects at higher intensity (**British Journal of Sports Medicine**, 2023).
- 💓 **People got used to their body's signals.** People who ran on a treadmill six times for 20 minutes over two weeks had lower **anxiety sensitivity**, the fear of body signals like a racing heart (**Depression and Anxiety**, 2008).
- ⚡ **Six minutes of hard exercise raised BDNF.** After six minutes of all-out cycling, blood **BDNF** rose far more than after 90 minutes of light cycling. But there were only 12 participants, and memory wasn't measured (**Journal of Physiology**, 2023).
- 🏋️ **Strength training helped judgment.** Women aged 65–75 who did strength training once or twice a week for a year improved on **executive function** tests (**Archives of Internal Medicine**, 2010).

### 💬 "Can exercise really reduce anxiety?"

This post began as notes on *Move the Body, Heal the Mind* by **Jennifer Heisz**, a neuroscientist at McMaster University in Canada. In the book, Heisz writes that she struggled with anxiety for a long time, and that feeling better through movement led her to study exercise and the brain.

After reading the book, I too shifted away from grand workout plans toward moving my body for even six minutes a day. ## Deep Dive: How does exercise change an anxious brain?

### 1. Does exercise really reduce anxiety?

A team at the University of South Australia gathered 97 systematic reviews on exercise and mental health, covering 1,039 trials and about 128,000 participants (Singh et al., **British Journal of Sports Medicine**, 2023).

- Exercise reduced **depression**, **anxiety** and psychological distress, with a medium effect size.
- **Higher-intensity exercise** had bigger effects, and benefits appeared even in short programs of 12 weeks or less.
- Many types of exercise worked, including walking, strength training and yoga.

The book's core message that exercise reduces anxiety is backed by large-scale evidence. But this analysis didn't only look at people diagnosed with anxiety disorders, so for an anxiety disorder that needs treatment, exercise is best seen as a complement to treatment, not a replacement.

### 2. Why does exercise that leaves you breathless help anxiety?

The book explains that the pounding heart and breathlessness of exercise resemble the body's response to anxiety, and that with repeated exercise the brain comes to see these signals as less threatening. This has been tested in experiments.

A team at Southern Methodist University recruited 60 people with high **anxiety sensitivity**, meaning they were unusually afraid of their body's signals. Some of them ran on a treadmill hard enough to breathe heavily, six times for 20 minutes over two weeks (Smits et al., **Depression and Anxiety**, 2008).

- The exercise group showed a clear drop in **anxiety sensitivity** compared with the group that only waited.
- Anxiety sensitivity is a trait that can lead to anxiety problems like panic, so lowering it also helps prevent anxiety.

The original post said "even about 20 minutes of walking or light cardio lowers anxiety," but the experiment used something a bit harder than walking: **running hard enough to get breathless**. Getting used to that breathless feeling was the key.

### 3. Is six minutes of hard exercise really enough?

The book's much-discussed "six-minute workout" comes from a University of Otago study in New Zealand. When 12 people cycled all-out for six minutes, blood **BDNF** rose four to five times more than after 90 minutes of light cycling (Gibbons et al., **Journal of Physiology**, 2023).

Two points from this result need correcting.

- The study measured **BDNF in the blood**, not memory. The original post's claim that "it can have a bigger memory effect than long light exercise" isn't confirmed by this study.
- There were only 12 participants, and the result looked at the period right after a single session.

Still, the fact that short, hard exercise strongly moves a substance that helps the brain is good news for busy people. Other ways to raise BDNF are covered separately in [How to Increase BDNF](/en/blog/how-to-increase-bdnf-exercise-food-reading-meditation-sleep-evidence-and-duration/).

### 4. Is morning exercise better for focus?

A meta-analysis of 79 studies found that a single bout of exercise **slightly** improved cognitive functions like attention and judgment right afterward (Chang et al., **Brain Research**, 2012). The **dopamine** and **norepinephrine** that rise during exercise are thought to play a role.

But the original post's claim that "exercising in the morning affects your focus longer throughout the day" wasn't confirmed by this analysis. Effects were mostly measured shortly after exercise. So rather than morning always being best, moving briefly **right before work that needs focus** is closer to the evidence.

### 5. Is walking in nature twice as effective?

A University of Essex team analyzed 10 studies with 1,252 people on exercise in nature, so-called **green exercise** (Barton & Pretty, **Environmental Science & Technology**, 2010).

- Exercising in nature, such as parks or by water, improved **mood** and **self-esteem**.
- The effect was largest in the first **five minutes**, and greater near water.

But the figure "twice as much as indoors" couldn't be found in this analysis. The original post's "twice the effect" is corrected to "mood improved even in a short time."

### 6. Does strength training prevent dementia?

A University of British Columbia team divided 155 women aged 65–75 into groups doing strength training once a week, twice a week, or balance and stretching exercises, for a year (Liu-Ambrose et al., **Archives of Internal Medicine**, 2010).

- Both strength-training groups improved by more than 10% on tests of **executive function**, the ability to plan and resist interference.
- The balance-and-stretching group's scores actually dropped slightly.

There's evidence that strength training protects judgment, but **it hasn't been shown to prevent dementia**. The original post also said to take it "as something that may help rather than a firm claim," and the research supports about that much.

### 7. Summary: What held up and what was corrected

| Original claim | What the research says | Type of evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Exercise reduces anxiety | True. Anxiety and depression symptoms fell by a medium amount | Overview of reviews (BJSM 2023) |
| Exercise makes body signals feel less threatening | True. Anxiety sensitivity fell after two weeks of running | Randomized trial (2008) |
| Six minutes of hard exercise is better for memory | BDNF rose sharply, but memory wasn't measured, and there were 12 participants | Experiment (J Physiol 2023) |
| Morning exercise boosts focus all day | Cognition improved slightly right after exercise, but I found no evidence morning is better | Meta-analysis (Brain Res 2012) |
| Walking in nature is twice as effective | Mood and self-esteem improved, but there was no "twice" figure | Meta-analysis (2010) |
| Strength training leads to dementia prevention | Executive function improved, but dementia prevention wasn't confirmed | Randomized trial (Arch Intern Med 2010) |

First, pick the state closest to how you feel right now. You'll see the matching method and evidence.

**Step 1. Three times a week, 20 minutes breathing hard**
If anxiety is frequent, walk fast or run three times a week for 20 minutes, at a pace where you can talk but not sing. The racing heart may feel uncomfortable at first, but getting used to that feeling is exactly how exercise reduces anxiety.

**Step 2. Six hard minutes on busy days, a short session before focus**
On days with no time, move for just six minutes, mixing in stair climbing or 30-second sprints. Before important work, walking for 10–20 minutes right beforehand is a good choice.

**Step 3. Add strength training 1–2 times a week and walks in the park**
Add bodyweight strength exercises like squats and push-ups once or twice a week. On low days, even five minutes of walking in a park or by water helps. Whether to exercise on days your body is already tired is covered in [Will Exercising When Exhausted Make Me More Tired?](/en/blog/already-exhausted-will-exercise-make-me-more-tired/).

## Conclusion

The core message of *Move the Body, Heal the Mind*, that moving your body changes an anxious brain, is backed by large-scale research. In particular, the finding that getting used to breathlessness reduced anxiety shows well why exercise helps when you're anxious. But parts where the numbers or scope ran ahead of the evidence, like "six minutes improves memory," "walking in nature works twice as well" and "strength training prevents dementia," were corrected to match the research. Rather than a grand plan, start today with 20 minutes of walking that gets you breathing hard.

### 🙋 Frequently Asked Questions

#### I have an anxiety disorder. Can exercise alone make it better?

Exercise clearly helps reduce anxiety symptoms, but for a diagnosed anxiety disorder, think of it as a complement to treatment rather than a replacement. If you're worried about a severe racing heart or panic during exercise, start at a lower intensity and share your plan with your doctor or counselor.

#### Exercise makes my heart race, and that makes me more anxious. What should I do?

That's a natural reaction at first. In the experiment, people became less afraid of the feeling by repeating exercise that left them breathless. For the first few days, start by raising your walking pace little by little, and add slow exhaling breaths. The breathing method is in the [5-minute breathing habit](/en/blog/breath-is-your-brains-switch/) post mentioned above.
