## 📌 Key Takeaways

> **The short answer:** Tidying your room made it easier to focus on one task, but new ideas actually came more readily in a **slightly messy room**. Research points to using a **spot with a clear view** for focused work, a different spot for generating ideas, and in either case keeping your **phone in another room**.

- 🧹 **Tidy and messy rooms helped different things.** People in a tidy room chose apples as a snack and donated more, while people in a messy room came up with more **original ideas** (**Psychological Science**, 2013).
- 👀 **Objects in view compete in the brain.** When many objects are visible at once, their signals in the **visual cortex** suppress one another, and focusing attention on one place reduces that competition (**Journal of Neuroscience**, 2011).
- 📱 **A phone takes up brainpower just by being visible.** People with their phone on the desk scored lower on **working memory** than those who left it in another room (**Journal of the Association for Consumer Research**, 2017).
- 🏠 **Feeling that your home is cluttered showed up in the body too.** Wives who described their homes as cluttered had **cortisol** that fell less across the day and a rising depressed mood (**Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin**, 2010).

## Deep Dive: How does space change the brain's focus?

### 1. Why does a cluttered desk scatter your focus?

Our eyes take in many objects at once, but the amount the brain can process in detail is limited. A Princeton team looked at this process with fMRI (McMains & Kastner, **Journal of Neuroscience**, 2011).

- When several objects were in view together, their signals in the **visual cortex** suppressed one another and weakened. In effect, the objects **compete** for attention.
- When the objects could be seen as one grouped whole, or when a person **focused attention** on one object, the competition shrank.

In short, a cluttered desk gets in the way because the brain keeps spending effort choosing "what to look at." Clearing objects unrelated to your current task out of view reduces that competition itself.

### 2. Is a tidy room always better?

Here is the unexpected result. A University of Minnesota team sent participants into either a neatly ordered room or a room with papers and objects strewn about (Vohs et al., **Psychological Science**, 2013).

- People in the **tidy room** picked an **apple** over chocolate as they left more often, and **donated** more to charity.
- People in the **messy room**, asked to think of new uses for ping-pong balls, produced more ideas that judges rated as **more original**.
- When choosing from a menu, people in the tidy room chose the familiar "classic" option more, while people in the messy room chose the "new" option more.

The tidy space nudged people toward rules and familiar choices; the messy space nudged them toward thinking outside convention. So the original claim that "a tidy space is good for flow" fits best **when you need to focus on one task**.

### 3. Is a phone on the desk fine if it's switched off?

The biggest distraction in a space was the phone. A University of Texas team had about 800 people leave their phone face down on the desk, in a pocket or bag, or in another room, and then solve tasks (Ward et al., **Journal of the Association for Consumer Research**, 2017).

- People who left their phone in **another room** scored highest on the **working memory** task, and those with it on the desk scored lowest.
- The phones were silenced or switched off, and most participants didn't feel distracted by them. Yet the scores still differed.

The interpretation is that trying not to think about your phone is itself mental work. Going one step beyond turning off notifications, it's better to put it **out of sight**.

### 4. Can the feeling that your home is cluttered affect your body?

A UCLA team asked 30 dual-earner couples to give a tour of their own home while describing it, and measured **cortisol** in saliva over three days (Saxbe & Repetti, **Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin**, 2010).

- Wives who described their homes as "cluttered" or "full of unfinished projects" had cortisol that fell less across the day, and their **depressed mood** rose toward evening.
- People who described their homes as "restful" or "restorative" showed the opposite pattern.

Cortisol is normally high in the morning and falls toward evening; a flatter slope is read as stress that isn't winding down. But this was an observational study, so it can't tell whether clutter caused stress or whether stressed people saw their homes as more cluttered.

### 5. Does the brain really release dopamine during flow?

In the original post I wrote that "in flow, the mind-wandering regions quiet down and **dopamine** boosts focus." Checking the original papers, this needed some correction.

- In an fMRI study that induced flow with mental arithmetic matched to each person's skill, flow came with more activity in the **inferior frontal gyrus** and **putamen**, and less in the **medial prefrontal cortex**, used when thinking about oneself, and the **amygdala** (Ulrich et al., **NeuroImage**, 2014). The idea that regions tied to mind-wandering go quiet fits this result.
- Dopamine was not measured directly during flow. A PET study found a correlation: people who usually experience flow more readily had more **dopamine D2 receptors** in the **striatum** (de Manzano et al., **NeuroImage**, 2013). It also had only about 25 participants.

### 6. Are there spaces the brain likes?

There is also research touching on Professor Jeong's talk topic, "spaces the brain prefers." When people solved tasks in rooms with ceilings set at 8 feet or 10 feet, those under the **high ceiling** recognized words related to "freedom" faster and grouped objects by broader relationships. Under the **low ceiling**, people focused more on individual details (Meyers-Levy & Zhu, **Journal of Consumer Research**, 2007).

Rather than spaces being good or bad, each space suits different work. Open spaces may fit big-picture thinking; cozy ones may fit careful checking. **Step 1. Put your phone in another room**
When you start focused work, leave your phone in another room. Silencing it or turning it face down wasn't enough. If you're worried about urgent messages, go check at set times.

**Step 2. Clear the view at your focus spot**
Leave only what this task needs on the desk, gather the rest into one box, and move it out of sight. It doesn't need to be perfectly tidy. The key is reducing the number of objects in view.

**Step 3. Change seats when you need ideas**
When you need new ideas, move to another spot where a little mess is fine. Somewhere with a high ceiling or wide window is even better. In a small home, simply turning your chair around in the same room works too.

## Conclusion

The question raised by Raemian Gallery's exhibition and talks, "does space make me?", can be partly answered by research. Space clearly changed the brain's focus. But a tidy room wasn't always the answer. When you need to sink deeply into one task, clear your view and put the phone away; when you need new thinking, change seats. Choosing the small spaces in your home to fit the work is the first step toward a space that feels like you.

### 🙋 Frequently Asked Questions

#### Are people who can't keep things tidy more creative?

That's hard to say. The experiment randomly placed the same kinds of people in messy or tidy rooms, showing that a space you spend a short time in can shift the direction of your thinking. It did not look at tidying habits and innate creativity. If your focus keeps slipping, practicing bringing your attention back helps along with the space. Try the 10-minute timer in [How to Improve Reading Focus](/en/blog/why-resolutions-keep-lasting-three-days-train-the-amcc-by-reading/).

#### What if my home is too small to separate spots?

Change how the same desk looks depending on the time. When focusing, keep only what you need on it; when brainstorming, spread out notes and materials. Turning your chair or moving to the window changes your view. Keeping your phone in another room is the surest method even in a small home.
