## 📌 Key Takeaways

> **The short answer:** A walk in nature can genuinely restore attention and mood. The stress hormone **cortisol** fell most efficiently when people stayed in nature for **20–30 minutes**, and people who walked in a park improved more on an attention test than people who walked downtown. Rather than hitting an exact number of minutes each day, aim to spend a total of **120 minutes** or more in nature each week.

- 🌳 **Cortisol fell most efficiently at 20–30 minutes.** When 36 city dwellers spent time in a natural space of their choice over eight weeks, cortisol dropped about 21% more per hour at 20–30 minutes, and longer stays added little (**Frontiers in Psychology**, 2019).
- 🧠 **Walking in a park raised attention scores more.** People who walked in a park for about 50 minutes improved more on an attention test of repeating digits backward than people who walked downtown for the same time (**Psychological Science**, 2008).
- 🌀 **A 90-minute walk in nature reduced rumination.** People who walked in nature instead of along a busy road ruminated less, repeatedly dwelling on the same worries, and activity in a related brain region also fell (**PNAS**, 2015).
- 📅 **120 minutes a week became a benchmark.** In an analysis of about 20,000 adults in England, people who spent a total of 120 minutes or more a week in nature reported better health and well-being, and it didn't matter whether they got it in one go or spread it out (**Scientific Reports**, 2019).

### 💬 "Can a 15-minute lunchtime walk really clear my head?"

After a day of checking messenger notifications, concentrating in meetings, and writing reports, by afternoon you find yourself rereading the same sentence several times. Many people have felt how stepping outside for a short walk down a tree-lined street leaves their head a little lighter.

The original version of this post explained that feeling as "a 15-minute habit reboots the brain." The overall direction, that nature restores attention, fit the research. But when I looked up the original papers, several numbers and claims about walk length, houseplants, immunity, and sleep differed from the studies, so I've corrected them.

## Deep Dive: How does nature let a tired brain rest?

### 1. Why does a day in the city leave you so drained?

University of Michigan psychologist Stephen Kaplan proposed **attention restoration theory** (Kaplan, **Journal of Environmental Psychology**, 1995). The theory divides attention into two kinds.

- **Directed attention**: the effortful attention you use to focus on one thing, such as work or study, while suppressing distractions. It tires with long use.
- **Soft fascination**: stimuli like swaying leaves, running water, and clouds that draw attention without effort. The theory holds that directed attention can rest during these moments.

Cities are full of stimuli you have to tune out, such as traffic noise, advertising, and notifications, so they keep you using directed attention. Nature, by contrast, is rich in soft fascination, which, the theory says, gives effortful attention a chance to rest. The original post's "voluntary attention" and "involuntary attention" refer to these two concepts.

### 2. Does walking in nature really bring focus back?

It can. A University of Michigan team had participants do a task that wore out their attention, then walk once through an arboretum and once through a busy downtown area (Berman et al., **Psychological Science**, 2008).

- After walking in the arboretum, scores on an **attention test** of repeating digits backward rose clearly; after walking downtown, they stayed about the same.
- In a second experiment, simply looking at **photos** of nature improved the ability to control attention more than looking at city photos.

The original post described this study as "even walking the same 30 minutes improves memory and working memory by more than 20% on average." The actual walk was **about 50 minutes**, and the effect was found on an **attention test** rather than on memory in general. The studies also involved only a few dozen participants.

An analysis of 42 studies also found that after exposure to nature, some abilities such as **working memory**, **cognitive flexibility**, and **attentional control** improved, but not every cognitive ability did (Stevenson et al., **Journal of Toxicology and Environmental Health, Part B**, 2018).

### 3. How much did stress hormones fall, and how fast?

A University of Michigan team had 36 urban adults spend more than 10 minutes in a natural space of their choice at least three times a week for eight weeks, and measured salivary **cortisol** before and after (Hunter et al., **Frontiers in Psychology**, 2019).

- Cortisol naturally declines over the day, but time in nature lowered it by about **21%** per hour beyond that.
- The most efficient duration was **20–30 minutes**. Staying longer brought only a small additional drop.
- People who also walked saw a drop in salivary amylase, another stress marker.

I found no study directly supporting the original post's claim that "involuntary attention activates after 10–15 minutes of nature exposure and brain fatigue rapidly lifts." The duration confirmed by research is closer to 20–30 minutes than 15.

### 4. Does a nature walk help with rumination, too?

It did. A Stanford University team had 38 participants walk for 90 minutes either along a nature path or beside a busy road (Bratman et al., **PNAS**, 2015). Those who walked in nature showed less **rumination**, repeatedly dwelling on the same worries, and lower activity in a brain region linked to rumination (the subgenual prefrontal cortex). Those who walked beside the road showed no such change.

### 5. Do greenery outside the window or a plant on your desk help?

Somewhat. But a few parts of the original post need correcting.

- **Greenery outside the window**: A University of Melbourne team had students, midway through a boring attention task, look for **40 seconds** at a photo of either a flower-and-grass-covered roof or a concrete roof. Students who saw the green roof made fewer errors afterward (Lee et al., **Journal of Environmental Psychology**, 2015). The original post's "5-minute window gazing" fits this direction.
- **Office plants**: When plants were brought into offices in the UK and the Netherlands, employee productivity rose by about **15%** (Nieuwenhuis et al., **Journal of Experimental Psychology: Applied**, 2014). The original post's 15% figure matches this study.
- **I'm correcting the air-purification claim.** The claim that potted plants purify indoor air had weak support. When researchers recalculated results from many studies, matching the air-cleaning effect of ventilation would take 10 to 1,000 plants per square meter (Cummings & Waring, **Journal of Exposure Science and Environmental Epidemiology**, 2020). It's more accurate to say a plant's benefit comes from **looking at it**, not from the air.

### 6. Does it improve immunity and sleep, too?

I'm correcting two claims from the original post.

- **NK cells**: A Japanese team reported that when 12 men walked in a forest over a three-day, two-night trip, the activity of immune **NK cells** rose and the effect lasted about a week (Li, **Environmental Health and Preventive Medicine**, 2010). But the study was small and involved several days in a forest, so we can't say a 15-minute walk strengthens immunity.
- **The glymphatic system and gut health**: I found no study directly showing the chain the original post described, that "nature exposure improves sleep and activates the brain's waste-clearing system (the glymphatic system)" or that "lower stress hormones improve gut health," so I removed it.

### 7. Summary: what held up and what I corrected

**Step 1. Walk a green route for 20 minutes a day**
At lunch or on your way home, put your phone in your bag and walk for about 20 minutes along a tree-lined street or through a park. It doesn't have to be a big forest. The study participants also used nature they chose themselves, like nearby parks and yards.

**Step 2. When focus fades, look outside for 40 seconds**
On days you can't walk, briefly look at the trees or sky outside your window whenever your focus fades. Just 40 seconds of looking at greenery reduced errors afterward.

**Step 3. Reach a weekly total of 120 minutes**
Rather than keeping to an exact time each day, aim for a weekly total of 120 minutes. One long outing on the weekend or several shorter ones on weekdays worked about the same.

## Conclusion

Nature letting a tired brain rest turned out to be more than a feeling. After a walk in the park, attention came back; staying 20–30 minutes lowered stress hormones; and ruminating on worries eased. But there was no neat number like "15 minutes reboots the brain." Instead of straining to keep a set number of minutes each day, add up the time you spend in nature this week and aim for 120 minutes.

### 🙋 FAQ

#### Does it still work if I live in a city without a big park?

Yes. Participants in the cortisol study also used nature they chose themselves, such as nearby parks, yards, and tree-lined streets. There are experiments where simply looking at nature photos, or at a green roof for 40 seconds, improved attention. What matters is putting your phone down and paying attention to your surroundings, not the size of the space. Getting sunlight at the same time also helps your circadian clock, which I covered in the post on [sunlight and focus](/en/blog/sunlight-focus-and-brain-function/).

#### Can nature walks cure depression or anxiety disorders?

Nature walks are a lifestyle habit that reduces rumination and supports mood, but they cannot replace treatment for depression or anxiety disorders. If symptoms last more than two weeks or daily life becomes difficult, see a professional. Walking is a good habit to keep alongside treatment.
