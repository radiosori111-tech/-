## 📌 Key takeaways

> **The short answer:** Affirmations may fail not because your willpower is weak but because repeating **hard-to-believe statements** makes your mind argue back. In fact, when people with low self-esteem repeated a grand affirmation, they felt worse. What research supported instead was **self-talk that uses your own name**, **writing about the values that matter to you**, and **pairing your words with a small action**.

- 😔 **For some, affirmations backfired.** When people with low self-esteem repeated "I'm a lovable person," their **mood and self-evaluation got worse** than those who didn't (**Psychological Science**, 2009).
- 🗣️ **Using your name instead of "I" eased nerves.** People who talked to themselves using their own name or "you" before a speech felt less anxious and performed better than those who used "I" (**Journal of Personality and Social Psychology**, 2014).
- ✍️ **Writing about values lit up reward regions.** "Self-affirmation," reflecting on values that matter to you, activated brain regions tied to self-related processing and reward (**Social Cognitive and Affective Neuroscience**, 2016).
- 🙋 **"Be a voter" rather than "vote"?** One study found identity-based wording raised turnout, but a larger field experiment didn't replicate it (**PNAS**, 2011, 2016).

### 💬 "I said 'I can do it' every morning"

Most of us have tried shouting "I can do it!" every morning. A few weeks later we quietly stop, and life doesn't seem much different, which can feel deflating. When I first wrote this post, I put it down to "not the affirmation itself, but the way it reaches the brain," and recommended changing your identity first and writing affirmations in the present tense.

Looking at the research this time, the idea that "a big gap provokes pushback" was confirmed by experiments. On the other hand, the explanations that "present-tense, first-person affirmations sink in more deeply" and that "the reticular activating system filters for what matters to you" needed correcting.

## Deep Dive: how does the brain take in affirmations?

### 1. Can affirmations backfire?

What I wrote originally, that "when someone who believes they're timid repeats 'I'm full of confidence,' the brain treats it as information that clashes with reality," was confirmed by experiment. Joanne Wood's team at the University of Waterloo had participants focus on the statement "I'm a lovable person" for four minutes (Wood et al., **Psychological Science**, 2009).

- People with **low self-esteem** who repeated the statement ended up **in a worse mood** and rated themselves lower than those who didn't.
- People with high self-esteem felt slightly better, but the difference was small.

The researchers interpreted this as follows: repeating a statement too far from what you currently believe brings up a "that's not true" rebuttal along with it, which can actually reinforce negative thoughts. The key point is that affirmations can backfire for exactly the people who seem to need them most.

### 2. So what kind of words reach the brain?

In my original post I wrote that "present-tense, first-person statements imprint more strongly on the brain." I found no research directly confirming this, and there were results pointing the other way. Ethan Kross's team at the University of Michigan had participants prepare a speech on short notice, with one group talking to themselves using "I" and another using **their own name or "you"** (Kross et al., **Journal of Personality and Social Psychology**, 2014). Those who used their name felt less anxious and were rated as giving better speeches. It's the effect of seeing yourself from a little distance.

Another approach is **self-affirmation**. Rather than repeating "I've already succeeded," you pick a value that truly matters to you (family, honesty, learning, and so on) and write a few lines about why it matters. A University of Pennsylvania team showed that this kind of self-affirmation activates the **ventromedial prefrontal cortex**, involved in self-related processing, and the **ventral striatum**, involved in reward (Cascio et al., **Social Cognitive and Affective Neuroscience**, 2016). One experiment in US schools found that values writing narrowed achievement gaps (Cohen et al., **Science**, 2006), but later replication studies found smaller or mixed effects.

### 3. Is "change your identity first" based on research?

In my original post I wrote that "according to the habit research of Wendy Wood and James Clear, change happens on three levels: outcomes, processes, and identity." On checking, **Wendy Wood** is a psychologist who studies habits, and her work shows that habits become automatic through **repetition in the same context** more than through identity (Wood & Neal, **Psychological Review**, 2007). The "three levels" framework is an idea James Clear proposed in his book *Atomic Habits*, not an experimental finding.

There are experiments on identity wording. A Stanford University team reported that asking in a pre-election survey how important it was "to **be a voter**" rather than "to **vote**" led to higher actual turnout (Bryan et al., **PNAS**, 2011). But a larger field experiment with tens of thousands of people found no such effect (Gerber et al., **PNAS**, 2016). Identity wording is an interesting idea, but it's not yet a reliable method.

### 4. Does the reticular activating system filter for what you want?

In my original post I wrote that "the **reticular activating system** filters out what matters to you from all the information around you, so affirmations make related opportunities stand out." The reticular activating system is a network in the brainstem that mainly regulates **wakefulness (arousal)** (Moruzzi & Magoun, **Electroencephalography and Clinical Neurophysiology**, 1949). Attention networks in the frontal and parietal lobes play a bigger role in deciding what we pay attention to.

Still, the phenomenon of "noticing what you want more easily" is real. Of people focused on counting basketball passes, about half failed to see a person in a gorilla suit walk across the screen (Simons & Chabris, **Perception**, 1999). We see what we're aiming for more readily and easily miss the rest. So rather than "affirmations change the future," the accurate statement is "deciding what to look for makes it easier to see."

## The fix: three steps to affirmations that reach the brain

**Step 1. Scale it down to something believable**
Instead of a statement far from who you are now, like "I'm full of confidence," bring it down to what you can do today. If a "that's not true" pops up in your head, the statement is too big.

**Step 2. Use your name, and pair it with an action**
In the morning, say something like "Sam, you can start today with a 10-minute walk after lunch," using your own name. Then attach an action plan such as "If an excuse comes up, then I'll walk first." You can rework your own sentence right below.

**Step 3. Write down the evidence in the evening**
Before bed, write one line about what you actually did that day. "I walked for 10 minutes today" is evidence your brain finds easier to believe than "I'm someone who takes care of my body." On hard days, it also helps to pick one value that matters to you and write a few lines about why.

## Conclusion

A thousand affirmations may have failed to change your life not because your brain is stubborn, but because hard-to-believe statements triggered an inner rebuttal. What's closer to the research is saying what you'll do today using your own name, doing it, and leaving one line of evidence in the evening. When small actions pile up, rather than grand sentences, that's what turns "I'm this kind of person" into the truth.

### 🙋 Frequently asked questions

#### So should I stop doing affirmations altogether?

Not at all. They helped slightly for people with high self-esteem, and believable-sized statements have few downsides. But fantasizing that "I've already made it" can sap your energy, so picture the obstacles and a plan too. I covered this in detail with WOOP in [three ways to achieve your goals](/en/blog/three-ways-to-achieve-your-goals/).

#### Why use my name instead of "I" when I talk to myself?

The researchers explain that speaking to yourself by name or as "you" lets you step back and look at the situation as if advising a friend, so you're less swept up in emotion. Naming your feelings had a similar effect, which I covered in [why we repeat things we regret](/en/blog/if-you-keep-doing-things-you-regret-the-brain-acted-first/).
