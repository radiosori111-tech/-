## 📌 Key takeaways

> **The short answer:** The **aMCC** (anterior midcingulate cortex) is indeed a brain region tied to the will to push through hard things, but the claim that "doing things you don't want to do grows your aMCC" hasn't been confirmed in human experiments yet. The methods with clearer evidence for getting past day three are **regular exercise** and **"if X happens, then I will do Y" plans**.

- ⚡ **Stimulating the aMCC produced a will to persevere.** When a weak electrical current was applied to the aMCC of two patients awaiting epilepsy surgery, both described **a feeling that they would push through** whatever challenge came (**Neuron**, 2013).
- 🧓 **Older adults with youthful memory had a thicker aMCC.** "SuperAgers," people over 60 with the memory of someone in their 20s, had better-preserved cortex in regions including the aMCC (**Journal of Neuroscience**, 2016). But this was an observational study.
- 🏃 **Exercise boosted self-control elsewhere too.** People who did a two-month exercise program smoked, drank, and snacked less, and studied better (**British Journal of Health Psychology**, 2006).
- 📝 **Make plans specific.** "If X, then Y" plans had a medium-to-large effect on reaching goals across 94 studies (2006).

### 💬 "My resolutions end in three days. Is my willpower just weak?"

When a plan you set in the morning fizzles out before three days are up, it's easy to blame a weak-willed personality. When I first wrote this post, I offered **BDNF** and the **aMCC** as the answer: "push through the things you don't want to do and your aMCC grows, and uncomfortable jolts like cold showers wake up your aMCC too."

It's an idea you hear a lot on podcasts and YouTube, so I passed it along as is. But looking up the original papers this time, I found that confirmed findings and speculation were mixed together. I sort them out below.

## Deep Dive: how much has been confirmed about the aMCC and BDNF?

### 1. Is the aMCC really the "willpower region"?

The **aMCC** sits deep in the middle of the brain, in the front-middle part of the cingulate cortex. Researchers at Harvard Medical School and Northeastern University reviewed past studies and concluded that the aMCC acts as a central hub that pulls together information about body state, emotions, and goals to decide **how much energy to spend on hard tasks** (Touroutoglou et al., **Cortex**, 2020).

The most striking evidence comes from an observation by Josef Parvizi's team at Stanford University. When a weak electrical stimulus was applied to the aMCC of two patients undergoing tests before epilepsy surgery, both described feeling something like **"I know a storm is coming, but I'm going to get through it,"** along with bodily changes such as a pounding chest (Parvizi et al., **Neuron**, 2013). But with only two participants, it's hard to say the aMCC is "all there is" to willpower.

### 2. Was the SuperAgers' aMCC really different?

I confirmed against the original study what I wrote in my first post, that "SuperAgers have a more active aMCC than their peers." Adults over 60 whose memory tests matched those of people in their 20s had better-preserved **cortical thickness** in regions including the aMCC (Sun et al., **Journal of Neuroscience**, 2016). But because this study compared brain scans taken at a single point in time, we can't tell whether their aMCC grew thicker because they did lots of hard things.

> In my original post I wrote that "if you only seek comfort and avoid challenges, your aMCC shrinks, and if you get through things you don't want to do, it grows." This explanation has spread widely through popular talks, but I couldn't find an experiment that had people do unpleasant tasks and checked whether their aMCC grew. So I downgraded it to "a plausible hypothesis."

### 3. Do cold showers wake up the aMCC?

I couldn't find research showing that cold showers stimulate the aMCC. The study most often cited on cold water and brain chemicals is a Czech experiment in which adult men sat **immersed in 14°C water for an hour**, and their blood noradrenaline and dopamine rose sharply (Šrámek et al., **European Journal of Applied Physiology**, 2000). Those conditions are very different from a short cold shower, and the study didn't measure the aMCC at all. If cold showers feel refreshing, go ahead, but there's no evidence yet that they build willpower.

### 4. How are BDNF and willpower connected?

**BDNF** is a protein that helps nerve cells survive and connect, and exercise raises it most reliably. In a meta-analysis of 29 studies, even a single bout of exercise raised blood BDNF, and the rise was larger in people who exercised regularly (Szuhany et al., **Journal of Psychiatric Research**, 2015).

There's also direct evidence linking exercise to willpower. A team at Macquarie University in Australia had 24 inactive adults follow an exercise program for two months (Oaten & Cheng, **British Journal of Health Psychology**, 2006). Although they only did the exercise program, participants did better on a concentration task and reported **smoking, drinking, caffeine, and junk food going down**, along with better study habits and emotional control. Whether that's due to BDNF or the aMCC isn't known yet, but the conclusion of my original post, "build staying power through exercise," points the same way as this study.

## The fix: three steps to get past day three

**Step 1. Turn your resolution into "if X, then I will Y"**
Rather than "I should exercise," pair a situation with an action: "If I want to sit on the couch after work, then I will put on my running shoes first." In a meta-analysis of 94 studies, these **implementation intentions** had a medium-to-large effect on reaching goals (Gollwitzer & Sheeran, **Advances in Experimental Social Psychology**, 2006). **Step 2. Move hard enough to get out of breath, three times a week**
Three times a week, walk briskly or run for 20 to 30 minutes. Exercise raised BDNF, and people who exercised for two months improved their self-control even in areas unrelated to exercise.

**Step 3. Aim for 66 days**
How long it took a new behavior to become automatic ranged from 18 to 254 days, with a median of **66 days** (Lally et al., **European Journal of Social Psychology**, 2010). Falling off on day three isn't the end; missing a single day didn't materially affect habit formation. Just start again the next day. I covered the details in [the 66-day habit study](/en/articles/habit-66-days/).

## Conclusion

The aMCC is a fascinating brain region tied to the will to push through hard things, but "doing things you don't want to do grows the aMCC" is still a hypothesis. What has clearer evidence right now for getting past day three is **a specific "if X" plan** and **regular exercise**. Today, build one action sentence above, and move hard enough to get out of breath just three times this week.

### 🙋 Frequently asked questions

#### Does willpower run out the more you use it?

The "ego depletion" theory, that willpower gets used up like a muscle, was once famous, but when 23 labs in several countries repeated the experiment together, the effect barely appeared (Hagger et al., **Perspectives on Psychological Science**, 2016). Rather than blaming yourself for "running out" of willpower, it's better to change your plan and your environment. I covered how to set goals in more detail in [three ways to achieve your goals](/en/blog/three-ways-to-achieve-your-goals/).

#### Besides exercise, what raises BDNF?

Exercise has the most evidence, and learning, sleep, and diet are being studied too. On the other hand, chronic stress and sugary, fatty diets lowered BDNF in animal studies. I covered the causes in [four causes of low BDNF](/en/blog/what-causes-bdnf-deficiency-and-does-brain-health-improve/).
