## 📌 Key takeaways

> **The short answer:** Before a run, five minutes of a **warm-up that gets you moving**, with walking and light drills, beats long **static stretches**. Pre-exercise static stretching didn't reduce injuries, but a dynamic warm-up program did. There's no evidence, though, that "skipping the warm-up halves running's brain benefits."

- 🏃 **A dynamic warm-up reduced injuries.** When 1,892 young female soccer players did a 15- to 20-minute dynamic warm-up program before playing, their injury risk fell by about a third (**BMJ**, 2008).
- 🧘 **Pre-exercise static stretching didn't prevent injuries.** Comparing 1,538 military recruits over 12 weeks, lower-limb injury rates were the same whether or not they did static stretching before exercise (**Medicine & Science in Sports & Exercise**, 2000).
- ⏱️ **Long stretches sap strength briefly.** In a meta-analysis of 104 studies, strength dropped by a little over 5% on average right after static stretching, but holds of 45 seconds or less had almost no effect (**Scandinavian Journal of Medicine & Science in Sports**, 2013).
- 🧊 **Cool-downs did less than you'd think.** A post-exercise cool-down didn't do much for soreness or next-day recovery, but it helped prevent dizziness from stopping suddenly (**Sports Medicine**, 2018).

## Deep Dive: what's been confirmed about warm-ups and cool-downs?

### 1. Does skipping the warm-up really halve the brain benefits?

I found no study showing, as my original title claimed, that "skipping the warm-up halves the brain benefits," or that "BDNF only fully rises once the body is warmed up." That exercise raises **BDNF**, a fertilizer for the brain, is confirmed by many studies (Szuhany et al., **Journal of Psychiatric Research**, 2015), and it rose more at higher intensity (Ferris et al., **Medicine & Science in Sports & Exercise**, 2007), but there was no result showing the effect was halved without a warm-up.

> So I corrected the "halved" and "nearly half is decided" wording in the title and opening paragraph. The real reason a warm-up matters is not to protect brain benefits but to **run for a long time without getting hurt**. Running's brain benefits show up after months of steady practice, so not stopping because of injury is what ultimately protects the brain.

### 2. Which warm-ups reduced injuries?

A team at the Oslo Sports Trauma Research Center split 1,892 young female soccer players into two groups and had only one do a 15- to 20-minute warm-up program mixing running, jumping, balance, and strength moves (a forerunner of FIFA 11+) all season (Soligard et al., **BMJ**, 2008). That group's injury risk was about **a third** lower, and severe injuries fell by nearly half.

Warm-ups also helped performance. In a meta-analysis of 32 studies, warming up improved performance on about 79% of the measures examined (Fradkin et al., **Journal of Strength and Conditioning Research**, 2010). As body temperature and heart rate rise gradually, muscles and joints move more freely.

### 3. Should you do static stretching before a run?

What I wrote originally, that "right before running, dynamic stretching should come before static stretching," fits the research.

- An Australian military team split 1,538 recruits for 12 weeks and had only one group do static stretches of the leg muscles before exercise (Pope et al., **Medicine & Science in Sports & Exercise**, 2000). The two groups' lower-limb injury rates **didn't differ**.
- In a meta-analysis of 104 studies, strength dropped by about 5.4% and power by about 2% on average right after static stretching. But holds of **45 seconds or less** had almost no effect (Simic et al., **Scandinavian Journal of Medicine & Science in Sports**, 2013).

In other words, a short stretch before running is fine, but it won't prevent injuries, so if time is short, spend it on **a warm-up that gets you moving**.

### 4. Do cool-downs and stretching help with soreness and recovery?

In my original post I wrote that a cool-down "helps clear the fatigue products that build up in muscles," and that skipping it "could hurt your condition and sleep quality the next day." The research was a bit different.

- A Dutch and Australian team that reviewed cool-down studies found that cool-downs **did little to change** muscle soreness, next-day performance, or injury risk. Lactate returned to normal within an hour or two even without a cool-down (Van Hooren & Peake, **Sports Medicine**, 2018).
- The effect of post-exercise stretching on soreness was also very small, about 1 to 2 points on a 100-point scale (Herbert et al., **Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews**, 2011).

That doesn't make cool-downs useless. If you stop suddenly after running hard, blood can pool in your legs and your blood pressure can drop, causing **dizziness**, and walking for 3 to 5 minutes to stop gradually prevents this. Stretching after exercise is fine too if it feels good, but don't expect it to get rid of soreness.

## The fix: a five-minute routine before and after your run

**Step 1. Before the run: warm up by moving (5 minutes)**
Follow the routine below as is. Raise your effort only until you're slightly out of breath, then start your real pace. On cold days, take a little longer.

**Step 2. During the run: take the first few minutes slowly**
Even after warming up, run the first few minutes at a pace where you can hold a conversation. What matters more for preventing knee pain is not increasing your distance suddenly. I covered the details in [beginner runner knee pain](/en/blog/running-for-brain-health-beginner-knee-pain-and-the-wrong-shoes-for-your-arch/).

**Step 3. After the run: walk for 3 to 5 minutes as you stop**
Don't collapse the moment you cross the finish line; walk for 3 to 5 minutes to bring your heart rate and breathing down gradually. Light stretches for your calves and thighs afterward are fine if they feel good.

## Conclusion

The five minutes before and after a run aren't magic that saves your brain benefits from being "halved"; they're **a habit that lets you run for a long time without getting hurt**. Before running, warm up with walking and light drills rather than long stretches, and when you finish, don't stop dead; walk for a few minutes. Keeping it up steadily is what brings the real benefits running has for the brain.

### 🙋 Frequently asked questions

#### Does foam rolling help with warming up or recovery?

Some studies find foam rolling briefly increases range of motion and slightly reduces soreness after exercise, but the effects are small and short-lived. Use it if it feels good, but it can't replace a warm-up that gets you moving. For how to start running without overdoing it, see [how to start running](/en/blog/bdnf-just-10-minutes-of-running-changes-the-brain-how-to-start/).

#### Is it okay to run right after waking up?

Right after waking, your body is stiff and your temperature is low, so it's better to warm up a little longer than usual. Drink a glass of water and start slowly with walking. I covered how to choose running shoes in [running shoes for flat feet](/en/blog/flat-feet-high-arches-normal-arches-how-to-choose-running-shoes/).
