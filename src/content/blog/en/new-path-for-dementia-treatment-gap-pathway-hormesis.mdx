## 📌 Key Takeaways

> **The short answer:** **Hormesis** is when a small amount of harmful stress actually strengthens the body's defenses. Korean researchers announced that in dementia mice, quinolinic acid, known as a neurotoxin, boosted the cleanup ability of brain immune cells at **low concentrations**. But it was a mouse study, and the hormesis approach with the most evidence for everyday life in people is **regular exercise**.

- 🧹 **A brain-cleaner gene changed dementia risk.** People with a certain variant in TREM2, a gene active in the brain's immune cells, **microglia**, had about three times the risk of Alzheimer's disease (**NEJM**, 2013).
- 🧪 **A low-dose toxin was reported to aid cleanup.** KIST and Boston University researchers announced that giving dementia mice low concentrations of **quinolinic acid** raised microglia's cleanup ability and reduced amyloid (KIST announcement, 2025).
- 💊 **An amyloid-lowering drug slowed decline a little.** In 1,795 people with early Alzheimer's disease, lecanemab slowed cognitive decline by about 27% over 18 months (**NEJM**, 2023).
- 🏃 **Hormesis turned out to be delicate.** People who took vitamins C and E while exercising saw less of the improvement in insulin sensitivity that exercise brings (**PNAS**, 2009).

### 💬 "Can a poison really become medicine?"

I'd only known "a poison can become medicine" as a vague figure of speech. Then I saw news that researchers at KIST and Boston University School of Medicine had announced that a toxic substance in the brain, at low concentrations, actually boosts the cleanup ability of brain immune cells.

Reading it reminded me of running "Mikkumso" (Future Dream Hope Creation Lab), a creative program for children at a library. In a completely free environment with no constraints, the children actually lost direction and got distracted. But when I gave a task with a little tension, like "Read this book by next week and write a one-line review," children who grumbled at first would suddenly change their expression and focus. It felt similar, in that just the right amount of pressure drew out their strength.

This post originally introduced this research, calling it "the first concrete evidence that hormesis works in the brain's immune mechanisms." Checking again, I could only confirm the study's details through the institution's announcement and press coverage, and some parts, like "amyloid treatments failed every time," are no longer accurate. I rewrote it alongside research that can be checked.

## Deep Dive: The brain's cleaners and hormesis

### 1. Are microglia really linked to dementia?

They are. **Microglia** are immune cells living in the brain that engulf and clear dead cells and protein debris like **amyloid beta**. Strong evidence that these cells matter for dementia came from genetics.

An international team centered at UCL in the UK analyzed **TREM2**, a gene active mainly in microglia. People with a variant called R47H in this gene had about **three times** the risk of Alzheimer's disease (Guerreiro et al., **NEJM**, 2013). It's interpreted as meaning dementia risk rises when microglia can't do their job well. The original post's explanation that "microglia losing their function is one of the core problems in dementia" fits this research.

### 2. Did the toxin quinolinic acid really help the brain clean up?

**Quinolinic acid** is made as tryptophan breaks down, and at high concentrations it has been known as a **neurotoxin** that overexcites and damages neurons (Guillemin, **FEBS Journal**, 2012).

Researchers at KIST and Boston University School of Medicine announced that this quinolinic acid, at **low concentrations**, actually increases microglia's cleanup ability. According to KIST's announcement and press coverage, giving dementia model mice low-dose quinolinic acid reduced amyloid plaques in the brain and improved memory, and the researchers called this signaling flow the "GAP pathway." The study was reported to be published in 2025 in the journal Signal Transduction and Targeted Therapy.

However, I couldn't directly confirm the original paper's bibliographic details and specific figures. So the original post's statement that "within days, plaques shrank noticeably and memory improved to near-normal levels" is reported only as the institution's announcement. Above all, it's a **mouse experiment**, and quinolinic acid isn't a drug used in people. The researchers said they plan to find candidate compounds targeting this pathway and run preclinical testing.

### 3. Did amyloid treatments really fail every time?

The original post said "dementia drug development has focused on directly removing amyloid beta but has failed in clinical trials time after time." That was true for a long time, but things have changed somewhat.

In a phase 3 trial in 1,795 people with early Alzheimer's disease, the amyloid-lowering antibody **lecanemab** slowed cognitive decline by about **27%** compared with placebo over 18 months (van Dyck et al., **NEJM**, 2023). But there were side effects like brain swelling and microbleeds, and it didn't stop or reverse the disease.

So the amyloid-lowering approach has shown some effect too, and KIST's approach of reviving the brain's cleanup ability is more accurately seen as a new attempt in a different direction.

### 4. Does hormesis apply to daily life?

**Hormesis** is when a small amount of stress or toxic stimulus switches on cellular defenses and actually increases resilience. Neuroscientist Mark Mattson of the US National Institute on Aging explained that exercise, mild fasting and some plant compounds put mild stress on cells that switches on defense genes (Mattson, **Ageing Research Reviews**, 2008).

An experiment in people showed hormesis is more delicate than expected. A University of Jena team in Germany had young men exercise for four weeks, with one group also taking the antioxidants vitamin C and E (Ristow et al., **PNAS**, 2009).

- The exercise-only group improved **insulin sensitivity**, and cellular defense genes switched on.
- The group that also took antioxidants showed **less** of these changes.

The interpretation is that the reactive oxygen produced during exercise sends the body a signal to "adapt," and the antioxidants erased that signal too. The principle that right-sized stress helps is correct, but it doesn't mean eliminating that stress is always good, or that more is always better. The original post's claim that "fasting switches on autophagy" was mainly confirmed in animal studies and hasn't been directly confirmed in the human brain.

**Step 1. Make exercise your "right-sized stress"**
In people, the clearest confirmed form of hormesis is exercise. Move hard enough to get breathless for 30–40 minutes three times a week. Don't overdo it at first; raising the intensity little by little is how you keep it "just right."

**Step 2. Avoid high-dose antioxidants right after exercise**
Antioxidants in vegetables and fruit are fine, but taking high-dose vitamin C and E supplements every time you exercise can reduce exercise's adaptive effects. Unless you have a deficiency or a doctor recommends them, food is enough.

**Step 3. Give your brain a slightly hard new challenge**
Just as the library children focused when faced with a small task, give your brain new challenges that are neither too easy nor too hard. Learning a new instrument, language or sport are good examples.

## Conclusion

The principle of hormesis, that a poison can become medicine, is real. KIST researchers announced that in dementia mice, the neurotoxin quinolinic acid woke up the brain's cleaners at low concentrations, pointing to a new direction for dementia treatment. But it's still a mouse study, and in people hormesis proved more delicate than expected. The surest "right-sized stress" you can give yourself today is walking until you're breathless. I'll keep watching whether this research makes its way to people.

### 🙋 Frequently Asked Questions

#### Can taking quinolinic acid supplements or similar substances prevent dementia?

No. Quinolinic acid is a neurotoxin that damages neurons at high concentrations, and this result came from giving mice precisely controlled amounts. It's not a drug or supplement that can be used in people, and the researchers said they're still at the stage of finding new, safe candidate compounds.

#### What can I do now to help prevent dementia?

The Lancet Commission on dementia estimated that modifiable risk factors like high blood pressure, physical inactivity, smoking, heavy drinking and hearing loss are linked to about 40% of all dementia. Take care of exercise, blood pressure, quitting smoking and moderate drinking together. Research showing that late-start exercise also changed the brain is covered in [Can a Late-Start Exercise Habit Change Your Brain?](/en/blog/brain-health-plasticity-exercise-habits-compound-effect/).
