## 📌 Key Takeaways

> **The short answer:** For 1-minute high-intensity exercise, about **three times a day** showed a meaningful difference. When people who didn't exercise moved hard enough to get breathless for 1–2 minutes three times a day, their risk of death was **about 40%** lower. Start with short everyday bursts, like climbing stairs or dashing to the bus stop.

- ⏱️ **Three 1–2 minute bursts a day made a difference.** Tracking wrist-device data from about 25,000 people who didn't exercise for roughly seven years, those who did three short breathless bursts a day (**VILPA**) had about 40% lower risk of death than those who did none (**Nature Medicine**, 2022).
- 🚴 **One all-out minute matched 50 minutes for fitness.** People who did 10-minute workouts containing one minute of all-out sprinting for 12 weeks improved their **cardiorespiratory fitness** as much as those who exercised steadily for 50 minutes (**PLoS ONE**, 2016).
- 🪜 **Even 20-second stair climbs raised fitness.** Climbing stairs fast for 20 seconds three times, three days a week for six weeks, improved cardiorespiratory fitness (**Medicine & Science in Sports & Exercise**, 2017).
- 🧠 **The hippocampus grew in a walking study.** Evidence that the hippocampus grew by 2% came not from high-intensity exercise but from a study of walking three times a week for a year (**PNAS**, 2011).

This post originally summarized research suggesting that "short high-intensity exercise like a one-minute sprint works as well as or better than long walks." Rechecking the original papers this time, the overall direction was right, but one number and the evidence behind "it rewires the brain" needed correcting.

## Deep Dive: How much do short high-intensity bursts change the body and brain?

### 1. Can 1–2 breathless minutes a day really make a difference?

A University of Sydney team in Australia had 25,241 UK Biobank participants who said they **didn't exercise** wear wrist activity trackers for a week, then followed them for about seven years (Stamatakis et al., **Nature Medicine**, 2022).

- The team called everyday movement hard enough to leave you breathless for about 1–2 minutes, like climbing stairs, running for a bus or walking briskly with heavy groceries, **VILPA** (vigorous intermittent lifestyle physical activity).
- People who did VILPA about three times a day had 38–40% lower **all-cause mortality** and **cancer mortality**, and 48–49% lower **cardiovascular mortality**, than those who did none.

The numbers in the original post (about 38–40% lower all-cause mortality and up to 49% lower cardiovascular mortality) match the original paper. But this was an **observational study**, so it couldn't fully rule out that people who were healthier to begin with moved hard more often. Keep in mind it wasn't an experiment that assigned people to do VILPA.

### 2. Can one all-out minute match 50 minutes of exercise?

A McMaster University team in Canada divided 27 inactive men into three groups for 12 weeks (Gillen et al., **PLoS ONE**, 2016).

- One group did a **10-minute workout** three times a week with three 20-second all-out cycling sprints (one minute of all-out effort in total).
- Another group did **50 minutes of steady moderate** cycling three times a week.
- After 12 weeks, both groups improved **cardiorespiratory fitness** similarly, by about 19%, and insulin sensitivity improved similarly too. The group that didn't exercise didn't change.

The same team found that climbing stairs fast for 20 seconds three times, three days a week for six weeks, also improved cardiorespiratory fitness (Allison et al., **Medicine & Science in Sports & Exercise**, 2017). It's good news for busy people, but both studies had only a few dozen participants.

### 3. Do short high-intensity bursts raise BDNF more?

The original post said "six minutes of high-intensity exercise raised **BDNF** four to five times more than 60 minutes of light cycling." The original paper compared it with **90 minutes** of light cycling, not 60. This 12-person study measured BDNF in the blood, not memory or brain structure (Gibbons et al., **Journal of Physiology**, 2023). It's also covered in detail in [Does Exercise Help Anxiety?](/en/blog/neuroscientist-anxiety-move-exercise-brain-science-guide/).

The explanation that **lactate** raises BDNF comes from mouse experiments. Injecting mice with lactate increased BDNF in the hippocampus, and blocking lactate's action reduced the BDNF increase from exercise (El Hayek et al., **Journal of Neuroscience**, 2019). Whether the same process happens in the human brain hasn't been directly confirmed yet.

### 4. Does high-intensity exercise grow the hippocampus?

The original post said "the hippocampus shrinks 1–2% each year, but high-intensity stimulation reverses this." The numbers are right, but the study behind them wasn't about high-intensity exercise.

- When 120 older adults did **brisk walking** for 40 minutes three times a week for a year, the **hippocampus** grew by about 2%, while the stretching-only group's shrank by about 1.4% (Erickson et al., **PNAS**, 2011). The 1–2% yearly shrinkage with age also comes from this paper.
- I couldn't find a study showing that short high-intensity exercise alone grew the human hippocampus.

So for the hippocampus, combining **steady walking or aerobic exercise** with short bursts is closer to the evidence than doing short bursts alone. The roles of IGF-1, VEGF and nitric oxide mentioned in the original post also come mainly from animal studies.

### 5. Summary: What held up and what was corrected

| Original claim | What the research says | Type of evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 3–4 bouts of 1–2 minute VILPA a day cut death risk by 38–40% | The numbers are right. But it's observational, so it can't be called the cause | Observational study (Nat Med 2022) |
| Short high-intensity exercise rivals long exercise | True for fitness. A 10-minute workout with one all-out minute raised fitness as much as 50 minutes | Randomized trial (PLoS ONE 2016) |
| Six high-intensity minutes raise BDNF 4–5 times more than 60 minutes of cycling | The comparison was 90 minutes, and it was a 12-person study of blood BDNF only | Experiment (J Physiol 2023) |
| Lactate enters the brain and raises BDNF | Confirmed in mice; not yet directly confirmed in people | Animal study (J Neurosci 2019) |
| High-intensity stimulation reverses hippocampal shrinkage | Evidence for a growing hippocampus came from a year of brisk walking | Randomized trial (PNAS 2011) |

## Solution: 3 Steps to One-Minute Exercise Three Times a Day

**Step 1. Create 1–2 breathless minutes three times a day**
Rather than carving out separate time, attach it to things you already do. Taking stairs instead of the elevator, walking fast or running to the bus stop, and a one-minute spurt during a lunch walk all count as VILPA. Try the routine below as is.

**Step 2. Intensity only up to "hard to talk"**
It doesn't have to be 100% all-out. Being breathless enough that even a short conversation is hard is enough. If you have heart or joint disease or haven't exercised in a long time, talk with a doctor first, and always warm up before you start. Why warming up matters is covered in [The 5-Minute Warm-Up Before and After Running](/en/blog/skip-the-5-minute-warm-up-and-cool-down-and-you-halve-runnings-brain-benefits/).

**Step 3. Add steady walking or running three times a week**
Short high-intensity bursts helped fitness and survival, but the evidence for a growing hippocampus came from a year of steady walking. Adding 30–40 minutes of walking or light running three times a week to your one-minute bursts covers both kinds of evidence.

| 3. Steady walking | Walk briskly or run lightly | 3 times a week, 30–40 minutes | PNAS (2011) | A year of walking grew the hippocampus by about 2% |

## Conclusion

It's fine if you don't have time for one long workout. People who moved hard enough to get breathless for 1–2 minutes three times a day had lower risk of death, and a 10-minute workout with one all-out minute raised fitness as much as 50 minutes. But "one minute rewires the brain" ran ahead of the evidence. For the brain, especially the hippocampus, add steady walking. Start like I did, by splitting movement across before work, breaks and the commute home.

### 🙋 Frequently Asked Questions

#### Can I just do one-minute bursts and skip other exercise?

It's definitely better than doing nothing. But most evidence for the hippocampus and memory comes from studies of steady walking or aerobic exercise for months or more. Make one-minute bursts your baseline, and add a 30-minute walk on days you have time. Other ways to raise BDNF are in [How to Increase BDNF](/en/blog/how-to-increase-bdnf-exercise-food-reading-meditation-sleep-evidence-and-duration/).

#### Is high-intensity exercise OK at an older age?

The average age of VILPA study participants was in their 60s, and the activities were everyday ones like climbing stairs. But if you have heart disease, high blood pressure or joint problems, talk with a doctor first, and begin at a lower intensity, "a little out of breath." Stop right away if you have chest pain or dizziness.
