## 📌 Key takeaways

> **The short answer:** Research points to four **causes of low BDNF**: **chronic stress, lack of exercise, sugary and fatty diets, and aging**. But most BDNF in the blood sits inside platelets, so a single blood test can't tell you how much BDNF your brain has. Changing the habits behind it is more practical than measuring it.

- 😣 **Stress:** When rats were restrained so they couldn't move each day, BDNF fell in the **hippocampus**, the region that handles memory (**Journal of Neuroscience**, 1995). In people, those with depression had lower blood BDNF on average, but the gap shrank in larger studies (**Molecular Psychiatry**, 2014).
- 🏃 **Lack of exercise:** Even a single workout raised BDNF (meta-analysis, 2015), and **harder workouts** raised it more (2007). Older adults who walked for a year grew their hippocampus by 2% (**PNAS**, 2011).
- 🍩 **Sugary, fatty diets:** Rats fed lots of refined sugar and fat had less hippocampal BDNF and learned worse (**Neuroscience**, 2002). Few studies have tested this directly in people.
- 🧓 **Age:** Older people had lower blood BDNF, and lower BDNF went with a smaller hippocampus (**Journal of Neuroscience**, 2010).

## Deep Dive: why does BDNF drop?

### 1. Does stress really lower BDNF?

In animals, clearly. A team at the US National Institute of Mental Health restrained rats for hours a day and found that **BDNF gene expression in the hippocampus fell**; injecting the stress hormone **corticosterone** alone lowered it in a similar way (Smith et al., **Journal of Neuroscience**, 1995). What I wrote originally, that "the brain of a rat under long-term stress comes to resemble a BDNF-deficient brain," fits this line of research. Ronald Duman's team at Yale pulled these results together into the **neurotrophic hypothesis**: that a stress-driven drop in BDNF may be one cause of depression (Duman & Monteggia, **Biological Psychiatry**, 2006).

In people, the picture is more cautious. In a meta-analysis of 179 studies and more than 9,000 people, those with depression had lower blood BDNF on average, and it rose after antidepressant treatment. But **the more participants a study had, the smaller the gap**, suggesting the effect may have been overstated at first (Molendijk et al., **Molecular Psychiatry**, 2014).

### 2. Can a blood test tell me my BDNF level?

You might think, "Then why not just measure BDNF with a blood test?" But in human blood, BDNF is **mostly stored inside platelets** and is released when drawn blood clots (Fujimura et al., **Thrombosis and Haemostasis**, 2002). So serum BDNF readings swing widely with platelet counts and with how blood is drawn and stored.

Interestingly, one measurement found that during exercise, **70 to 80% of blood BDNF came from the brain** (Rasmussen et al., **Experimental Physiology**, 2009). So the blood number isn't unrelated to the brain, but there is still no test that lets you say from a single reading, "My brain is low on BDNF." In research, it's used to compare averages across groups of people.

### 3. How does lack of exercise lower BDNF?

Exercise is the most reliable way to raise BDNF, so not exercising means missing that chance.

- In a meta-analysis of 29 studies, **a single bout of exercise** raised blood BDNF, and the rise was bigger in people who exercised regularly (Szuhany et al., **Journal of Psychiatric Research**, 2015).
- When the same people cycled at different intensities, BDNF rose more at **the harder intensity** (Ferris et al., **Medicine & Science in Sports & Exercise**, 2007). In my original post I cited a Korean paper (Korean Journal of Sport Science, 2020) to say high-intensity exercise raises BDNF more, which points the same way.
- When 120 older adults walked three times a week for a year, **their hippocampus grew by about 2%**, while it shrank in the group that only stretched. How much it grew tracked changes in blood BDNF (Erickson et al., **PNAS**, 2011).

I also checked the Johns Hopkins study behind my original line that "BDNF affects heart muscle too." Mice without BDNF signaling couldn't contract and relax their hearts properly (Feng et al., **PNAS**, 2015). That was in mice, though.

### 4. Does eating a lot of sugar lower BDNF?

In animals, yes. Fernando Gómez-Pinilla's team at UCLA found that rats fed a diet high in refined sugar and fat for two months had **less BDNF in the hippocampus** and did worse at learning a maze (Molteni et al., **Neuroscience**, 2002). The same team reported that rats given fructose to drink had worse memory and brain insulin signaling, and that adding omega-3 limited the damage (Agrawal & Gomez-Pinilla, **Journal of Physiology**, 2012). But few studies have directly linked sugar intake to brain BDNF in people.

> In my original post I wrote that "a moderate amount of sugar releases serotonin and lifts your mood, but eating it too often depletes serotonin and causes depression." I couldn't find evidence for this. In fact, a meta-analysis of 31 studies found that eating carbohydrates (sugar) **did not improve mood**, and within an hour afterward people felt less alert and more tired (Mantantzis et al., **Neuroscience & Biobehavioral Reviews**, 2019). So I removed the serotonin-depletion story and kept only the fact that sugary, fatty diets lowered BDNF in animals.

### 5. Does BDNF drop as we age?

In a study of about 140 adults over 60, older people had lower blood BDNF, and lower BDNF went with **a smaller hippocampus** and poorer memory (Erickson et al., **Journal of Neuroscience**, 2010). You can't change your age, but as the one-year walking study shows, the hippocampus can still grow with exercise later in life, which is encouraging.

### 6. Summary: strength of evidence by cause

| Cause | What research showed | Type of evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Chronic stress | Hippocampal BDNF fell in rats. People with depression had lower blood BDNF on average, though the gap may be overstated | Animal study (1995), human meta-analysis (2014) |
| Lack of exercise | Exercise raised BDNF, more so at higher intensity. A year of walking enlarged the hippocampus | Human meta-analysis (2015), randomized trial (PNAS 2011) |
| Sugary, fatty diet | Rats had less hippocampal BDNF and learned worse | Animal studies (2002, 2012) |
| Age | Older people had lower blood BDNF and a smaller hippocampus | Human observational study (2010) |
| Can a blood test tell? | Most blood BDNF is in platelets, so it's hard to use for individual diagnosis | Human measurement studies (2002, 2009) |

## The fix: three steps to protect your BDNF

First, check which causes apply to you.

**Step 1. Stress: don't let unrelieved stress pile up**
You can't get rid of stress entirely, but you can keep it from dragging on. Pick your own way to unwind and put it in your schedule. The one with the most evidence behind it is exercise, the next step.

**Step 2. Exercise: hard enough to get out of breath, three times a week**
Three times a week, 20 to 30 minutes at a time, walk or run at a pace where you can talk but not sing. As you get used to it, raise the intensity little by little. Higher intensity raised BDNF more, but pushing too hard at the start rarely lasts.

**Step 3. Diet: start with sugary drinks**
The easiest place to start is swapping the **sugary drinks** you have every day for water or unsweetened tea. Cut back on refined sugar and fatty processed food, and eat more fish, vegetables, and nuts. I covered how setting meal times cuts down on snacking in [intermittent fasting results](/en/blog/intermittent-fasting-a-secret-for-those-who-gained-weight-over-the-holidays/).

## Conclusion

The causes of low BDNF turned out to be no different from the things that harm the body as a whole: long-term stress, an inactive life, sugary and fatty food, and age. A single blood test can't tell you your brain's BDNF, but you can start changing the habits behind it today. If even one item on the checklist above applied to you, start with that one step.

### 🙋 Frequently asked questions

#### Can I just take BDNF as a supplement or in food?

BDNF is a protein, so when you eat it, it's digested like any other protein. There's no evidence that eating it tops up BDNF in the brain. There is, however, research on foods and eating patterns that help the body make BDNF. I sorted the human and animal studies in [Which foods are rich in BDNF?](/en/blog/which-foods-are-rich-in-bdnf/).

#### If I stop exercising, does the BDNF I gained drop quickly?

Blood BDNF raised by a single workout usually returns to baseline shortly afterward. That's why **regular** exercise matters, and people who exercised regularly had bigger BDNF rises during exercise. I covered what to do and how much in [How to increase BDNF](/en/blog/how-to-increase-bdnf-exercise-food-reading-meditation-sleep-evidence-and-duration/).
