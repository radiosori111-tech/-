## 📌 Key takeaways

> **The short answer:** Inflammation in the body can genuinely lower mood and motivation. When healthy people were given an inflammatory response, they felt more depressed and **the brain's response to anticipated reward** fell. But blanket claims like "leaky gut is the root cause of brain inflammation" haven't been confirmed in people. What has evidence behind it is **enough sleep**, **a whole diet like the Mediterranean diet**, and **fermented foods**.

- 🔥 **Inflammation alone lowered motivation.** When 39 healthy adults received a tiny injection of a bacterial toxin (endotoxin) to trigger an inflammatory response, they felt more depressed and the **ventral striatum** responded less when they anticipated a reward (**Biological Psychiatry**, 2010).
- 🧠 **Glia make up about half the brain.** In the human brain, the numbers of neurons and non-neuronal cells were nearly 1 to 1 (**Journal of Comparative Neurology**, 2009). The original post's "80–90% of brain cells are glia" was an outdated estimate.
- 🥛 **Eating more fermented foods lowered inflammatory markers.** When 36 adults ate more fermented foods such as yogurt, kimchi, and kombucha for 10 weeks, their gut microbes became more diverse and 19 inflammation-related proteins, including **IL-6**, fell (**Cell**, 2021).
- 🥗 **Changing the whole diet reduced depressive symptoms.** When 67 people with depression followed a diet close to the Mediterranean diet for 12 weeks, 32% improved substantially, compared with 8% in the control group (**BMC Medicine**, 2017).

### 💬 "Why is my head still foggy after more than 8 hours of sleep on the weekend?"

Until recently, even after sleeping more than 8 hours on weekends, my head felt foggy and my stomach bloated for no clear reason, and I struggled with it for a long time. I assumed I was just run down or stressed.

Then I came across the terms "brain immunity" and "gut-brain axis," and in the original version of this post I wove glial cells, the glymphatic system, leaky gut, and glutamine into a single story. When I looked up the original papers again one by one, what had been confirmed in people was mixed with what remained at the level of animal experiments or hypotheses, and some references didn't match their sources. So I kept only what has been confirmed and corrected the rest.

## Deep Dive: how are inflammation and a foggy head connected?

### 1. Can inflammation in the body really change mood and motivation?

It can. A UCLA team split 39 healthy adults into two groups, injecting one with a very small dose of **endotoxin** (LPS), a component of bacterial cell walls, and the other with saline (Eisenberger et al., **Biological Psychiatry**, 2010).

- People who received endotoxin had more inflammatory substances in their bodies and reported feeling more **depressed**.
- When they saw a cue that they might win money, the response of the **ventral striatum**, which is involved in reward and motivation, was smaller.

In other words, just as everything feels like too much effort when you have a cold, inflammation in the body can signal the brain to lower motivation. But this was inflammation deliberately triggered in a short experiment; it doesn't mean all everyday chronic fatigue comes from inflammation.

### 2. What do glial cells do in the brain?

**Glial cells** are the cells that support neurons.

- **Microglia**: the brain's immune cells, which clear damaged cells and debris and prune synapses.
- **Astrocytes**: they wrap around blood vessels in the brain and take up the excitatory substance (glutamate) released by neurons to keep things in balance.
- **Oligodendrocytes**: they make the sheath (myelin) that wraps nerve fibers so signals travel quickly.

I'm correcting two things.

- **"80–90% of brain cells are glia" was wrong.** When researchers actually counted the cells of the human brain, there were about 86 billion neurons and a similar number of non-neuronal cells, nearly 1 to 1 (Azevedo et al., **Journal of Comparative Neurology**, 2009). A review of 150 years of cell-counting research also concluded that the claim of "ten times as many glia" had no basis (von Bartheld et al., 2016).
- **Splitting microglia into "attack (M1)" and "calming (M2)" types is an outdated framework.** A 2022 consensus paper by researchers from around the world stated that microglial states are far more varied, and dividing them into two doesn't match reality (Paolicelli et al., **Neuron**, 2022). So I removed the original post's explanations that "this nutrient switches microglia to M2."

### 3. Does sleep wash waste out of the brain?

It appeared so in mice, but it's still debated.

- A University of Rochester team reported that when mice fall asleep, the space between brain cells widens by about **60%**, and cerebrospinal fluid flows through, clearing waste such as **beta-amyloid** faster than during wakefulness (Xie et al., **Science**, 2013). This clearance route is called the **glymphatic system** (Iliff et al., 2012).
- In 2024, however, a UK team measured mice with a different method and reported that clearance from the brain actually **decreased** during sleep and anesthesia (Miao et al., **Nature Neuroscience**, 2024).

How much sleep helps clear the human brain hasn't been settled. But it is clear that too little sleep lowers focus and mood the next day, so getting enough sleep is still the first thing to take care of. I removed the original post's claim that "using your phone at night disrupts AQP4 and lets dementia-related waste build up," because I found no study confirming it in people.

### 4. Are the gut and brain really connected?

They are. The gut and brain exchange signals through the **vagus nerve**, immune substances, and substances made by gut microbes, which is called the **gut-brain axis** (Cryan & Dinan, **Nature Reviews Neuroscience**, 2012). But much of the evidence this review summarized comes from animal experiments.

The original post asserted that "leaky gut → endotoxin entry → irritation of the blood-brain barrier → runaway microglia" was the root cause of brain fog. Each step has been observed in animal experiments or some patient studies, but I found no human study showing that this whole chain causes a foggy head in ordinary people. I covered immune antibodies that protect the gut and brain in the post on [IgA antibodies and the gut-brain connection](/en/blog/iga-antibodies-gut-brain-connection/).

### 5. What foods help with inflammation and mood?

What human trials confirmed was the **whole diet**, more than any particular supplement.

- **Fermented foods**: When a Stanford University team had 36 adults eat more fermented foods (yogurt, kefir, kimchi, kombucha, and so on) for 10 weeks, their gut microbes became more diverse and 19 inflammation-related proteins, including **IL-6**, fell. In the group that ate more fiber, these changes weren't clear (Wastyk et al., **Cell**, 2021).
- **A diet close to the Mediterranean diet**: In the SMILES trial by an Australian team, when 67 people with depression followed a diet centered on vegetables, fruit, whole grains, fish, olive oil, and nuts for 12 weeks, **32%** improved substantially, more than in the group that received only social support (**8%**) (Jacka et al., **BMC Medicine**, 2017).
- **I'm correcting the glutamine claim.** The original post introduced glutamine as the key nutrient that "fundamentally blocks" leaky gut and brain inflammation. In human research, there is a trial in which patients with **irritable bowel syndrome** that developed after gut infection took glutamine for 8 weeks and their symptoms and gut permeability improved (Zhou et al., **Gut**, 2019). But that's a result in a specific patient group, and I found no evidence that it reduces brain fog or brain inflammation in healthy people.

**Step 1. Protect your sleep first**
Increase the number of nights you sleep 7 hours or more, even on weekdays. How much sleep helps clear the brain is still debated, but it's clear that too little sleep lowers focus and mood the next day.

**Step 2. Move your whole diet toward the Mediterranean diet**
Eat more vegetables, fruit, whole grains, fish, olive oil, and nuts, and fewer sugary drinks and processed foods. Changing your whole diet fits the human research better than any single supplement.

**Step 3. Add fermented foods once or twice a day**
Try adding fermented foods such as unsweetened yogurt, kimchi, or kefir once or twice a day. In a 10-week experiment where people ate more fermented foods, inflammatory markers fell.

## Conclusion

Even now I can't say for sure whether the foggy weekend mornings came from "brain immunity." But the direction the research pointed was clear. Inflammation in the body can lower mood and motivation, and the methods with human evidence for lowering inflammation weren't elaborate supplements but the basics of daily life: sleep, your whole diet, and fermented foods. Tonight, start by moving your bedtime 30 minutes earlier.

### 🙋 FAQ

#### Will glutamine supplements clear my head?

I found no evidence that glutamine reduces brain fog or brain inflammation in healthy people. There is only a trial in which symptoms improved in patients with irritable bowel syndrome that developed after a gut infection. If you have gut symptoms, see a doctor to find the cause before choosing a supplement. This is especially important if you have kidney or liver disease.

#### Are breathing and exercise related to inflammation too?

In many studies, regular moderate exercise was associated with lower inflammatory markers in the body, and breathing with slow exhalations helps ease tension. I covered breathing techniques in [the 5-minute breathing habit that calms anxiety and switches on focus](/en/blog/breath-is-your-brains-switch/).
