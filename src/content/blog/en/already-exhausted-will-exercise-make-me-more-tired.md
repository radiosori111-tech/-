On a day spent standing at work and battling stress, what do you do on your break or after work? Most of us probably flop onto the sofa or a chair and rest. At work, I too used to wait for mealtime and then rush to lie down and nap with the time left after eating. One book changed me, so that after a meal I run, even just 1 km, instead of sleeping.

It's the brain-science bestseller *Spark* by Dr. John Ratey of Harvard Medical School.

> "The more stressed and tired you are,
> the more you should walk or run for 15 minutes."

## Resting still doesn't relieve the brain's fatigue

We need to understand what the fatigue we feel really is. The stress of standing at work all day isn't simple muscle fatigue; it's <strong>"neurological fatigue."</strong>

<strong>Under work stress, the brain pumps out the stress hormone cortisol</strong>.

If you only sit still and rest, this cortisol keeps circulating in the blood, <strong>wearing away brain cells (especially in the hippocampus, which handles memory)</strong> and causing <strong>chronic fatigue</strong>.

## Three brain-science miracles from a 15-minute run on your break

Dr. Ratey stresses that light aerobic exercise triggers the following biological changes in the brain.

1. <strong>An immediate drop in the stress hormone (cortisol):</strong> <strong>light running gets stagnant blood flowing, rapidly lowering cortisol levels in the brain</strong>, and increases antioxidant enzymes to <strong>protect brain cells</strong>.
2. <strong>Release of BDNF, fertilizer for the brain:</strong> when your heart rate rises, brain-derived neurotrophic factor (**BDNF**) is released. It's a "miracle protein" that repairs damaged brain cells and recharges the brain's energy.
3. <strong>The happiness-hormone switch turns on:</strong> fifteen minutes of brisk movement immediately releases **serotonin** (mood regulation) and **dopamine** (motivation), <strong>switching off the brain's overload and calming the parasympathetic nervous system</strong>.

I've found that running even a short 0.5 km or 1.5 km leaves my body feeling refreshed in a way that a quick nap lying down doesn't. Wondering whether it was just a coincidence, I've now been doing short runs for three days, from September 15 to September 17.

## Won't exercising before work make me more tired? (How to do it right)

To give you the conclusion first: <strong><em>done at the right intensity, exercise actually lowers fatigue and brings the brain's focus to its peak</em></strong>.

<!-- related-link -->
When you're worn out, flipping the brain's switches works better than pushing through on willpower. The how-to continues in [**burnout and the brain's switches**](/en/blog/burnout-the-brain-needs-a-switch-not-persuasion/).

I'm not telling you to do high-intensity intervals until you collapse.

(Recommended intensity: a "light jog (Zone 2 pace)" or "brisk walk" at which you can still talk to the person next to you or breathe through your nose.)

This level of intensity <strong>floods the brain with oxygen and nutrients without building up lactate (a fatigue byproduct)</strong>. It becomes <strong>the perfect "brain reset" button for the work ahead</strong>.

<strong>Don't slump on your break, looking at your phone because you're tired.</strong>
<strong>Change into comfortable sneakers right now and walk outside for just 15 minutes.</strong>
<strong>Your brain will feel refreshed first, and it will make the real energy to carry you through the rest of the day.</strong>

> "Running is a <strong>natural vaccine</strong> that <strong>detoxifies</strong> the poison called stress."
