## 📌 Key takeaways

> **The short answer:** Done lightly, it actually leaves you less tired. Keeping up light exercise at a pace where you can still talk reduced fatigue and raised energy, and just 10 minutes of running woke up the prefrontal cortex, which handles focus. On tiring days, what to avoid isn't exercise itself but high intensity, which sharply raises stress hormones.

- 🏃 **Light exercise reduced fatigue.** When young adults who felt tired for no clear reason did light exercise for six weeks, they **felt less fatigued and more energetic**, and fatigue fell most in the low-intensity group (**Psychotherapy and Psychosomatics**, 2008).
- 🪑 **If you sit for long periods, "split walking" worked well.** On days when people walked 5 minutes every hour, they had better **energy and mood** all day and felt less fatigue in the afternoon (**International Journal of Behavioral Nutrition and Physical Activity**, 2016).
- 🧠 **The brain responds within 10 minutes.** After 10 minutes of moderate running, mood improved, people did better on a test of focus, and the **prefrontal cortex** was more active (**Scientific Reports**, 2021).
- ⚖️ **Intensity is the key.** The stress hormone **cortisol** barely rose with exercise at moderate intensity or below, but rose sharply at high intensity (**Journal of Endocrinological Investigation**, 2008). On tiring days, **light** is the answer.

### 💬 "If I'm already worn out, won't exercise wear me out more?"

On days spent on your feet and under stress, you flop onto the sofa or a chair during breaks and after work. At the office, I used to spend the time left after lunch lying down, trying to nap. Then I read *Spark* by John Ratey, a psychiatrist at Harvard Medical School, and was moved by the idea that the more stressed and tired you are, the more you should take a short walk or run.

So instead of lying down after lunch, I tried running short distances, 0.5 km or 1.5 km. I felt more refreshed than after a quick nap. Wondering if it was a coincidence, I kept it up for three days starting September 15, and now I run regularly. This post checks how far that feeling holds up in research.

## Deep Dive: what does exercise do for the brain on a tiring day?

### 1. What fatigue really is: the brain tires before the body

The fatigue of a day on your feet dealing with people is not just about muscles. When tension and focus go on and on, the brain keeps its **stress response** switched on, and the longer that lasts, the more we feel our mind is heavy before our body is. That's why lying down sometimes doesn't leave you refreshed.

My original post said "if you just rest, cortisol corrodes the hippocampus," and I want to correct that. Research linking long-term stress and high stress hormones to changes in the hippocampus is mostly about **animal studies or chronic stress lasting months or more**. A day of rest does not damage your brain. How you rest, though, can change **the quality of recovery**.

### 2. Light exercise reduced fatigue

Patrick O'Connor's team at the University of Georgia divided young adults who often felt tired for no clear reason into three groups (Puetz, Flowers & O'Connor, **Psychotherapy and Psychosomatics**, 2008). For six weeks, one group did **low-intensity** and one **moderate-intensity** cycling for 20 minutes three times a week, and one group didn't exercise.

- Both exercise groups **gained energy**.
- **Fatigue** fell in both groups, and **more so in the low-intensity group**.

The same team had earlier pooled more than 70 studies, finding that regular exercise increases energy and reduces feelings of fatigue (Puetz, O'Connor & Dishman, **Psychological Bulletin**, 2006). In other words, **the more tired you are, the more it helps to move gently rather than hard**.

### 3. Five minutes of walking between stretches of sitting

A team at the University of Colorado had adults who sit a lot, as in office work, spend days in three different ways (Bergouignan et al., **International Journal of Behavioral Nutrition and Physical Activity**, 2016).

- A day sitting for six hours straight
- A day with a 30-minute walk in the morning, then sitting
- A day of sitting broken up by **5 minutes of walking every hour**

On the days with 5 minutes of walking every hour, people had better **energy and mood** all day and felt less **fatigue** in the afternoon. A single block of exercise isn't the only answer; **short bursts of movement between tasks** also wake the brain up.

### 4. After a 10-minute run, the prefrontal cortex woke up

Hideaki Soya's team at the University of Tsukuba in Japan had healthy adults run for 10 minutes at moderate intensity and then measured blood flow in the brain (Damrongthai et al., **Scientific Reports**, 2021). After running, **mood** improved, people completed the attention-demanding **Stroop task** faster, and **both sides of the prefrontal cortex**, which plans and judges, were more active. What my original post called a "**brain reset**" is closest to this change in research terms.

Exercise also briefly raises **BDNF**, often called the brain's fertilizer, in the blood, and a meta-analysis found that regular exercise raises resting levels too (Szuhany et al., **Journal of Psychiatric Research**, 2015). BDNF, however, is less a "miracle protein that instantly repairs damaged brain cells" than **a substance that helps neurons grow and connect on top of regular exercise**.

### 5. Why intensity matters: cortisol and lactate

Anthony Hackney's team at the University of North Carolina had the same people exercise for 30 minutes at different intensities and measured blood **cortisol** (Hill et al., **Journal of Endocrinological Investigation**, 2008). At a light 40% of maximal oxygen uptake, cortisol actually dipped slightly; at 60% and 80% it rose sharply. Exercising until you're gasping on an exhausted day adds one more stress to the body.

My original post also called **lactate** a "fatigue substance," which I want to correct as well. Exercise physiology today sees lactate not as a cause of fatigue but as **a fuel and signal** used by muscle, heart and brain alike (Brooks, **Cell Metabolism**, 2018). Light exercise is good not because it avoids lactate, but because it is **an intensity that wakes the brain without straining the body**.

## The plan: a 15-minute 3-step plan for office workers

**Step 1. Intensity: only as hard as you can talk**
**Easy walking or jogging** where you can still talk to someone, or breathe through your nose, is enough. If you get too breathless to talk, switch to walking. Not raising the intensity on tiring days is key to recovery.

**Step 2. Time: 10–15 minutes on a break, or 5 minutes every hour**
Try walking or jogging gently for 10–15 minutes of the time left after lunch. On days you can't get out for long, it's fine to get up **every hour for 5 minutes** and walk the stairs or the hallway. Instead of slumping over your phone, start by changing into comfortable shoes.

**Step 3. Duration: aim for six weeks**
In the experiment where fatigue fell, people kept going three times a week for **six weeks**. Don't stop at a day or two of feeling refreshed; set six weeks as one goal and keep a record. I keep going by logging every run in a running app.

## Conclusion

Exercise on a tiring day wears us out more **when the intensity is high**. Move lightly, briefly and regularly, and, like the people in these studies, your fatigue goes down and your energy goes up. Just 10 minutes of easy running changed mood and focus and woke up the prefrontal cortex, which plans and judges. So on your next break, swap your phone for comfortable shoes and walk for 15 minutes. **Movement is the nearest pause you can give a tired brain.**

### 🙋 Frequently asked questions

#### Should I move even on days I'm truly exhausted?

No. If you're tired from lack of sleep, **sleep comes first**. What exercise eases is the fatigue that builds from long sitting and accumulated stress; it can't replace sleep. And if fatigue lasts more than a few weeks despite rest, or comes with fever, weight loss or shortness of breath, see a doctor before turning to exercise.

#### Is it OK to run right after lunch?

Walking gently is fine. Running hard right after a meal, though, easily brings on a side stitch or stomach discomfort. After eating, wait 15 to 30 minutes, **start with walking**, and move on to an easy jog if you feel comfortable. If you're new to running, read [Why running is good for your brain, and how to start as a beginner](/en/blog/bdnf-just-10-minutes-of-running-changes-the-brain-how-to-start/) too.
