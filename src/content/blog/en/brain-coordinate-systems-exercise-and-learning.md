The human brain <strong>processes the world in two coordinate systems at once</strong>. One is <strong>allocentric, looking down on the structure of the world from outside</strong>; the other is <strong>egocentric, always taking "my body" as its reference point</strong>. What I finally realized after years of floundering at the gym came down to these two coordinate systems. Back when I chased only the numbers on the weights, I was using the wrong muscles.

## The real reason gym beginners get hurt: the Mind-Muscle Connection

When you first start working out, two things catch your eye: the numbers on the plates of the barbell, and your reflection in the mirror. That was true for me, too. <strong>The day I set a goal of a 60 kg hip thrust</strong>, my brain was focused entirely on "pushing the barbell up." As a result, my knees did the work instead of my glutes, and in the third month I went to the hospital with pelvic pain.

Only later did I realize this wasn't just a matter of form. From a brain-science perspective, <strong>a number on the weights is a thoroughly allocentric goal.</strong> Allocentric here means <strong>taking only the objective structure of the world as the reference, independent of my body</strong>. Like a coordinate on a map, the fact that "the barbell weighs 60 kg" doesn't change no matter what state my body is in. When the brain fixates only on this external goal, <strong>which muscles are actually contracting inside my body, and how, gets pushed aside.</strong>

<strong>The egocentric approach, by contrast, takes my body as the origin.</strong> Internal sensations such as "how much are my glutes stretching right now?" or "aren't my knees working harder than my glutes?" come from this coordinate system. <strong>The brain's hippocampus maintains an allocentric model of the external world, while the somatosensory cortex working alongside it processes egocentric information from inside the body in real time.</strong> The somatosensory cortex is the brain region that processes sensory signals coming up from the skin, muscles, and joints. Put simply, it's like a map that tells the brain "what state my body is in right now."

The method I tried after my injury was simple: <strong>lightly tapping the area I was about to work before starting a set.</strong> Rubbing your glutes with both hands for about 30 seconds before a glute exercise engraves an address in the brain's somatosensory cortex: "this is where signals will be focused from now on." This is called the <strong>Mind-Muscle Connection</strong>, <strong>a training technique that deliberately concentrates nerve signals on a specific muscle to raise exercise efficiency and muscle growth</strong>. After I started using it, the glute activation I felt doing hip thrusts on the bench changed dramatically, and my knee pain disappeared.

Another shift that made a big difference was <strong>changing my perspective from "pushing the barbell" to "pushing the earth away."</strong> If you think of a squat as "lifting a heavy barbell," the brain takes the simplest route and piles the load onto the knees and lower back. But the moment you imagine "pushing the earth away with the soles of my feet to stand my body upright," the brain recruits the whole chain of lower-body muscles in balance. This difference is directly tied to **proprioception**, the sensory system in which receptors in muscles, tendons, and joints report the position and movement of your body to the brain in real time. Put simply, it's thanks to this sense that you know where your arm is even with your eyes closed.

- **Tap the target muscle before a set:** marks an "address" in the somatosensory cortex and improves the efficiency of nerve signaling
- **Cut out visuals and focus on internal sensation:** instead of the mirror or barbell, immerse your brain fully in the feeling of the muscle contracting and relaxing
- **Switch your cues to egocentric language:** changing the command from "push the barbell" to "stand your body up" changes how the prime movers are recruited

<strong>In short:</strong> many exercise injuries come from focusing only on an allocentric goal (weight) and losing the egocentric internal sense, and Mind-Muscle Connection training is the key to linking these two coordinate systems.

## Learning sticks when you do it with your body: the neuroscience of embodied learning

When I applied the principle I learned from exercise to studying, honestly, the result surprised me. <strong>Reading text with your eyes essentially locks the brain into a fully allocentric, passive mode.</strong> You're just <strong>"looking on" at the world's information from an outside observer's point of view</strong>, so the knowledge evaporates easily. But the moment you connect that knowledge to your body's senses, <strong>the brain classifies it as "important information linked to survival" and moves it into long-term memory</strong>.

This is <strong>the core of embodied learning</strong>. Embodied learning is <strong>a method that integrates the body's movement and senses directly into the cognitive process to improve learning efficiency</strong>. It isn't simply advice to "use your body while studying"; it's a neuroscientific strategy that activates the link between the **hippocampus** and the motor cortex. When I tried it myself, just reading concepts aloud while writing them by hand made a big difference in how much I remembered the next day. When sight, hearing, and touch are stimulated together, the brain processes the same information redundantly through three routes.

The brain's hippocampus originally evolved for spatial navigation. <strong>The hippocampus</strong> is <strong>a core brain structure for forming memories and spatial cognition</strong>, the gateway new information must pass through to become long-term memory. Interestingly, <strong>the hippocampus becomes active when we walk around a room or move through space. That's why "memorizing while moving through space"</strong> is not just a folk remedy. When you actually walk around a room repeating concepts, the hippocampus's **grid cells** bind physical spatial coordinates and memory information together as they store them ([Source: Hankook Ilbo](https://www.hankookilbo.com/news/article/A2026093009590001528)).

There's one more method that, in my experience, had a different kind of effect: explaining what you've learned to someone else. <strong>When you switch coordinates from student to teacher and speak aloud as if explaining, the brain doesn't just "store" knowledge but "reconstructs" it.</strong> As the egocentric view shifts to the other person's point of view, <strong>it becomes clear what you know and what you don't</strong>. Structurally, this is the same as switching your perspective in exercise from "push the barbell" to "stand your body up." The very act of moving the origin of the coordinates creates new processing pathways in the brain.

In this context, the risk of learning only through digital devices becomes clearer. <strong>Learning through a screen keeps the brain firmly in a passive, allocentric view and leaves less room for egocentric processing.</strong> This is also why <strong>pausing a video midway and predicting "what comes next?"</strong> is effective. The moment prediction begins, the brain <strong>switches from a passive allocentric mode to an active egocentric mode</strong>. The same principle explains why training children to find their way with a paper map instead of navigation is known to matter for brain development. Rotating information from a map (allocentric) to match your body's current sense of direction (egocentric) is the best exercise for stimulating the hippocampus and grid cells at the same time ([Source: **Nature Reviews Neuroscience**](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41583-021-00477-z)).

<strong>In short:</strong> reading text only with your eyes is merely "looking on" at allocentric knowledge, and embodied learning, which moves the body and shifts perspective, is the key to stimulating the hippocampus and turning knowledge into long-term memory.

## Frequently asked questions

### Does the Mind-Muscle Connection actually help muscle growth?

Yes, it's not just a psychological effect. Stimulating the target muscle before exercise to mark an "address" in the somatosensory cortex actually improves the efficiency of nerve signals to that muscle. When I applied it myself, the sensation of my chest muscles contracting and relaxing became much clearer at the same weight, and my risk of injury dropped.

### Is it bad to exercise while watching yourself in the mirror?

A mirror is useful early on for correcting form. The problem is relying on it too much. If you focus only on third-person visual information (allocentric), it's easy to lose the body's internal proprioception (egocentric). Once your form is reasonably established, it's more effective in the long run to also practice exercising with only your internal sense, without a mirror.

### Does walking around while memorizing really work?

It's more than a difference in habit. <strong>The hippocampus originally evolved for spatial navigation, so when your body moves through space, its memory-forming function is activated too</strong>. When you walk around a room repeating concepts, the hippocampus's grid cells store spatial coordinates and memory information together, so you can recall them more vividly later.

### Can AI have this egocentric coordinate system?

Not at present. When AI processes satellite maps, lidar data, or 3D scans of cities, these are all allocentric models. Even if a self-driving system appears to process egocentric information with cameras and sensors, there is no origin from which a bodily sensation like "my left side feels cool" or "I'd rather avoid this direction" could arise. Only humans, with living bodies, can move between the two coordinate systems in real time.

### Are there books or resources that connect exercise and learning through neuroscience?

Research on allocentric and egocentric coordinate systems and the role of the hippocampus appears regularly in neuroscience journals such as **Nature Reviews Neuroscience**. In Korea, the Hankook Ilbo has run a feature series summarizing examples of neuroscience applied to learning. If you're more interested in practical application than academic background, it helps to search the field of embodied cognition, which deals with the relationship between movement and cognitive function.

## Conclusion

Looking back, the day my pelvis gave out at the gym was actually lucky. Without that experience, I would have kept watching the numbers and using the wrong muscles. <strong>Shifting your gaze from allocentric goals (weight, the mirror) to egocentric sensations (muscle contraction, the position of your body)</strong> isn't just a technique. It changes the very way the brain processes the world.

The same goes for studying. Knowledge learned only in front of a screen stops at looking on at allocentric information. For it to become knowledge you can actually use, you need the experience of rotating it again with your own body as the origin: <strong>walking, speaking, writing by hand, and moving into another person's point of view.</strong> The more this builds up, the more flexibility of thought you gain to move freely between perspectives whatever problem you face. Today, while you exercise or study, try at least once to take your eyes off the numbers and the screen and listen to the sensations inside your body.
