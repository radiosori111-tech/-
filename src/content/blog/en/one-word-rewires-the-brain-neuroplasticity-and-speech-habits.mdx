## 📌 Key Takeaways

> **The short answer:** Using **your own name** instead of "I" in self-talk made people less swept up in emotion. Talking to themselves by name cut the brain's emotional response **within a second**, and brain areas used for effortful control didn't work harder. In hard moments, talk to yourself by name, and say what you want to remember out loud.

- 🪞 **Using your name cut emotional reactions within a second.** People who talked to themselves by name instead of "I" while viewing unpleasant images showed a drop in emotional brain-wave responses within a second (**Scientific Reports**, 2017).
- 🧠 **The effortful-control brain didn't work harder.** In the same study's fMRI experiment, talking by name reduced activity in the **medial prefrontal cortex**, used when dwelling on oneself, while areas used to suppress emotion by willpower didn't work harder (**Scientific Reports**, 2017).
- 🗣️ **Words read aloud were remembered better.** Many experiments confirmed the **production effect**: words read aloud from a list were remembered better than words read silently from the same list (**JEP: Learning, Memory, and Cognition**, 2010).
- 🔁 **Repeated actions become habit circuits.** When the same action is repeated, the brain's **basal ganglia** shift toward handling it automatically (**Nature Reviews Neuroscience**, 2006).

### 💬 "'Ugh, I blew it again.' Can those words really change the brain?"

"Ugh, I blew it again." "Why am I always like this?" There are things we mutter to ourselves without thinking, or repeat like a verbal tic. This post originally summarized how such speech habits affect the brain and how to change them.

Looking again, the original post had wrong citations. It reported the title and conclusion of a third-person self-talk study backwards, gave the wrong title for a habit research paper, and cited an unverifiable "Neuron review." It also had unsupported statements like "a single negative word stimulates the amygdala and raises cortisol." This time I tracked down the original papers one by one and corrected them.

## Deep Dive: How does self-talk work in the brain?

### 1. Does talking to yourself by name really calm emotions?

It did, and very quickly. A team from Michigan State University and the University of Michigan ran two experiments (Moser et al., **Scientific Reports**, 2017).

- In the first, participants viewed unpleasant images while talking to themselves with "I," as in "why do I feel this way seeing this," or with **their own name**, as in "why does Alex feel this way." Measuring brain waves, the wave reflecting emotional response dropped **within a second** when they used their name.
- In the second, they recalled painful memories with the same kinds of self-talk during fMRI. Using their name reduced activity in the **medial prefrontal cortex**, used when dwelling on oneself.

The original post introduced this study as "third-person self-talk activates the frontal lobe," but the actual conclusion was closer to the opposite. The **frontoparietal control network**, used to force emotions down, didn't work harder. The researchers described it as a way to regulate emotion without effort. The original's method of saying "(my name), you're flustered right now" fits this study well.

### 2. Does talking to yourself by name change actual behavior?

Ethan Kross's team, from the same research group, had participants suddenly prepare a speech in front of others, with one group talking to themselves with "I" and the other with their own name or "you." Those who used their name felt less anxious and were judged to give better speeches (Kross et al., **Journal of Personality and Social Psychology**, 2014). This study is also covered in [Why Affirmations Fail Even After 1,000 Times](/en/blog/why-affirmations-fail-even-after-1000-times-brain-science/).

### 3. Does saying things out loud etch them deeper?

The original post's explanation of the **production effect** is right when it comes to memory. A University of Waterloo team in Canada had participants read some words in a list aloud and the rest silently (MacLeod et al., **JEP: Learning, Memory, and Cognition**, 2010).

- Across many experiments, **words read aloud** were remembered better than words read silently.
- The researchers explained that words spoken aloud leave a distinctive trace of "something I said," making them easier to pick out later.

But the original claim that "the brain takes what you say aloud as something that actually happened" isn't what this research showed. The production effect is a memory result, and it appeared mainly when comparing spoken and unspoken words within the same list. So it's worth using to read aloud what you want to memorize, but it's hard to use as evidence that declaring something aloud changes reality.

### 4. Do repeated speech habits really carve a path in the brain?

The original's quote, "neurons that fire together wire together," summarizes an idea Canadian psychologist **Donald Hebb** proposed in a 1949 book (Hebb, *The Organization of Behavior*, 1949). And the growing role of the **basal ganglia** as repeated actions become habits is well documented in animal studies (Yin & Knowlton, **Nature Reviews Neuroscience**, 2006). The original post gave this paper's title as "Mechanisms of habit formation and neuroplasticity," but the actual title is "The role of the basal ganglia in habit formation."

So the explanation that repeating the same words in the same situation makes them pop out more automatically is right in principle. But I couldn't find a study directly measuring the brain change a single speech habit leaves, like an "eight-lane highway."

### 5. Does "I'm dying here" stimulate the amygdala and cortisol?

The original post said "if you constantly say 'I'm dying here,' the amygdala sounds an alarm, cortisol rises and neurons are damaged." I couldn't find research directly confirming this process in people. It's true that prolonged stress is bad for the body and brain, but there's no basis for asserting that a habitual phrase triggers that kind of stress response. So I removed this part.

**Step 1. In hard moments, use your name instead of "I"**
When you slip up or feel nervous, start with "Alex, right now…" instead of "Why do I…" You can do it silently in your head. In the studies, it was silent self-talk too.

**Step 2. End sentences with a question or next action**
Instead of ending with "I blew it," end with a question or next action, like "What should we change next time?" or "What was the first line?" It's practice in shifting attention from dwelling on emotion to what to do.

**Step 3. Say only what you want to remember aloud**
When studying or preparing a talk, pick only the key sentences and read them aloud. The production effect showed up better when only some things were spoken aloud than when everything was.

## Conclusion

The original post's phrase that a single word "redesigns" the brain ran a bit ahead. But research confirmed that how you talk to yourself changes how you handle emotion. Using your name instead of "I" cut emotional reactions within a second, with no need to strain. And what was said aloud was remembered better. Next time you slip up, try saying, "Alex, you're flustered right now."

### 🙋 Frequently Asked Questions

#### It feels awkward to talk to myself by name in front of others.

You can do it silently in your head. In the studies, participants mostly talked to themselves silently too. If your name feels awkward, switching to "you," as in "you're nervous right now," had a similar effect.

#### Does repeating positive statements change the brain?

Repeating positive statements alone didn't do much, and one study even found it backfired for people with low self-esteem (Wood et al., **Psychological Science**, 2009). Rather than repeating statements, creating distance with your name and ending with a question and next action is closer to the evidence. How to name your emotions is covered in [Why You Repeat Things You Regret](/en/blog/if-you-keep-doing-things-you-regret-the-brain-acted-first/).
