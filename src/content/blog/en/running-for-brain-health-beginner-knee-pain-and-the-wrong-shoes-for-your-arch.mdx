## 📌 Key takeaways

> **The short answer:** Choosing running shoes to match your arch type didn't reduce injuries. What mattered more for **beginner runners' knee pain** was **increasing distance too quickly**. It's closer to the evidence to pick shoes by **how comfortable they feel** rather than by arch type, and to build up distance gradually.

- 🏃 **Running injuries are common, and the knee is the top site.** Lower-limb injury rates in distance runners ranged from 19% to 79% across studies, and the knee was the most common site (**British Journal of Sports Medicine**, 2007).
- 👟 **Shoes matched to foot shape didn't help.** US Marine Corps recruits were given running shoes based on the shape of their soles, but their **injury rates were similar** to those of recruits given the same shoe regardless (**American Journal of Sports Medicine**, 2010).
- 🦶 **Pronation didn't mean more injuries.** When 927 beginner runners were followed for a year, those whose feet rolled inward **weren't at higher risk** while wearing a neutral shoe (**BJSM**, 2014).
- 📈 **Ramping up distance fast is risky.** Beginner runners who increased their weekly distance by more than 30% had more of certain overuse injuries (**JOSPT**, 2014).

### 💬 "A few days of running and my knee aches." Is it really the shoes?

More and more people are taking up running these days. But it's also very common to put the running shoes back in the closet after a few days because of aching knees or shins. When I first wrote this post, I pointed to **running shoes that don't fit your arch** as the cause and laid out the rule: stability shoes for flat feet, cushioned shoes for high arches.

You often hear this in running stores too, but looking at the research this time, the evidence that it reduces injuries was weak. Below I correct it and sum up what was actually linked to knee pain.

## Deep Dive: why do beginner runners get knee pain?

### 1. If running is good for the brain, why do injuries matter?

Regular aerobic exercise enlarged the **hippocampus**, which handles memory. When 120 older adults walked three times a week for a year, their hippocampus grew by about 2% (Erickson et al., **PNAS**, 2011). But these changes appeared only when people kept it up **steadily** for months.

And the most common thing that breaks that steadiness is injury. A review of studies on distance runners found lower-limb injury rates ranging from 19% to 79%, with the **knee** the most common site (van Gent et al., **British Journal of Sports Medicine**, 2007).

### 2. Do running shoes matched to your arch reduce injuries?

In my original post I wrote that you should pick "stability shoes for flat feet, cushioned shoes for high arches, and neutral shoes for normal arches." There's research that tested exactly this.

- A US military team photographed the soles of Marine Corps recruits and gave motion-control shoes to flat-footed recruits, stability shoes to those with normal arches, and cushioned shoes to those with high arches, while another group got stability shoes regardless of foot shape (Knapik et al., **American Journal of Sports Medicine**, 2010). By the end of training, **injury rates in the two groups were similar**.
- A Danish team gave 927 beginner runners the same neutral running shoe and followed them for a year (Nielsen et al., **British Journal of Sports Medicine**, 2014). People whose feet rolled inward, called **pronation**, were not at higher risk of injury, and per 1,000 km run, those with mild pronation actually had fewer injuries.

> In my original post I wrote that "beginners' knee pain and plantar fasciitis are often caused by a mismatch between arch type and shoe." Since choosing shoes by arch type didn't reduce injuries in the studies above, I corrected that explanation. Knowing your arch isn't a bad thing in itself, but the evidence for picking shoes by it alone is weak.

### 3. So what was linked to knee pain?

The same Danish team analyzed the running logs of 873 beginner runners and reported that people who **increased their weekly distance by more than 30%** over two weeks had more of certain overuse injuries, such as pain at the front of the knee (Nielsen et al., **Journal of Orthopaedic & Sports Physical Therapy**, 2014). Interestingly, there wasn't a big difference between people who increased by less than 10% and those who increased by 10% to 30%, so the evidence that you must stick to the popular "10% rule" was weak. The key is **not increasing a lot all at once**.

As for shoes, Benno Nigg's team at the University of Calgary proposed a new standard. Try several running shoes and pick **the one that feels most comfortable**, and your body can move closer to the path it naturally wants to take. They called this the "comfort filter" (Nigg et al., **British Journal of Sports Medicine**, 2015). A Luxembourg team that followed 264 runners also found that those who **rotated several pairs** of running shoes had about a 39% lower risk of injury (Malisoux et al., **Scandinavian Journal of Medicine & Science in Sports**, 2015).

## The fix: three steps to running without knee pain

First, check your running habits.

**Step 1. Distance: don't increase it by more than 30% in two weeks**
Start with 20 to 30 minutes mixing walking and running. When you add distance, don't jump up all at once; increase it bit by bit as your body adapts.

**Step 2. Shoes: choose by comfort rather than arch type**
In a store, try on several pairs and walk or jog lightly in them. Check that there's about a finger's width of space in front of your big toe and that the sides don't squeeze your foot, then pick **the most comfortable pair**. Trying them on in the afternoon, when your feet are a bit swollen, also helps.

**Step 3. Pain: rest instead of pushing through**
If pain starts, rest for a few days, and when you return, start shorter than before. If the pain keeps coming back, it's safest to have the cause checked by a doctor. For warming up and cooling down, see [the 5-minute warm-up and cool-down](/en/blog/skip-the-5-minute-warm-up-and-cool-down-and-you-halve-runnings-brain-benefits/).

## Conclusion

It's easy to blame beginner knee pain on shoes, but in research, running shoes matched to arch type didn't reduce injuries. What mattered more was **not suddenly increasing how much you run** and **shoes that feel comfortable on your feet**. Running for a long time without pain is what brings brain changes like a larger hippocampus. This week, rather than adding distance, run only as much as feels comfortable.

### 🙋 Frequently asked questions

#### If I have flat feet, do I have to wear stability shoes?

Not necessarily. Flat-footed recruits given motion-control shoes had injury rates similar to the group in standard shoes. If stability shoes feel more comfortable, they're a fine choice, but there's no need to force yourself into them if they don't. I covered the features of each arch type in more detail in [how to choose running shoes by arch type](/en/blog/flat-feet-high-arches-normal-arches-how-to-choose-running-shoes/).

#### How much running a day is good for the brain?

One study found that running at moderate intensity for just 10 minutes improved mood and focus. Starting short and keeping it up is what matters most. I covered how to get started in [why running is good for the brain and how to start](/en/blog/bdnf-just-10-minutes-of-running-changes-the-brain-how-to-start/).
