## 📌 Key Takeaways

> **The short answer:** Sunlight is likely to help focus. In everyday life, **people who got more bright light during the day** were more alert and responded faster, and the bigger the difference between daytime and nighttime light, the better they did on cognitive tasks. But this was an observational study, so it's too early to say light was the cause.

- ☀️ **People who got more daylight were more alert.** When 58 adults wore light sensors for a week, those who got more bright light during the day felt more alert and did better on cognitive tasks (**Communications Psychology**, 2023).
- 🪟 **Working by a window meant far more light.** Office workers with windows got much more daylight than those without, and slept an average of 46 minutes longer at night (**Journal of Clinical Sleep Medicine**, 2014).
- 🏕️ **Following natural light moved the body clock earlier.** A week of camping with no artificial light moved the body clock about two hours earlier, so people got sleepy near sunset and woke near sunrise (**Current Biology**, 2013).
- 👁️ **The eye has cells just for measuring light.** Separate from the cells that see objects, the retina has **intrinsically photosensitive retinal ganglion cells** that measure light and signal the body clock (**Science**, 2002).

## Deep Dive: How does light change the brain's focus?

### 1. Is daytime light really linked to focus?

A University of Manchester team in the UK had 58 adults wear a wrist light sensor for a week, while measuring their sleepiness and cognitive task performance several times a day with a smartphone app (Didikoglu et al., **Communications Psychology**, 2023).

- People who'd received **bright light** in the preceding hours felt more alert and performed better on cognitive tasks.
- People living with **bright days and dark nights**, a big difference between daytime and nighttime light, did better.
- People who habitually went to bed and woke early showed a clearer effect of light.

Measuring in everyday life rather than a lab is a strength, but it's also an **observational study**. People already feeling good may have gone outside more, so it can't be concluded that light boosted focus. Lab studies have repeatedly found that bright light reduces sleepiness and raises alertness (Cajochen, **Sleep Medicine Reviews**, 2007).

### 2. How does the eye tell the brain how much light there is?

As the original post explained, separate from the cells that see objects, the retina has **intrinsically photosensitive retinal ganglion cells** (ipRGCs). Using a protein called **melanopsin**, these cells respond to light, especially bluish light, and signal the brain's body clock that "it's daytime." A Brown University team first showed that these cells set the body clock (Berson et al., **Science**, 2002). This principle, and how morning light affects night sleep, is covered in detail in [how 10 minutes of morning sunlight sets your body clock](/en/articles/morning-light/).

### 3. Does a window seat really make a difference?

It did. A Northwestern University team compared 49 office workers in offices with and without windows (Boubekri et al., **Journal of Clinical Sleep Medicine**, 2014).

- People who worked by windows got far more **daylight** during work hours.
- They slept an average of **46 minutes longer** at night, with better sleep quality and more activity.

The original post's advice to "choose a window seat when working indoors" fits this study well. But this study also compared people at their existing desks rather than randomly assigning them.

### 4. Does exercising at night really throw off your rhythm?

The original post said the bright artificial light you get at night is a bigger problem than night exercise itself. A camping experiment by a University of Colorado team shows this well. When participants camped for a week with no artificial light, getting only natural light, their body clocks (melatonin rhythms) moved about **two hours earlier**. Normally, late-night artificial light had been delaying their body clocks (Wright et al., **Current Biology**, 2013).

So rather than night exercise itself, spending long hours under bright light late at night is what tends to delay the body clock. The link between evening exercise and sleep is also covered in [Can Exercise Stop Late-Night Snack Cravings?](/en/blog/break-bad-habits-reset-the-brains-reward-circuit-with-sweat/).

**Step 1. Get light outside in the morning**
Like me, make time to run or walk in the morning. If that's hard, a 10-minute walk on your commute is enough. Even outside on a cloudy day is far brighter than indoor lighting.

**Step 2. Seek windows and the outdoors during the day**
Move your desk to a window, or go outside for 10–15 minutes on breaks and at lunch. The more light people got during the day, the more alert they were.

**Step 3. Dim the lights at night**
Starting 1–2 hours before bed, dim the lights at home and lower screen brightness. The key is making a big difference between daytime and nighttime light.

## Conclusion

When focus drops, we usually try to add something: coffee, supplements, more sleep. But research and my experience pointed in a slightly different direction: first create the difference of bright days and dark nights. That simply moving my runs to the morning changed how my body felt may well have come from this difference. Today at lunch, try going outside for just 10 minutes.

### 🙋 Frequently Asked Questions

#### Does sunlight still help on cloudy days?

It's weaker than on clear days, but outside on a cloudy day is still far brighter than most indoor lighting. So even on cloudy days, going out beats staying indoors.

#### If I don't have a window seat, can I just change my lighting?

There are experiments showing bright lights or light therapy lamps raise alertness, but how well they can replace natural light varies by person and product. I haven't tried a light therapy lamp myself. The easiest approach is going outside for 10–15 minutes at lunch. If you have an eye condition or bipolar disorder, talk with a doctor before using a light therapy lamp.
