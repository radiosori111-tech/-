## 📌 Key takeaways

> **The short answer:** Neuroplasticity is the brain's ability to change its structure and connections through experience and practice. Adult brains do change, but they change when you keep doing something new and a little hard, and they drift back when you stop.

- 🧠 **The brain changes with use.** People who learned to juggle for three months gained **gray matter** in areas that track moving objects, and lost it again when they stopped (**Nature**, 2004).
- 🚕 **Paths used for years grow thicker.** London taxi drivers, who must memorize a maze of streets, had a larger rear **hippocampus**, the area for spatial memory, and it was larger the longer they had driven (**PNAS**, 2000).
- 🎨 **New, slightly hard learning worked.** Adults aged 60 to 90 who **learned new skills** such as digital photography or quilting improved their memory; those who only did familiar activities did not (**Psychological Science**, 2014).
- 💬 **Changing the interpretation changes the brain's response.** When people reinterpreted the same scene, activity in the **amygdala**, which amplifies emotion, fell and the **prefrontal cortex** became more active (**Journal of Cognitive Neuroscience**, 2002).

### 💬 Will "I'm tired, I'll rest" ever change my brain?

I first wrote this in the last week of 2022. I wanted my brain to change like a successful person's, but after work I'd say "I'm tired, I'll rest" and end the day scrolling on my phone. So around then I started four things.

1. **Reading at least 10 minutes a day.** I was reading Monty Lyman's *The Painful Truth*, recommended by a YouTube channel.
2. **Writing a blog.** I started with a "let's just try it" attitude. It wasn't easy, but I kept going.
3. **Exercising 30 minutes a day.**
4. **Trying things I'd never done.** I subscribed to an online class for a year and learned to draw on an iPad.

That year had five days left, counting that day. I was about to write "only five days left," but deliberately changed it to "**five whole days** left," because I believed the brain responds differently depending on how it interprets what it is given. This post checks how far that belief holds up in research.

## Deep Dive: does the brain really change with what you do?

### 1. Three months of juggling grew gray matter

Bogdan Draganski's team at the University of Regensburg in Germany had adults who had never juggled practice a three-ball cascade for three months, scanning their brains before and after (Draganski et al., **Nature**, 2004).

- Those who learned to juggle gained **gray matter** in areas that perceive and judge moving objects (the mid-temporal area and the intraparietal sulcus).
- Three months after they stopped practicing, that extra gray matter **shrank back**.

The brain is not plastic that changes shape once and stays that way; it is living tissue that **changes while you use it and returns when you don't**. It's like a mountain trail that widens when people walk it often and grows over when no one does.

### 2. The London taxi drivers' hippocampus

Eleanor Maguire's team at University College London scanned the brains of taxi drivers, who must learn more than 20,000 London streets (Maguire et al., **PNAS**, 2000). The drivers had a larger **rear hippocampus**, the area for spatial memory, than people of the same age, and it was larger **the longer they had driven**. A skill used over and over for years had left its mark on the brain's structure.

### 3. You need to learn something new and a little hard

So will any activity do? Denise Park's team at the University of Texas at Dallas assigned more than 200 adults aged 60 to 90 to different activities for 15 hours a week over 14 weeks (Park et al., **Psychological Science**, 2014).

- Those who learned demanding new skills such as **digital photography or quilting** improved their **episodic memory**.
- Those who did **familiar, comfortable activities** such as listening to music or doing puzzles spent the same time without improving their memory.

Just as I learned iPad drawing that year, **new, slightly hard learning** is the key that moves the brain. Santiago Ramón y Cajal, the Nobel Prize–winning neuroscientist, wrote: "**Every man can, if he so desires, become the sculptor of his own brain.**"

### 4. "Only five days" vs. "five whole days": reinterpretation changes the brain's response

Kevin Ochsner and James Gross's team at Stanford showed people unpleasant photos and had them sometimes simply feel and sometimes **reinterpret** what they saw (Ochsner et al., **Journal of Cognitive Neuroscience**, 2002), for example thinking of a hospital scene as "someone who will soon get better."

- When people reinterpreted, the **negative emotion** they felt decreased.
- Activity in the **amygdala**, which amplifies emotion, fell, while the **prefrontal cortex**, which plans and regulates, became more active.

Changing "only five days" to "five whole days" was exactly this technique, which psychology calls **reappraisal**. A change of words doesn't change the situation, but it does change **the brain's response** to it.

### 5. Summary: what's confirmed and what to be careful about

## The plan: 3 steps to change your brain with new habits

**Step 1. Pick one new thing that's a little hard**
Choose **a skill you're learning for the first time** rather than something you're already good at: an instrument, drawing, a foreign language or a new sport, something clumsy enough at first that you have to think. In the study of older adults, only those learning new skills, not those doing comfortable activities, improved their memory.

**Step 2. A little every day at the same time, and don't stop**
In the juggling study, the extra gray matter shrank back when practice stopped. Keep going for even 10 minutes a day at **the same time and place**. The time it took for a new habit to become automatic ranged from 18 to 254 days, with a median of 66 days (Lally et al., **European Journal of Social Psychology**, 2010).

**Step 3. Put the situation into different words**
When you think "I only managed this much," try saying "I managed this much." It isn't denying the facts; it's **practicing seeing the same fact from another angle**. This reappraisal changes how the brain's emotion circuits respond.

## Conclusion

Successful people don't start out with different brains. What research shows is that **the brain changes with what you do**. Learn to juggle and the areas that read motion grow; memorize streets for years and the hippocampus gets bigger; learn new skills in later life and memory improves. And when practice stops, those changes fade too. So if there is a secret of success, it is less a grand resolution than **doing a little of something new and slightly hard, again today**. You and I are both changing right now. Let's make each day a little better than yesterday.

### 🙋 Frequently asked questions

#### Can the brain still change as we get older?

Yes. The participants whose memory improved after learning new skills were 60 to 90 years old (Park et al., **Psychological Science**, 2014). In another experiment, adults aged 55 to 80 who did a year of aerobic walking grew their hippocampus by about 2% (Erickson et al., **PNAS**, 2011). Change may slow with age, but the ability to change does not disappear.

#### How long does it take for the brain to change?

It varies by study, but in the juggling experiment brain changes appeared after **three months** of practice, and habits took a median of **66 days** to become automatic. What matters more than the length is **not stopping**, since the extra gray matter shrank back once practice ended.
