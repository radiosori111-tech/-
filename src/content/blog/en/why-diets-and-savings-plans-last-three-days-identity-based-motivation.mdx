## 📌 Key takeaways

> **The short answer:** When diets and savings plans end within days, what matters more than willpower is **how close your future self feels to your present self** and **how you interpret the hard moments**. People who heard the same future described as **"7,300 days"** instead of "20 years" wanted to start saving sooner, and people who read difficulty as "it's hard because it matters" gave up less.

- 📆 **Counting in days brings the future closer.** People told that retirement was "7,300 days" away rather than "20 years" said they would start saving **much sooner** (**Psychological Science**, 2015).
- 👴 **Seeing your future self boosts saving.** People who saw an age-progressed image of their own face said they would put more money into retirement accounts (**Journal of Marketing Research**, 2011).
- 🎓 **Students who pictured "possible selves" did better.** Middle schoolers who pictured their future selves along with the obstacles in the way had better grades and attendance over two years (**Journal of Personality and Social Psychology**, 2006).
- 💪 **The "willpower runs out" theory didn't replicate.** When 23 labs repeated the experiment together, the ego-depletion effect barely appeared (**Perspectives on Psychological Science**, 2016).

### 💬 "If I quit after three days, is my willpower really that weak?"

If you gave up a diet three days in, it's easy to blame weak willpower. In the original version of this post, I introduced the **identity-based motivation (IBM)** theory of **Daphna Oyserman**, a psychologist at the University of Southern California (USC). The idea was that success depends on whether a goal feels like something "people like me" do, how you interpret difficulty, and whether your strategy fits you.

This time I looked up the original research behind the theory. Its main claims were confirmed by experiments, and there was a particularly interesting result you can use for saving right away. But some practical advice still has weak evidence, and I've noted that.

## Deep Dive: why do resolutions keep failing?

### 1. What does identity-based motivation theory say?

Oyserman's theory comes down to three points (Oyserman et al., **Journal of Personality and Social Psychology**, 2007).

- **Does the action feel like me:** We do things that feel like "what people like me do" and avoid things that don't.
- **Do I need to act now:** We act now when a future goal feels connected to our present self.
- **How do I read difficulty:** The same difficulty can be read as "it's hard because it matters" or "it's hard because it's not for me."

The research team showed that US minority students engaged less in health behaviors when they felt that exercise or healthy eating "isn't what people like us do." My original explanation that "goals that don't feel like you don't last" fits this research.

### 2. Does a closer-feeling future really make you start sooner?

The most practical result from this theory comes from an experiment on **units of time**. Neil Lewis of the University of Michigan and Oyserman told people how long they had until retirement, either as "20 years" or as "7,300 days" (Lewis & Oyserman, **Psychological Science**, 2015). Even though it was the same span, **people who heard it in days** felt the future was closer and said they would start saving **much sooner**.

A Stanford University team showed participants **an aged version of their own face** in virtual reality (Hershfield et al., **Journal of Marketing Research**, 2011). Those who saw their older selves said they would put more money into retirement accounts than those who saw their current selves. The more our future self feels like "someone else," the harder it is to hold back today for that person.

### 3. Does how you read hard moments really matter?

In the first version of this post I described two interpretations: "this is so hard, it must really matter" and "this is so hard, it must not be for me." Oyserman's team built a scale to measure these two interpretations and showed across several studies that people who read difficulty as **a sign of importance** got better grades and stuck with tasks longer, while people who read it as **a sign of impossibility** gave up more easily (Fisher & Oyserman, **Motivation Science**, 2017).

The intervention worked in practice too. The team ran classes in which low-income US eighth graders pictured a "possible future self," the obstacles in the way, and what they could do now (Oyserman et al., **Journal of Personality and Social Psychology**, 2006). Students randomly assigned to these classes had **better grades and attendance** for up to two years afterward, and less depression.

### 4. Should you rewrite goals as "I'm someone who…"?

In the first version I recommended turning goals into identity statements, such as "I'm someone who cares for my body" instead of "I have to lose weight." A famous experiment on identity wording found that asking about "being a voter" instead of "voting" raised turnout (Bryan et al., **PNAS**, 2011). But a larger field experiment didn't replicate it (Gerber et al., **PNAS**, 2016). Changing the identity wording alone may not be enough; what seems more important is **concretely linking** your future self to today's actions.

> In the first version I wrote that strategies matching your personality last longer, such as group exercise if you like socializing and solo exercise if you prefer being alone. It's plausible advice, but I couldn't find research comparing it directly, so I downgraded it to "worth trying." Also, the "ego depletion" theory, which says we fail because our willpower runs out, showed almost no effect in a large replication across 23 labs (Hagger et al., **Perspectives on Psychological Science**, 2016). That's one more reason to blame willpower less.

### 5. Summary: what held up and what I corrected

| Claim in the first version | What research shows | Type of evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Goals that don't feel like you don't last | People did less of behaviors they felt weren't for "people like us" | Experiments (JPSP 2007) |
| How you read hard moments changes the outcome | People who read difficulty as importance gave up less | Scale studies (Motiv Sci 2017) |
| Identity-based interventions are used in schools | A randomized trial improved grades and attendance for two years | Randomized trial (JPSP 2006) |
| Rewrite goals as identity statements | Identity-wording experiments had mixed replications | Field experiments (PNAS 2011, 2016) |
| Strategies matching your personality last longer | I found no study comparing this directly | Little evidence |

## The fix: three steps to get past day three

**Step 1. Count the future in days to bring it closer**
Turn "retirement in 20 years" or "10 kg in three months" into a number of days. Then decide one action for **today, the first of those days**. You can count it out right below.

**Step 2. Picture your future self and the obstacles together**
Picture the future self you want in concrete terms, then write down the obstacles in the way and what you'll do when they show up. You can do this directly in the WOOP planner in [three ways to achieve your goals](/en/blog/three-ways-to-achieve-your-goals/).

**Step 3. When it's hard, reread it as "hard because it matters"**
When a plateau or temptation makes you want to quit and the thought "this just isn't for me" comes up, rephrase it as "this is hard because it matters." Missing a single day didn't materially affect habit formation, so just start again the next day (Lally et al., **European Journal of Social Psychology**, 2010).

| Step | What | How much | Evidence | What the evidence showed |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Count in days | Convert the time to your goal into days and pick today's action | Once per goal | Psych Sci (2015), J Mark Res (2011) | The closer the future felt, the sooner people wanted to start saving |
| 2. Picture your future self | Write down your future self, obstacles, and what you'll do | 5 minutes | JPSP (2006) | Grades and attendance improved for two years |
| 3. Reread | Interpret hard moments as "hard because it matters" | Whenever you want to quit | Motiv Sci (2017), Eur J Soc Psychol (2010) | People who read difficulty as importance gave up less |

## Conclusion

Diets and savings plans may collapse after three days not because willpower runs dry, but because our future self feels too far away and we read hard moments as "this isn't for me." The same future feels closer when counted in days, and the same difficulty leads to less quitting when read as "hard because it matters." Think of today as day one of 7,300, and take just one tiny first step.

### 🙋 Frequently asked questions

#### Does this work for dieting too?

Research on making the future self feel closer has mostly focused on saving, but interpreting difficulty and picturing "possible selves" have also been studied for health behaviors. For dieting, also try setting meal times to cut down on snacking, which I covered in [intermittent fasting results](/en/blog/intermittent-fasting-a-secret-for-those-who-gained-weight-over-the-holidays/).

#### I've heard we give up because the brain uses so much energy. Is that true?

It's true that the brain uses about 20% of the body's energy, but whether that directly causes three-day resolutions needs a separate look. I continue that story in [why we give up on diets and tasks](/en/blog/why-we-give-up-diets-and-tasks-the-brain-uses-20-percent-of-energy/).
