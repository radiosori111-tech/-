## 📌 Key takeaways

> **The short answer:** Intermittent fasting helps you lose weight mainly because **a shorter eating window makes you eat less**, not because of the fasting hours themselves. When people ate the same calories, adding a time limit made no meaningful difference to weight loss after a year. So after the holidays, the keys are to **set your eating hours to cut out snacks, eat less within them, and protect your muscle**.

- ⚖️ **Calories decided the weight loss.** Two groups cut calories by the same amount, and only one ate within 8 hours. After a year, the difference in weight loss was not meaningful (**NEJM**, 2022).
- 💪 **Limiting time alone cost muscle.** In a 12-week trial of 16:8 eating alone, weight fell about as much as in the control group, and arm and leg **muscle mass** fell more (**JAMA Internal Medicine**, 2020).
- 🌅 **Timing may matter on its own.** When men ate the same food but finished it all before 3 p.m., their **insulin sensitivity** improved even though their weight did not change (**Cell Metabolism**, 2018). That study had only eight people, though.
- 🎉 **Holiday weight gain is smaller than you think, but it sticks.** People gained an average of 0.37 kg over the year-end holidays, and it was still there the following spring (**NEJM**, 2000).

### 💬 "I ate all holiday long, and my body never got a break"

The 2023 Lunar New Year holiday with my parents was a lot of fun, but I also came back with some "bulking up" I didn't need. Holiday food is high in calories, but looking back, what really did it was eating at all hours, even between meals. My stomach felt heavy all the time, so I decided to "give my body a break" and started **intermittent fasting** again.

When I first wrote this post, I said intermittent fasting flushes out toxins, extends lifespan, and even reduces cancer cells. Going back to the original papers this time, I found that results confirmed in people were mixed up with results seen only in animals. I'll correct them one by one below.

## Deep Dive: what does intermittent fasting do to the body?

### 1. How much weight do we really gain over the holidays?

A team at the US National Institutes of Health weighed 195 adults from before Thanksgiving through the following fall (Yanovski et al., **NEJM**, 2000). Over the six-week holiday season, they gained an average of only **0.37 kg**, less than you might expect. But that weight did not come off after the holidays, and it made up more than half of the weight they gained over the whole year.

The big jump on the scale right after a holiday is largely water held from salty food and carbohydrates, plus food still being digested. The real problem is the small amount that stays and builds up year after year. That's why the few days after a holiday matter.

> In my original post I wrote that eating nonstop creates "false hunger" and "toxins," and that foods that don't go well together produce gas. Feeling bloated after overeating is real, but I couldn't find research showing that intermittent fasting flushes "toxins" out of the body, so I removed that explanation.

### 2. Why does intermittent fasting help you lose weight?

Intermittent fasting includes **alternate-day fasting** (fasting every other day), **5:2 fasting** (eating very little two days a week), and **time-restricted eating** (eating only during set hours each day, like 16:8). The 16:8 most people talk about is the last kind.

About 12 to 36 hours after your last meal, the sugar stored in your liver (glycogen) runs low, and the body starts burning fat to make **ketones**. Mark Mattson and Rafael de Cabo of the US National Institute on Aging call this **metabolic switching** and propose that repeating it may bring health benefits (de Cabo & Mattson, **NEJM**, 2019).

But when researchers compared weight loss in people, the results were different.

- A team at Southern Medical University in China had 139 adults with obesity all **cut calories** by the same amount, and only one group ate between 8 a.m. and 4 p.m. (Liu et al., **NEJM**, 2022). After a year, the time-restricted group lost 8.0 kg and the calorie-only group lost 6.3 kg, a difference that was not statistically meaningful.
- A team at UCSF had 116 people eat **only between noon and 8 p.m.**, with no calorie limit (Lowe et al., **JAMA Internal Medicine**, 2020). After 12 weeks, the time-restricted group lost 0.94 kg and the control group, eating three meals, lost 0.68 kg, almost no difference.
- Alternate-day fasting also didn't beat simply eating a bit less every day, and more people dropped out of it (Trepanowski et al., **JAMA Internal Medicine**, 2017).

In short, most of intermittent fasting's power to cause weight loss comes from **a shorter eating window that cuts out snacks and late-night eating and lowers the total amount**. Setting a time is a good framework for eating less, but if you binge inside that window, the effect disappears.

### 3. After 16 hours of fasting, does the body use only old cells and spare muscle?

In my original post, I cited the stages of fasting described by George Cahill of Harvard Medical School and wrote that 16 to 30 hours after a meal, the body makes glucose by **recycling old and sick cells** rather than muscle. Rereading the original paper, I found this was wrong.

- According to Cahill's research, early in a fast the liver makes new glucose mainly from **amino acids released from muscle protein**. Only after several days, once the brain starts using ketones for energy, does protein breakdown slow down (Cahill, **Annual Review of Nutrition**, 2006).
- In the UCSF trial above, the group that only followed 16:8 also **lost more arm and leg muscle** than the control group (Lowe et al., 2020).
- On the other hand, when men who were already strength training did 16:8 for eight weeks and kept training, they lost fat and kept their muscle (Moro et al., **Journal of Translational Medicine**, 2016).

**Autophagy**, in which cells break down and recycle their old parts, is real, and Yoshinori Ohsumi won the 2016 Nobel Prize for uncovering how it works. But the finding that fasting greatly boosts autophagy comes mainly from mice (Alirezaei et al., **Autophagy**, 2010), and we still don't know how much happens in people after a given number of hours. It's safer not to believe that "after 16 hours you use only old cells instead of muscle."

### 4. Is it good for insulin sensitivity and brain health?

The **timing** of meals may matter separately from the amount. Eight men with prediabetes ate the same food, but for five weeks they ate it all within six hours, finishing before 3 p.m., and for another five weeks they spread it over 12 hours (Sutton et al., **Cell Metabolism**, 2018). Their weight stayed the same, but during the early-eating weeks their **insulin sensitivity** and blood pressure improved. With only eight participants, though, larger studies are needed.

The longer lifespan, fewer cancer cells, and better thinking from higher **BDNF** that I listed in my original post come mostly from animal studies. Mattson's team also concluded that the link between fasting and brain health is clear in animals but still needs much more confirmation in people (Mattson et al., **Nature Reviews Neuroscience**, 2018).

## The fix: three steps for intermittent fasting after the holidays

**Step 1. Set your eating hours: start with 12**
Rather than trying to fast for 16 hours right away, start with a **12-hour fast** and shorten your window to 10 and then 8 hours as you get used to it. If you can, finish dinner early. You can set a window that suits you below.

**Step 2. Eat less within those hours: vegetables first, slowly**
In the end, the amount decides the weight loss. At meals, **eat vegetables and protein first** and rice or bread last. When 11 people with type 2 diabetes ate the same food in a different order, eating vegetables and protein first left their blood sugar about 29% and 37% lower at 30 and 60 minutes after the meal (Shukla et al., **Diabetes Care**, 2015). And when 30 women ate the same meal slowly, they ate less than when they ate quickly (Andrade et al., **Journal of the American Dietetic Association**, 2008). Take 20 to 30 minutes per meal and chew well.

**Step 3. Protect your muscle: protein and strength training**
Limiting time alone can cost muscle. Include **a protein dish** at every meal, and two or three times a week do exercises that work your muscles, like squats or push-ups. People who kept up strength training held on to their muscle while doing 16:8.

| Step | What | How much | Evidence | What the evidence showed |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Time | Set eating hours and cut snacks and late-night eating | Start with a 12-hour fast | NEJM (2022), Cell Metab (2018) | Calories decided weight loss, and eating early raised insulin sensitivity |
| 2. Amount | Eat vegetables and protein first, slowly | 20 to 30 minutes a meal | Diabetes Care (2015), J Am Diet Assoc (2008) | Post-meal blood sugar was lower, and eating slowly meant eating less |
| 3. Muscle | Get protein and do strength training | 2 to 3 times a week | JAMA IM (2020), J Transl Med (2016) | Limiting time alone cost muscle, and continued training preserved it |

## Conclusion

If you start intermittent fasting after the holidays, remember one thing. The fasting hours don't magically burn fat; **setting eating hours simply makes it easier to eat less**. That's why you need to cut the amount within your window and pair it with exercise that protects your muscle. So that the little weight from the holidays doesn't stay all year, start tonight by deciding when you'll finish dinner.

### 🙋 Frequently asked questions

#### Can I drink coffee or tea while fasting?

**Drinks with almost no calories**, like water, black coffee, and unsweetened tea, were allowed during fasting hours in most time-restricted eating studies. Add milk or syrup, though, and it counts as eating. If coffee on an empty stomach upsets you, start with water. If you're curious how your blood sugar changes while fasting, see [how to use a glucose meter at home](/en/blog/glucose-meter-at-home-brain-health-signals-from-one-drop-of-blood/).

#### Who shouldn't try intermittent fasting?

**People taking diabetes medication or insulin** risk low blood sugar and must talk to their doctor first. It's also not recommended if you are pregnant or breastfeeding, have a history of eating disorders, or are a growing teenager. If your blood sugar is normal but you feel tired and can't focus, also look at the [self-check signs of insulin resistance](/en/blog/normal-blood-sugar-but-an-aging-brain-insulin-resistance-self-check/).
