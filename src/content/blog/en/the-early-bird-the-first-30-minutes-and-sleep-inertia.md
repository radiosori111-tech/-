Honestly, for a long time I thought "what time you get up" was everything. I felt proud just to open my eyes at 5 a.m., and then lay in bed skimming KakaoTalk notifications and scrolling my Instagram feed. Back then I didn't know why my days always started in a haze even though I got up early. It turned out the problem wasn't the time I woke up, but how I spent the first 30 minutes after opening my eyes.

- <strong>The core problem:</strong> checking your smartphone right after waking disrupts sleep inertia and the **cortisol** awakening response (CAR), setting off anxiety and fatigue for the rest of the day.
- <strong>The solution:</strong> as soon as you get up, run a fixed routine (brushing your teeth, salt tea, simple exercise) to cut decision fatigue and gain a sense of control.
- <strong>The result:</strong> what matters more than wake-up time is filling <strong>"the first 30 minutes after you open your eyes"</strong> with actions you chose yourself, so the brain lands softly.

## Sleep inertia: the brain's most vulnerable moment

That groggy body and foggy head right after waking: it turns out I wasn't the only one. This state is called <strong>sleep inertia</strong>. Sleep inertia is <strong>a transitional state in which the slow waves (delta and theta) of sleep haven't fully switched over to the alpha and beta waves of wakefulness</strong>. Put simply, the brain is still half asleep, <strong>with consciousness only partly switched on</strong>.

That was exactly why I felt especially touchy or out of sorts when I picked up my phone at that hour. <strong>When provocative news or messages arrive in this brief gap while brain waves are shifting, the brain takes them as a severe threat signal</strong>. You're hit with the strongest stimuli at the moment your judgment is at its lowest.

## When cortisol is poison and when it's medicine

<strong>In the 30 to 45 minutes after waking, the body's level of cortisol (the stress hormone) rises sharply on its own</strong>. This is called the <strong>cortisol awakening response</strong> (CAR). <strong>CAR</strong> is <strong>a physiological mechanism in which the body rapidly releases the stress hormone cortisol to generate the energy to get through the day</strong>.

This response isn't bad. Used well, it's actually the source of your morning energy.
<strong>The problem</strong> arises <strong>when you're exposed to negative information or anxiety-provoking stimuli right as this cortisol is being released</strong>. <strong>The normal CAR response becomes distorted, leaving you anxious for no reason or unable to shake fatigue all day</strong>.

I used to be left with an unexplained uneasiness every morning; looking back, I was starting each day with a disrupted CAR. <strong>Now, as soon as I get up, I brush my teeth and drink a cup of warm salt tea. Then I do 100 squats. At first 100 sounded like a big number, but it actually takes less than five minutes</strong>. The refreshed feeling afterward was <strong>the sense of cortisol finally working in the direction of vitality</strong> rather than <strong>stress</strong>.

## The sense of control and dopamine a morning routine creates

The biggest change I actually felt, surprisingly, was that the question "what should I do?" disappeared. Human willpower and judgment are resources that get used up over the day. Deliberating from the moment you wake up about what to eat or what to do first is enough on its own to pile up <strong>decision fatigue</strong>. <strong>Decision fatigue is a state of cognitive exhaustion in which the quality of later decisions drops the more choices and judgments you make</strong>.

By contrast, when you start the morning with a routine decided the night before, <strong>the brain slips into autopilot without spending any energy</strong>. I fixed the order: brush my teeth, drink salt tea, do squats. <strong><em>The body moves without thinking. That in itself is a way of saving energy.</em></strong>
And honestly, this I didn't expect: I had no idea how big the sense of achievement would be after finishing 100 squats. When you complete a small goal, the brain releases a little **dopamine**.

<strong>That plants a sense of control: "I started today with my own hands." A morning begun passively, consuming someone else's content, starts from a completely different line.</strong>

| Morning choice | What happens |
| --- | --- |
| Checking social media and KakaoTalk | You start passively, consuming other people's content → <strong>sense of control drops</strong> |
| Running a fixed routine (teeth, water, exercise) | You complete an action you chose → <strong>a little dopamine, sense of control rises</strong> |
| Starting work after the routine | You move into focus mode without decision fatigue → <strong>morning productivity maximized</strong> |

## Questions you might have

### Q. Is not looking at your phone in the morning really that important?

A. In my experience, <strong>this isn't just self-help advice</strong>. Right after waking, sleep inertia leaves the brain's judgment at its lowest, and at the same time the cortisol awakening response (CAR) sends stress hormones soaring. If you take in provocative information while both conditions overlap, the brain drags that reaction through the whole day. When I compared the two myself, the difference was clear.

### Q. Does a morning routine have to be exercise?

A. It doesn't have to be. What matters is the experience of completing "a fixed action you chose yourself." Simply drinking a glass of water and opening the window releases dopamine and creates a sense of control. That said, after I added 100 squats, the feeling of cortisol being spent in the direction of vitality became much clearer.

### Q. If I wake up late, isn't it all pointless?

A. Even if you get up at 4 a.m., looking only at your phone in bed cancels out most of the physiological benefits of rising early. Conversely, even if you wake at 7 or 8 a.m., filling the first 30 minutes with an orderly routine <strong>makes a clear difference to your productivity and psychological stability for the day</strong>. <strong>What you do after waking is more decisive than when you wake.</strong>

### Q. Why drink warm salt tea in the morning?

A. During sleep the body loses water, and the autonomic nervous system stays in parasympathetic mode. Warm water raises body temperature and gently wakes the internal organs, helping the autonomic nervous system land softly. <strong>Adding a little salt is about replenishing electrolytes</strong>; in my case, my body seemed to wake up much faster when I did squats afterward.

## Closing

Back when I was fixated only on what time I got up, I opened my eyes early every day yet always lived as though the day had been taken from me. Now, even when I get up at 7, once I've filled the first 30 minutes with brushing my teeth, salt tea and 100 squats, I start the day more solidly than any dawn wake-up ever managed. Sleep inertia, the cortisol awakening response and the **circadian rhythm**, these three biological mechanisms were all saying the same thing: give the brain and body time to wake up on their own.

From here I plan to weave one more small habit into this 30-minute routine. <strong>Starting very small</strong> is, <strong>as my own experience has already proven, the secret to a routine that lasts</strong>. <strong>I recommend starting by deciding tonight, before you fall asleep, how you'll spend the first 30 minutes tomorrow morning.</strong>
