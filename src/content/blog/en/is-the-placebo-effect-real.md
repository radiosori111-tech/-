## 📌 Key takeaways

- 🦵 **Sham surgery eased pain as much as real surgery.** In a trial that split 180 people with knee osteoarthritis between real arthroscopic surgery and **sham surgery** with skin incisions only, neither real procedure beat the sham over two years (**New England Journal of Medicine**, 2002).
- 💊 **Placebo is not "just in your head."** When a drug that blocks **endorphins**, the body's own painkillers, was given, placebo pain relief disappeared (**Lancet**, 1978). The brain was drawing on its own painkillers.
- 🧠 **Expectation changes the brain areas that process pain.** People who expected relief showed less activity in the **thalamus, insula and anterior cingulate cortex** (**Science**, 2004).
- ⚖️ **But it is not a cure-all.** Placebo mostly changes **symptoms we feel**, such as pain or nausea; it does not cure **the disease itself**, such as a tumor or an infection.

### 💬 "Pain lives in the body. Why would thoughts change it?"

A few years ago a YouTube channel recommended **Monty Lyman**'s *The Painful Truth*, and I started reading it. Reading was one of the four things I did every day, as I described in my post on the secret of success. I was about a third of the way in when one experiment stopped me: people with knee pain had real surgery or fake surgery, and the results were the same.

I had always assumed pain measured damage exactly. The book instead describes pain as **an alarm the brain sounds to protect the body**. If it is an alarm, its volume can depend not only on the damage but on how the brain reads the situation. This post follows that idea only as far as the research actually goes.

## Deep Dive: how expectation changes pain

### 1. The knee sham-surgery trial: no difference

Bruce Moseley's team at the Houston VA Medical Center randomly split 180 people with knee **osteoarthritis** into three groups (Moseley et al., **New England Journal of Medicine**, 2002).

- One group had **arthroscopic debridement**, trimming inside the joint.
- One group had **lavage**, washing out the joint.
- One group was anesthetized and had **sham surgery**: skin incisions only, with no procedure inside the knee. Patients did not know which group they were in.

Over two years of tracking pain and function, neither real surgery did **better than the sham**. All three groups improved by similar amounts. My original post said the sham group did better; the accurate statement is that **there was no difference**. After this study, the use of arthroscopy for knee osteoarthritis was seriously reconsidered.

### 2. The brain unlocks its own painkillers

A team at the University of California, San Francisco gave people who had just had wisdom teeth removed a fake pill, telling them it was a painkiller (Levine, Gordon & Fields, **Lancet**, 1978). Some felt real relief. But when they were also given naloxone, a drug that blocks **endorphins**, the body's natural painkillers, that placebo relief disappeared.

This was the first evidence that placebo is not enduring pain through imagination but **the brain actually releasing painkilling chemicals**. It is as if the body keeps a medicine cabinet of painkillers, and **expectation** is the key that opens it.

### 3. Expectation changes the brain's pain areas

Tor Wager's team at the University of Michigan scanned people's brains while applying heat or electric shocks to the skin (Wager et al., **Science**, 2004). When a fake cream was applied and described as a pain-relieving cream before the same stimulus:

- People rated the **pain as weaker**.
- Activity in the **thalamus, insula and anterior cingulate cortex**, which process pain, fell along with it.
- **While waiting** for the stimulus, the **prefrontal cortex**, which plans and judges, was more active. Expectation came first, and then it turned down the pain signal.

Fabrizio Benedetti of the University of Turin and colleagues pulled such findings together, concluding that placebo works through real chemical signals in the brain such as endorphins and dopamine (Benedetti et al., **Journal of Neuroscience**, 2005).

### 4. The mirror image: learned helplessness

**Learned helplessness**, which my original post also discussed, is the flip side of placebo. In Martin Seligman's experiments in the 1960s, animals that repeatedly faced shocks they could not escape later stopped trying to escape even when they could.

Fifty years later, Seligman and Steven Maier revised that interpretation using brain research (Maier & Seligman, **Psychological Review**, 2016). Passivity, they argue, is less something learned than the brain's default reaction to prolonged bad events; what the brain learns is **the sense that "I can control this,"** which takes hold as the **prefrontal cortex** dampens the stress response. Seen this way, my original conclusion that **expectation and hope move the brain** points in the same direction as the research.

## The plan: three steps for facing pain

What placebo research teaches is not "take fake pills" but that **we can bring the brain's way of reading pain onto our side**. Put into daily life, it looks like this.

**Step 1. Reread pain as an alarm, not a breakdown**
Thinking "something is badly broken" turns the alarm up. **Pain neuroscience education**, which teaches that pain is a protective signal, has growing evidence for reducing musculoskeletal pain and the disability it causes (Louw et al., **Physiotherapy Theory and Practice**, 2016). Try putting it into words: "This is my body trying to protect me."

**Step 2. Pick one small action you can control**
The key to undoing helplessness is **the experience of control**. Choose something very small you can do even on painful days, such as a 5-minute walk, a warm compress or a stretch at a set time, and do it yourself. As small experiences of control add up, the brain learns "I can do this again."

**Step 3. Hold accurate expectations about treatment**
Asking what a treatment does and understanding it replaces vague worry with **accurate expectation**. But never delay or stop needed treatment because of placebo. Expectation is a force that helps treatment work, not one that replaces it.

## Conclusion

The placebo effect is real. It is not magic, though, but **a biological process in which expectation moves the brain's pain-control system**. Sham surgery eased knee pain as much as real surgery, and the brain released its own painkillers on expectation alone. So when pain comes, rather than panicking first, read it as "my body trying to protect me," and do one small thing you can control. **Expectation and hope are real forces that move the brain**, wherever you stand right now.

### 🙋 Frequently asked questions

#### Isn't placebo just "in your head" after all?

No. If it were only a feeling, a drug that blocks painkilling chemicals would have no reason to erase it. In fact, blocking endorphins removed placebo relief (Levine et al., **Lancet**, 1978), and brain scans showed activity in pain areas falling (Wager et al., **Science**, 2004). What placebo mainly changes, though, are **symptoms you feel**, such as pain, fatigue and nausea; there is no evidence that it changes the course of a disease.

#### Does it work if I know the pill is fake?

For some symptoms, yes. Ted Kaptchuk's team at Harvard openly told people with irritable bowel syndrome that they were taking placebo pills, and their symptoms still improved more than in a no-treatment group (Kaptchuk et al., **PLoS ONE**, 2010). This was done under medical supervision, however. Don't use it as a reason to change or stop your medication.
