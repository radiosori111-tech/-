If you gave up on a diet three days in, it's easy to blame weak willpower. But psychology has a theory that explains this failure in a completely different way: **identity-based motivation** (IBM), proposed by the American psychologist Daphna Oyserman.

> The real reason you gave up wasn't willpower; it just didn't feel like you.

The theory explains that when people move toward a goal, success or failure depends on how well the goal matches the way they see themselves. Goals like dieting, saving or studying keep collapsing not because you lack willpower, but because the behavior doesn't feel like "you."

In fact, many people repeat similar resolutions every new year and give up at around the same point. Identity-based motivation theory sees this repeated pattern not as personal weakness but as a structural problem: a mismatch between identity and behavior. Today let's walk through, step by step, what the theory actually says and how to apply it in everyday life.

## Who is Daphna Oyserman?

Daphna Oyserman is known as a scholar who has studied psychology and education together at the University of Southern California (USC). She has long researched motivation, self-regulation and social cognition, digging into why people push some goals through to the end and let others go easily.

The identity-based motivation theory she proposed is reportedly used widely not only in academia but also in education. Cases are often cited in which its principles are built into programs that boost students' motivation to learn or interventions that help teenagers plan their futures.

It may sound like complicated psychological jargon, but the core is simple: people choose their actions based on their sense of who they are, and if an action doesn't fit their self-image, they can't keep it up for long, no matter how good the goal.

## What happens when identity and behavior don't line up

Suppose someone who has always steered clear of exercise suddenly resolves to run every morning at dawn. They can hold out on willpower for the first few days, but as time goes on, a creeping sense of "this isn't really who I am" rises up.

According to identity-based motivation theory, this is exactly where giving up begins. The goal itself isn't bad; motivation drops sharply because the behavior needed to reach the goal clashes with the identity you hold.

Conversely, <strong>people who see themselves as "someone who moves their body at least a little every day" tend to keep up the same running habit far longer</strong>, because the identity is in place before the behavior.

## How you interpret the hard moments makes the difference

A particularly interesting part of this theory is how we interpret difficulty. Everyone hits hard moments on the way to a goal, and Oyserman explains that there are two ways to interpret that difficulty.

One is to take it as "If it's this hard, I must be doing something truly important." The other is to take it as "If it's this hard, it must not be for me." The same difficulty leads to completely different outcomes depending on how you interpret it.

People who take the first view see difficulty as a sign they're getting closer to the goal, so their motivation actually grows stronger, while people who take the second view hit the quit button right then. The same goes for hitting a plateau on a diet or seeing your grades stall while studying.

## Your strategy has to fit you too if it's going to last

Another pillar of identity-based motivation theory is identity-congruent strategy. Even for the same goal, the way you pursue it has to fit your disposition if you're going to keep it up.

If you enjoy being around people, a shared strategy such as group runs or a study group may suit you far better than working out alone at the gym. Conversely, forcing group activities on someone who's comfortable spending time alone can crush their motivation.

In the end, the practical message of this theory is that sustainable methods differ from person to person even for the same goal, and a method only takes root as a habit when it fits your identity.

## Ways to apply it right away in daily life

Applying this theory to real life is easier than you might think. First, <strong>rewrite your goals from "things I have to do" into sentences about "who I am."</strong> For example, <strong>instead of "I have to lose weight," say "I'm someone who takes care of my body."</strong>

Second, <strong>practice reinterpreting hard moments not as a signal to give up but as a sign that you're getting closer to your goal</strong>.

Third, <strong>it's important to find a way of doing things that suits your disposition, not just the method other people say is good</strong>.

Rather than making a grand resolution, starting with small goals that don't stray far from who you usually are can also be a good beginning. The core of this theory is that when identity changes first, behavior follows naturally.

## Why is this theory getting attention again now?

In an age overflowing with self-help content, advice that stresses willpower often wears people out instead. When you try hard and still don't reach your goal, you blame yourself, and that guilt makes the next attempt even harder, a vicious cycle that keeps repeating.

Identity-based motivation theory offers a way out of this cycle. It shifts how you think about the cause of failure from "Why is my willpower so weak?" to "Did this goal and method really suit me?"

Simply changing your perspective can change your attitude toward the same goal. If it's time to re-examine how you set goals, how you get through hard moments and how you choose strategies that suit you, this theory can be a good reference.

In the end, what matters may not be how fiercely you steel yourself, but how closely the goal connects with who you are now. Whatever goal you set next, it may help to ask yourself, at least once, whether it's connected to the person you really are.
