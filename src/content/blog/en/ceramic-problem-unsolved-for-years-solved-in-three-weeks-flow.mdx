## 📌 Key takeaways

> **The short answer:** Deep focus got its strength not only from holding on to one problem for a long time but also from **resting and sleeping while carrying it**. An **incubation** period away from the problem helped solve it, and a night's sleep boosted insight. The key to getting into deep focus is building notification-free focus time, light walks, and enough sleep into the same day.

- 🥚 **Time away grew the answer.** In a meta-analysis of 117 studies, an **incubation** period of setting the problem aside helped people solve it, especially after they had worked on it hard first (**Psychological Bulletin**, 2009).
- 😴 **Sleep more than doubled insight.** People who practiced a number task and then slept for a night were **more than twice as likely to discover a hidden rule** as those who stayed awake (**Nature**, 2004).
- 🚶 **Light mind-wandering helped creativity.** People who let their minds wander during an easy task solved creative problems better than those who did a hard task or simply rested (**Psychological Science**, 2012).
- 📱 **Switching tasks leaves thoughts behind.** Moving on before finishing a task left attention stuck on it, so people did worse on the next one (**Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes**, 2009).

### 💬 "How should I hold on to a problem I haven't solved in years?"

Some problems show no sign of a solution even after years of work. In the original version of this post, I summarized the experience that Professor **Hwang Nong-moon** of Seoul National University's Department of Materials Science and Engineering has described in his book *Immersion* (*Molip*) and in talks. He says that a ceramics research problem that had long resisted him as a researcher was solved in about three weeks, after he deliberately increased the time he spent thinking only about that one problem.

That's one person's experience, so it's hard to generalize from it as is. This time I looked at the idea that "holding on to a problem for a long time brings the answer" through experimental research. The research largely supported it, but it also pointed to one more important condition: **rest and sleep**.

## Deep Dive: how does deep focus work in the brain?

### 1. What exactly is deep focus?

In psychology, flow is a concept named by Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi: a state of being so absorbed in something that you lose track of time and self-consciousness fades (Csikszentmihalyi, *Flow*, 1990). A meta-analysis of studies found that flow appeared more often **when a task's difficulty matched one's skill**, and when goals were clear and feedback came quickly (Fong et al., **Journal of Positive Psychology**, 2015).

The deep focus Professor Hwang describes goes a step further, closer to "immersive thinking," keeping one problem in mind for days or weeks. Few studies have tested the effect of holding on for that long directly, but the principles involved have been confirmed in experiments.

### 2. Does stepping away from a problem really bring the answer?

My original post emphasized his training himself to "keep coming back to that problem the whole time he was awake." Research adds that **time spent stepping away** matters too.

- A team at Lancaster University in the UK analyzed 117 studies on whether an **incubation** period, working on something else for a while in the middle of a problem, helps people solve it (Sio & Ormerod, **Psychological Bulletin**, 2009). Incubation had a clear effect, especially for creative problems without a single right answer and **when people had worked on the problem enough before incubating**.
- A team at the University of California, Santa Barbara compared groups who, between rounds of a creative task, did an easy task that let their minds wander, did a hard task, or simply rested (Baird et al., **Psychological Science**, 2012). Only the group that **let their minds wander during an easy task** improved sharply when they returned to the problem they'd been working on.

In other words, "holding on for a long time" works better not as squeezing your brain nonstop, but when, after thinking hard enough, you do something light like taking a walk or washing dishes and **let the problem ripen in the back of your mind**.

### 3. Can sleep really find the answer?

Jan Born's team at the University of Lübeck in Germany had participants practice a tedious task of transforming number strings (Wagner et al., **Nature**, 2004). The task contained a **hidden rule** that made it much faster once noticed. People who **slept** for a night after practice were **more than twice as likely** to notice the rule as those who stayed awake for the same time.

While we sleep, the brain reorganizes what we learned during the day, and the interpretation is that in the process, connections invisible while awake can emerge. During weeks of holding on to a problem, the sleep in between may well have been working too.

The idea that training the way you think changes the brain also shows up in brain imaging research. See also [**the MRI study of pro gamers' trained brains**](/en/blog/pro-gamer-brain-mri-trained-brain/).

### 4. What gets in the way of deep focus?

My original advice to "cut down time spent on your phone or drifting into other thoughts" fits the research. Sophie Leroy of the University of Washington showed that switching to another task before finishing the first leaves **attention residue** on the first one, so people do worse on the next task (Leroy, **Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes**, 2009). Merely receiving phone notifications, without looking, increased errors on an attention task (Stothart et al., **Journal of Experimental Psychology: Human Perception and Performance**, 2015).

> What I wrote originally, that "anyone can reach this state through training," is Professor Hwang's claim, and I couldn't find research testing it on a large scale. So that it wouldn't read as "anyone can crack a tough problem in three weeks," I revised the post around principles confirmed by research: incubation, sleep, and reducing interruptions.

## The fix: a three-step daily deep-focus routine

**Step 1. Write the problem in one sentence and focus without notifications**
Write down the problem you want to hold on to in one sentence. Then put your phone in another room and think only about that problem for 25 to 50 minutes. Breaking the problem into pieces that are neither too easy nor too hard makes it easier to get absorbed.

**Step 2. Let the problem ripen while doing something light**
When the focus block ends, do something that doesn't take much brainpower, like walking, washing dishes, or showering. Even without forcing it, the problem keeps ripening in the back of your mind. Follow the routine below as is.

**Step 3. Write the problem down before bed and sleep well**
Before you fall asleep, write a short note of your thoughts so far and the questions left, and get a full night's sleep. Then start the next morning by looking at that note first. Sleeping on it and looking again may beat staying up all night holding on.

## Conclusion

Professor Hwang's account of solving a tough problem in three weeks is one person's experience, but the principles behind it are backed by research. Deep focus, though, wasn't squeezing your brain nonstop. Holding on long enough, letting go for a while, and falling asleep with the problem in mind were all part of it. If you have a worry you can't crack today, write it in one sentence, go for a walk, and write it down again before bed.

### 🙋 Frequently asked questions

#### Do I have to sit for hours to get into deep focus?

No. You can repeat 25 to 50 minutes of focus with short breaks; what matters is not letting go of the problem in your mind. If your focus keeps drifting, start by practicing coming back from mind-wandering. The 10-minute timer in [how to focus while reading](/en/blog/why-resolutions-keep-lasting-three-days-train-the-amcc-by-reading/) can help.

#### How is a little every day different from deep focus?

A little every day is good for making memories last, while deep focus is good for digging into one hard problem. You can use both. I covered the principle of spreading out study in [studying five minutes a day](/en/blog/five-minutes-a-day-why-the-brain-really-changes-neuroplasticity/).
