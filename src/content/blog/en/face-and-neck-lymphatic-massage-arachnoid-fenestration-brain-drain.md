For the first time in 250 years, the first gateway through which waste leaves the brain has been identified: tiny openings 4.5 µm across, called "arachnoid fenestrations." When I heard the news, I honestly slapped my knee. As someone who has done facial lymphatic massage every morning and evening, I thought, "So it really wasn't just skin care."

- <strong>A discovery 250 years in the making:</strong> arachnoid fenestrations, tiny openings 4.5 µm across, have been identified for the first time as the first gateway through which cerebrospinal fluid carrying brain waste exits.
- <strong>Aging and the brain's cleaning system:</strong> as we age, arachnoid fenestrations and meningeal lymphatic vessels shrink by more than half, sharply reducing how efficiently the brain clears waste.
- <strong>The promise of non-invasive treatment:</strong> animal experiments confirmed that delivering the VEGF-C gene to the nasal lining regenerated lymphatic vessels and restored cerebrospinal fluid drainage to youthful levels.

## The brain's self-cleaning system: its starting point finally revealed

The brain uses more energy than any other organ in the body, so it also produces a great deal of metabolic waste from cellular activity. The key player in clearing this waste is **cerebrospinal fluid** (CSF), the clear, colorless fluid surrounding the brain and spinal cord. It cushions the brain against outside impacts while also acting as a cleaner, carrying waste out of brain tissue.

About 500 mL of cerebrospinal fluid is newly produced each day, yet only about 150 mL actually exists in the **subarachnoid** space around the brain and spinal cord. In other words, cerebrospinal fluid is constantly being made while constantly draining away somewhere. But one part had long remained unknown: how cerebrospinal fluid passes through the arachnoid, the protective membrane tightly wrapping the brain, and where its first gateway lies.

A study published in the international journal **Cell** by the team of Director Gou Young Koh at the Center for Vascular Research of the Institute for Basic Science (IBS) filled exactly this 250-year-old gap (Source: Cell / IBS).

Combining special mice with fluorescently labeled lymphatic vessels and 3D imaging technology, the researchers precisely analyzed the arachnoid around the olfactory bulb (the smell-processing region at the front of the brain). They found that while the arachnoid was continuous elsewhere in the brain, here it was broken in many places and densely dotted with tiny openings averaging about 4.5 µm across. The researchers named this structure **arachnoid fenestrations**. Put simply, the thick protective membrane is punched with very small drain holes.

A similar structure was also confirmed in primates, not just mice. This suggests it is likely not an anatomical oddity but a functional structure conserved across mammals.

<strong>In short:</strong> with the first discovery of arachnoid fenestrations, the first gateway through which cerebrospinal fluid passes the arachnoid, the last piece of a 250-year-old puzzle about how the brain clears waste has fallen into place.

## Aging clogs the drains: can regenerating lymphatic vessels reverse it?

What impressed me most personally in this study was not the discovery of arachnoid fenestrations itself, but <strong>the results that put numbers on the changes with age</strong>. In <strong>mice aged 85 to 100 weeks, equivalent to old age in humans</strong>, the size and number of arachnoid fenestrations had <strong>decreased by 50 to 60%</strong> compared with young mice (8 to 10 weeks). The drainage passages themselves had narrowed by nearly half.

That's not all. <strong>Meningeal lymphatic vessels around the olfactory bulb decreased by 58%</strong>, and <strong>the passages through the cribriform plate (the thin bone between the brain and nasal cavity), through which lymphatic vessels and olfactory nerves pass, decreased by 64%</strong>. **Meningeal lymphatic vessels** are a network of lymphatic vessels in the membranes surrounding the brain; they absorb cerebrospinal fluid that has passed through the arachnoid fenestrations and carry it toward the lymph nodes in the neck. So there's a double blow: the drains (arachnoid fenestrations) clog, and the sewer pipes connected to them (meningeal lymphatic vessels) degenerate as well. Naturally, cerebrospinal fluid drains less efficiently, and the risk of waste building up in the brain rises.

Previous studies have consistently pointed to a link between degenerative brain diseases, including Alzheimer's disease, and declining waste clearance by cerebrospinal fluid (Source: Institute for Basic Science, IBS).

The researchers didn't stop there; they also tested the potential for treatment. They delivered the VEGF-C gene to the nasal lining of aged mice. **VEGF-C** stands for vascular endothelial growth factor C, a signaling protein that promotes the formation and regeneration of lymphatic vessels. The results were quite remarkable. The arachnoid fenestrations themselves didn't return to their original size, but meningeal lymphatic vessels around the olfactory bulb increased two- to threefold, and the amount of cerebrospinal fluid draining to the neck lymph nodes returned to the level of young mice. Achieving this with a non-invasive method, delivering the drug into the nose rather than by injection or surgery, is especially important for its potential clinical use.

That said, there's something I should point out honestly. Whether VEGF-C gene therapy actually slows cognitive decline or suppresses neurodegeneration still needs further verification. There is always a gap between animal studies and human trials. It's worth hoping for, but it's too early for overconfidence.

- <strong>Size and number of arachnoid fenestrations:</strong> about 50 to 60% lower in old mice
- <strong>Meningeal lymphatic vessels around the olfactory bulb:</strong> 58% lower with age; recovered two- to threefold after VEGF-C
- <strong>Passages through the cribriform plate:</strong> 64% lower with age
- <strong>Cerebrospinal fluid drainage:</strong> restored to young-mouse levels after VEGF-C

<strong>In short:</strong> aging degrades both the brain's waste outlets and its lymphatic vessels, but animal experiments confirmed that non-invasive nasal delivery of the VEGF-C gene can regenerate lymphatic vessels and restore drainage.

## When a medical discovery meets small everyday habits

The discovery of arachnoid fenestrations is a breakthrough that could change the paradigm of brain health. Although it will take time before gene therapy can be applied in daily life, simply understanding the structural mechanism by which brain waste drains out through the nasal cavity and the lymph nodes of the neck is meaningful in itself. Science has, in effect, shown that my small habits, massaging my face and neck every morning and evening to help lymphatic circulation and taking care to breathe deeply and stretch my neck, were never meaningless. Creating an environment in which the brain can clean itself may ultimately be the wisest first step to protecting brain health in an age when we live to 100.
