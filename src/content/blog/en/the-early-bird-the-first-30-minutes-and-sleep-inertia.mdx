## 📌 Key Takeaways

> **The short answer:** Feeling foggy right after waking is **sleep inertia**. For the first few minutes after waking, the brain did arithmetic worse **than after staying up all night**, and this state usually cleared within about 30 minutes. Fill the first 30 minutes with a light routine you set the night before, rather than important decisions or stimulating information.

- 😵 **The just-woken brain was duller than after an all-nighter.** Arithmetic ability 1–3 minutes after waking was worse than after more than 26 hours without sleep, with the steepest drop in the first few minutes (**JAMA**, 2006).
- 🧠 **The front of the brain woke up last.** Imaging blood flow right after waking showed the brainstem and thalamus woke up immediately, while the **prefrontal cortex**, which handles judgment, recovered more slowly (**Brain**, 2002).
- ⏰ **Sleep inertia usually lasts about 30 minutes.** In a review of studies, sleep inertia typically lasted 1–30 minutes and was longer after waking from deep sleep or when short on sleep (**Sleep Medicine Reviews**, 2000).
- 📈 **Cortisol rises on its own in the first 30 minutes after waking.** A 50–75% rise in **cortisol** within 30 minutes of waking was a normal response repeated every day (**Life Sciences**, 1997).

### 💬 "I woke at 5 a.m., so why does my day always start foggy?"

Honestly, for a long time I thought "what time you get up" was everything. I felt proud just to open my eyes at 5 a.m., yet I lay in bed scrolling KakaoTalk notifications and flipping through my Instagram feed. At the time I didn't know why my days always began in a fog even though I got up early.

Now, as soon as I get up, I brush my teeth, drink a cup of warm salt tea, then do **100 squats**. At first 100 sounded like a lot, but it actually took under five minutes. With the order set, the "what should I do?" question disappeared every morning, and even waking at 7, my day starts more solidly than my old dawn wake-ups.

In the original post I explained this change as "the phone disrupts the cortisol awakening response" and "finishing small goals releases dopamine." Looking up the original papers this time, the conclusion that the first 30 minutes matter held up, but the reasons were a bit different.

## Deep Dive: What happens in the brain right after you wake up?

### 1. How dull is the brain right after waking?

Much duller than you'd think. A US team had nine people sleep in a lab and repeatedly solve addition tasks for two hours starting right after waking (Wertz et al., **JAMA**, 2006).

- Arithmetic ability 1–3 minutes after waking was worse than **after more than 26 hours without sleep**.
- The steepest drop came in the first few minutes after waking, and full recovery of performance took longer.

The original post's description of "the time when judgment is lowest" matches this result. But it was a small study with nine participants. It's a good reason to postpone answering important emails or making big decisions right after waking.

### 2. Why doesn't the mind switch on as soon as you wake?

The original post described sleep inertia as "a transition where slow waves haven't fully turned into alpha and beta waves." Brain waves do change gradually, but the more important point is that brain areas wake up at different speeds.

A team at the Walter Reed Army Institute of Research in the US used PET to image blood flow in the brain right after waking and afterward (Balkin et al., **Brain**, 2002).

- Within five minutes of waking, areas responsible for arousal, like the brainstem and **thalamus**, quickly regained blood flow.
- But the **prefrontal cortex**, which handles judgment and planning, recovered later, slowly over about the next 15 minutes.

The feeling of having your eyes open but not thinking clearly comes from the areas that keep you awake already being on while the areas that judge aren't fully on yet.

### 3. How long does sleep inertia last?

According to a review of sleep inertia studies by a French team, sleep inertia typically lasted **1–30 minutes**. But waking from deep (slow-wave) sleep, or waking while short on sleep, made it longer and more severe (Tassi & Muzet, **Sleep Medicine Reviews**, 2000).

So the original post's "first 30 minutes" benchmark fits the research well. It also explains why mornings are harder when you've slept less. **Getting enough sleep before you wake**, more than waking early, is the foundation for reducing sleep inertia.

### 4. Does looking at your phone in the morning disrupt cortisol?

The **cortisol awakening response**, the rise in cortisol within 30 minutes of waking, is real. A University of Trier team in Germany confirmed that salivary cortisol rises an average of 50–75% within 30 minutes of waking, and that this response repeats quite consistently day to day (Pruessner et al., **Life Sciences**, 1997). As the original post explained, it's a normal response as the body prepares for the day.

But I couldn't confirm the original claim that "checking your phone right after waking disrupts the cortisol awakening response and causes anxiety and fatigue all day." I found no experiment directly testing morning phone use against the cortisol awakening response. Still, in light of the research in sections 1 and 2, putting off stimulating news or work messages during sleep inertia, when judgment is at its lowest, is a reasonable choice.

### 5. Does a morning routine reduce decision fatigue?

The original post said "willpower and judgment are resources used up over the day." When 23 labs replicated this "willpower depletion" theory together, the effect barely appeared (Hagger et al., **Perspectives on Psychological Science**, 2016). So the explanation that "making decisions in the morning drains your willpower" is corrected.

The real reason a set routine helps is different. Repeating the same action at the same time each day makes it gradually happen without thinking, which took about 66 days on average (Lally et al., **European Journal of Social Psychology**, 2010). During sleep inertia, when judgment is foggy, it helps to have an order your body remembers without deliberation. Also, I couldn't find direct human measurements behind the original claim that "finishing small goals releases a little dopamine." How mental fatigue changes choices through the day is covered separately in [Does Mental Fatigue Make You Give Up?](/en/blog/why-we-give-up-diets-and-tasks-the-brain-uses-20-percent-of-energy/).

### 6. Is morning salt tea OK?

The original post said warm salt tea replenishes electrolytes and wakes up the autonomic nervous system. Replacing water lost overnight with something warm is a fine habit, but I found no research testing the effects of morning salt tea. And since extra salt can raise blood pressure, if you have high blood pressure or heart or kidney disease, plain warm water or tea without salt is the safer choice.

## Solution: 3 Steps for the First 30 Minutes

**Step 1. The night before, set your morning order**
Before bed, decide the order of what you'll do in tomorrow's first 30 minutes. Mine is brushing my teeth, a cup of warm salt tea, and 100 squats. When judgment is foggy, just follow the order without deliberating. Try the routine below as is.

**Step 2. Put off important decisions and stimulating information for the first 30 minutes**
For the first few minutes after waking, judgment was foggier than after an all-nighter. Push back replies to important emails, big decisions and stimulating news by 30 minutes. Setting your alarm on a separate clock instead of your phone means you'll pick up your phone in bed less.

**Step 3. Sleep enough rather than waking early**
Sleep inertia was longer and worse the less people slept. Rather than moving your wake time earlier, set your bedtime first. Research showing that sleeping on a problem helps you find the answer is covered in [How to Get into Flow](/en/blog/ceramic-problem-unsolved-for-years-solved-in-three-weeks-flow/).

## Conclusion

More important than "the early bird catches the worm" was the fact that the brain right after waking isn't fully awake yet. A brain only minutes awake was duller than after an all-nighter, and the front of the brain, which handles judgment, woke up last. So fill the first 30 minutes with a pre-set routine you can do without deliberating, and start important work after that. Today, too, I open my day with brushing my teeth, a cup of salt tea and 100 squats.

### 🙋 Frequently Asked Questions

#### Does snoozing the alarm several times reduce sleep inertia?

It may do the opposite. Falling back asleep and waking again means going through sleep inertia once more, and it was worse when waking from deep sleep. Set the alarm once, and when it rings, sit up right away.

#### Does a morning routine have to include exercise?

No. What matters is a pre-set action that needs no judgment. A glass of water, opening a window or stretching is enough. How morning light helps your body rhythms is also covered in [Sunlight, Focus and Brain Function](/en/blog/sunlight-focus-and-brain-function/).
