## 📌 Key Takeaways

> **The short answer:** Burnout is hard to beat with willpower alone. In an analysis of studies on physicians, **changing the work environment and schedule** reduced burnout more than twice as much as approaches where individuals tried to steel themselves. Rather than adding more resolutions, change the conditions that wear you down one at a time, and decide your actions in advance with "if X, then I'll do Y" plans.

- 🔥 **Burnout comes from your relationship with work, not your personality.** It shows up as exhaustion, cynicism toward work, and a reduced sense of efficacy, and it is closely tied to job conditions such as workload, control, and reward (**World Psychiatry**, 2016).
- 🏥 **Changing the environment worked better.** In an analysis of 19 studies with 1,550 physicians, changing schedules or work structures reduced burnout more than twice as much as individual-focused approaches (**JAMA Internal Medicine**, 2017).
- 🔁 **If-then plans drove action better than resolutions.** In an analysis of 94 studies, people who decided in advance when, where, and what they would do followed through more than people who only set goals (**Advances in Experimental Social Psychology**, 2006).
- 💡 **Optogenetics won the 2026 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine.** This technique switches specific neurons on and off with light, and in animal experiments it showed precisely that behavior depends on the state of circuits rather than "persuasion" (**Nature Neuroscience**, 2005).

### 💬 "I pulled myself together every morning. Why did I keep getting more exhausted?"

At the height of my burnout, I told myself every morning, "Today I'll pull myself together." I spent months trying to change my thinking, but the lethargy and tension stayed, and I only grew more exhausted.

Things changed not through resolve but when I changed the conditions of my day. I went outside for about 20 minutes of sunlight before my coffee, and before exercising I spent 30 seconds picturing the feel of the muscles I was about to use. I also took magnesium and electrolytes. In other words, "changing the conditions first" worked better than "pulling myself together."

The original version of this post explained that experience through **optogenetics**, which won the 2026 Nobel Prize, saying "the brain needs a switch, not persuasion." When I looked up the original studies, the general direction held, but some of the analogy went too far and a few facts were wrong, so I've corrected them.

## Deep Dive: Why doesn't burnout lift with willpower alone?

### 1. What exactly is burnout?

The World Health Organization's International Classification of Diseases (ICD-11) classifies burnout not as a disease but as an **occupational phenomenon**. Christina Maslach of UC Berkeley, who has led burnout research for decades, describes three dimensions (Maslach & Leiter, **World Psychiatry**, 2016).

- **Exhaustion**: the feeling that your physical and mental energy has run out.
- **Cynicism**: growing distant from and indifferent to your work and the people in it.
- **Reduced efficacy**: the feeling that no matter how hard you try, you can't do the job well.

Maslach explains that burnout arises less from personal weakness than from a mismatch between the person and six job conditions: **workload, control, reward, fairness, community, and values**. If the conditions stay the same and you only steel your mind, it's easy to keep burning out in the same place.

### 2. Steeling yourself vs. changing the environment: which works better?

Changing the environment had a bigger effect. A team at the University of Manchester pooled 19 studies (1,550 participants) that tried to reduce burnout in physicians (Panagioti et al., **JAMA Internal Medicine**, 2017).

- Overall, the interventions reduced burnout slightly (effect size -0.29).
- **Individual-focused approaches**, such as mindfulness and stress-management training, had a small effect (-0.18).
- **Approaches that changed job conditions**, such as adjusting work schedules, reducing workload, and restructuring team meetings, had an effect more than twice as large (-0.45).

This doesn't mean personal effort is useless. It means that "I just need to get tougher" has limits, and that changing whatever conditions you can change was more effective. Because these studies focused on physicians, effect sizes may differ in other professions.

### 3. Is willpower a fuel that runs out the more you use it?

The "ego depletion" theory, which holds that willpower is like a fuel that gets used up, was once widely accepted. But when 23 labs repeated the experiment with the same protocol in more than 2,000 people, the effect was close to zero (Hagger et al., **Perspectives on Psychological Science**, 2016). I also covered this debate in the post on [three-day resolutions and identity-based motivation](/en/blog/why-diets-and-savings-plans-last-three-days-identity-based-motivation/).

Interestingly, people with good self-control weren't the ones who resisted temptation harder. A University of Pennsylvania team reported that people with high self-control scores got good outcomes thanks to **beneficial habits**, and that their outcomes had little to do with how much effort they spent resisting temptation (Galla & Duckworth, **Journal of Personality and Social Psychology**, 2015). What mattered was not the strength to hold out, but a structure that made holding out unnecessary.

### 4. Why do if-then plans work better than resolutions?

Instead of only setting a goal like "I'll exercise hard," you can make a **plan that ties a situation to an action in advance**, such as "If I walk through my front door after work, I'll put on my running shoes right away." This is called an **implementation intention**. A team led by Peter Gollwitzer at the University of Konstanz pooled 94 studies (more than 8,000 people) and found that people who formed implementation intentions followed through more than people who only set goals, with a medium-to-large effect size (0.65) (Gollwitzer & Sheeran, **Advances in Experimental Social Psychology**, 2006).

When the situation arrives, the action follows without deliberation, so you need to summon willpower less often. This is the closest thing to the "switch" the first version of this post talked about.

### 5. What did optogenetics show, and what didn't it show?

On October 5, 2026, the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine went to Karl Deisseroth, Peter Hegemann, and Georg Nagel, who opened up **optogenetics** (Nobel Committee announcement, 2026). Hegemann and Nagel found **channelrhodopsin**, a protein in single-celled pond algae that opens an ion channel when it absorbs light (Nagel et al., **PNAS**, 2003), and Deisseroth's team put this gene into neurons and showed they could switch them on with light at any moment (Boyden et al., **Nature Neuroscience**, 2005).

I'm correcting two points from the first version.

- **"Optogenetic therapy isn't used in people yet" was wrong.** In 2021, after a patient who had lost their sight to retinitis pigmentosa received optogenetic gene therapy, part of their vision returned, enough to locate and count objects with special goggles (Sahel et al., **Nature Medicine**, 2021). It is still at an early clinical stage, though, and is not used to treat brain disorders.
- **"Morning sunlight, visualization, and minerals switch on brain circuits like optogenetics" is only an analogy.** Optogenetics is a laboratory technique that shines light directly on cells carrying an inserted gene. There is no evidence that daily routines work the same way. Nor did the Nobel Prize "prove" my experience. What optogenetics showed is that behavior and emotion can change with the state of specific circuits.

### 6. How much is confirmed about morning sunlight, visualization, and minerals?

- **Morning sunlight**: Separate from vision, the retina has cells (ipRGCs) that pass light intensity to the brain's **circadian clock** (the suprachiasmatic nucleus) (Berson et al., **Science**, 2002). One study also found brain serotonin turnover was higher on brighter days (Lambert et al., **Lancet**, 2002). But the original claim that "many studies show 20–30 minutes of morning sunlight boosts dopamine and serotonin" was an exaggeration; only part of this has been confirmed directly in humans. I go into more detail in the post on [sunlight and focus](/en/blog/sunlight-focus-and-brain-function/).
- **Visualization before exercise**: In an analysis of 35 studies, **mental practice** (rehearsing a movement in your head) improved performance, but less than physical practice did (Driskell et al., **Journal of Applied Psychology**, 1994). The original claim that "the motor cortex can't tell real movement from imagined movement" was wrong. The two activate overlapping areas, but not identical ones.
- **Magnesium and electrolytes**: Magnesium is a mineral needed for electrical signaling in nerves and muscles, but I found no evidence that supplements reduce fatigue in people who aren't deficient. The change I felt may have come from the other habits I changed at the same time.

## Solution: 3 steps to change conditions instead of making resolutions

First, check which conditions are wearing you down right now. For each item you select, you'll see something you can try right away.

**Step 1. Find one condition you can change**
Among the conditions that wear you down, such as workload, schedule, or notifications, pick one you can change this week. If it's a condition you can't change alone, talking to your manager or team is the first step.

**Step 2. Turn resolutions into if-then sentences**
Instead of "I should exercise," write down a sentence that ties a situation to an action, such as "If I walk through my front door after work, I'll put on my running shoes right away." One a day is enough.

**Step 3. Restore your rhythm with morning light and light movement**
Go outside for morning sunlight, and take a gentle walk even on tired days. It helps set your circadian clock and ease fatigue.

## Conclusion

Looking back, the months I spent trying to pull myself together and ending up more exhausted were a predictable result. My burnout didn't continue because I was weak; it continued because the conditions wearing me down stayed the same. The research pointed to the same answer: changing conditions worked better than fixing my mindset, and tying situations to actions in advance worked better than repeating resolutions. Today, instead of making a resolution, pick one condition you can change.

### 🙋 FAQ

#### How is burnout different from depression?

Burnout refers mainly to exhaustion, cynicism, and reduced efficacy arising from your relationship with work, and the WHO classifies it as an occupational phenomenon rather than a disease. Depression is an illness in which interest and joy fade across your whole life, not just at work. The two often overlap, so if rest doesn't help and low mood lasts more than two weeks, get an assessment from a professional.

#### Can I recover from burnout without quitting my job?

In the research, burnout also decreased with approaches that changed conditions without leaving the job, such as adjusting schedules or workload. If it's hard to change things on your own, start by talking with your manager or team about adjusting your work.
