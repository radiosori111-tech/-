## 📌 Key takeaways

> **The short answer:** **Speech habits** and habitual emotional reactions become automatic through repetition in the same **situations**, and in the end those situations become the trigger. Because old reactions are covered over by new ones rather than erased, **habit reversal training**, which means noticing the trigger and repeatedly swapping in **a different action you can't do at the same time**, was effective.

- 🍿 **Habits are driven by the situation.** People who always ate popcorn at the movies ate week-old stale popcorn as much as fresh popcorn there, but ate less of the stale popcorn in a meeting room (**Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin**, 2011).
- 🧠 **Repeated behaviors shift to the basal ganglia.** A behavior first done with goals in mind is taken over by a different basal ganglia circuit with repetition and starts to happen automatically (**Nature Reviews Neuroscience**, 2006).
- 🔁 **Old reactions aren't erased; they're covered.** Even after a learned response had been "extinguished," it came back when people returned to the original situation (**Learning & Memory**, 2004).
- ✋ **Training to swap in another action worked.** Habit reversal training had large effects on habits such as tics, nail biting, and stuttering (**Clinical Psychology Review**, 2011).

### 💬 "It came out before I could even think"

Have you ever heard a sharp remark in a meeting and had a sharp reply fly out before you had time to think? Looking back later, you often wonder yourself why you reacted that way. In the original version of this post, I explained such reactions, like the knee-jerk reflex or Pavlov's dog, as **reflex circuits the brain has learned**.

The broad picture held up against the research. But the phrase "attitude isn't innate personality but an accumulation of reflexes" went too far, and "erasing old reflexes" was a bit different from what research shows. Below I lay it out again with evidence.

## Deep Dive: how are habitual reactions formed, and how do they change?

### 1. How are reflexes and habits different?

Pulling your hand back from a hot pot or your leg kicking up when your knee is tapped are **spinal reflexes**: they're handled right in the spinal cord without a round trip to the brain, which is why they're far faster than conscious thought. These reflexes are circuits we're born with.

**Conditioned reflexes**, by contrast, are built through experience. Russian physiologist Ivan Pavlov showed that when a sound was played every time dogs were fed, they eventually salivated at the sound alone (Pavlov, *Conditioned Reflexes*, 1927). My original comparison of your hand reaching for your pocket at a phone vibration to a conditioned reflex follows the same principle. Speech habits and habitual emotional reactions are close to learned responses like these that have hardened through daily repetition.

### 2. Where in the brain are repeated responses stored?

What I wrote originally, that "repeated behavior patterns are stored mainly in the **basal ganglia**," fits the research. According to a review of animal studies, a behavior first done by weighing "what will I get from this?" gets handed over with repetition to a different circuit within the basal ganglia (the dorsolateral striatum), so it starts to happen **automatically** in response to the situation without weighing the outcome (Yin & Knowlton, **Nature Reviews Neuroscience**, 2006). Ann Graybiel of MIT has reviewed how such entrenched habits and rituals are tied to the brain's evaluation circuits (Graybiel, **Annual Review of Neuroscience**, 2008).

### 3. What triggers a habitual reaction?

An experiment by Wendy Wood's team at the University of Southern California shows this well (Neal et al., **Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin**, 2011). The researchers gave people either freshly popped popcorn or **week-old stale popcorn** at a movie theater.

- People who **habitually** ate popcorn at the movies ate the stale popcorn as much as the fresh, even while saying it tasted bad.
- But the same people ate less of the stale popcorn when watching videos in a **meeting room**.
- People without the habit ate less stale popcorn regardless of where they were.

In other words, the habitual response was driven less by "I want to eat" and more by **a familiar situation**. The same tone popping out whenever you hear an objection in a meeting may happen because that situation has become the trigger.

### 4. Is attitude an accumulation of reflexes rather than personality?

In my original post I wrote that "much of our attitude is not innate personality but the result of reflex reactions repeated over a long time." That went a little too far. In a meta-analysis of twin and family studies, about **40%** of differences in personality traits were linked to genetics (Vukasović & Bratko, **Psychological Bulletin**, 2015). That doesn't mean personality is fixed, though. An analysis of 92 longitudinal studies found that traits such as conscientiousness and emotional stability changed meaningfully throughout adulthood (Roberts et al., **Psychological Bulletin**, 2006).

> So I changed "attitude is an accumulation of reflexes" to "attitude includes both inborn temperament and repeated habitual reactions, and the habitual part can be changed with practice."

### 5. Can old reactions be erased?

In my original post I wrote that "the surest way to erase an old reflex is to fill its place with a new one." The direction is right, but research shows old reactions **aren't erased**. Mark Bouton of the University of Vermont explained that "extinction," getting rid of a learned response, doesn't actually erase the original memory but **layers new learning on top of it** (Bouton, **Learning & Memory**, 2004). That's why old reactions easily come back in situations different from where extinction was learned, especially **the original situation**.

**Habit reversal training** puts this principle to use. You practice noticing the situations and cues that trigger the reaction, then repeatedly do an action you **can't do at the same time** as the original one (a competing response) in that moment, such as making a fist when you feel like biting your nails. In a meta-analysis, this training had **large effects** on habit problems such as tics, nail biting, and stuttering (Bate et al., **Clinical Psychology Review**, 2011).

### 6. Summary: what held up and what I corrected

| Repeated behaviors are stored mainly in the basal ganglia | True. With repetition, basal ganglia habit circuits take over | Review (Nat Rev Neurosci 2006) |

## The fix: three steps to change speech habits and habitual reactions

**Step 1. Write down the trigger situations**
For a week, jot down each time the reaction pops out: when, where, who you were with, and what you felt just before. Once you see a pattern, you'll see the trigger.

**Step 2. Choose an action you can't do at the same time**
Decide in advance what you'll do when the trigger comes. When a sharp remark is about to come out, actions you can't do at the same time as the original reaction work well, such as breathing out slowly with your mouth closed or asking "In what way?" Naming your feeling in that moment also helps (Lieberman et al., **Psychological Science**, 2007). Build your plan right below.

**Step 3. Repeat steadily in the same situation**
Because old reactions aren't erased, it's important to repeat the new action **in the original trigger situation** in particular. How long it took for a new behavior to become automatic varied, with a median of 66 days (Lally et al., **European Journal of Social Psychology**, 2010). If the old tone slips out once, you haven't failed; just do it again at the next chance.

## Conclusion

Speech habits and habitual emotional reactions aren't only about personality; they're also responses the brain has automated through repetition in the same situations. And the trigger was usually **the situation**. You can't erase old reactions all at once, but if you practice noticing the trigger and swapping in an action you can't do at the same time, repeating it in the original situation, the new response will cover the old one. This week, start by writing down the speech habit that pops out most often and what triggers it.

### 🙋 Frequently asked questions

#### If I change my speech habits, does my brain really change?

The principle of "neuroplasticity," that repeated words and thoughts change the brain's connections, is well established. But it takes steady repetition in the same situations, not just once or twice. How words affect the brain is covered next in [one word rewires the brain](/en/blog/one-word-rewires-the-brain-neuroplasticity-and-speech-habits/).

#### What should I do when anger makes me repeat the same mistake?

In a "hot" state of anger, judgment is pulled hard by emotion. Naming the feeling and doing a pre-decided action, such as saving the message as a draft and walking for 10 minutes, are covered in [why we repeat things we regret](/en/blog/if-you-keep-doing-things-you-regret-the-brain-acted-first/).
