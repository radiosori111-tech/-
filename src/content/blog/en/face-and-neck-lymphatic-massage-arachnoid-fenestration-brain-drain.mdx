## 📌 Key Takeaways

> **The short answer:** The evidence that lymphatic massage clears brain waste only goes as far as **mouse experiments** so far. Gently stroking mice's face and neck skin sent more cerebrospinal fluid out through neck lymphatics, but this hasn't been tested in people. The brain-cleaning habit with more evidence in people is **enough deep sleep**.

- 💧 **Cerebrospinal fluid drained behind the nose and into the neck.** In mice and monkeys, cerebrospinal fluid flowed through the **nasopharyngeal lymphatic plexus** behind the nose to neck lymph nodes, and this route weakened with age (**Nature**, 2024).
- 🖐️ **Stroking the neck increased drainage.** Gently stroking mice's face and neck skin increased cerebrospinal fluid drainage, and it worked in old mice too (**Nature**, 2025).
- 🧓 **The drainage route weakens with age.** Old mice had fewer **meningeal lymphatic vessels** and less cerebrospinal fluid drainage, and reviving the lymphatics improved memory task performance (**Nature**, 2018).
- 😴 **The brain was washed better during sleep.** When people fell into deep sleep, cerebrospinal fluid surged in large waves (**Science**, 2019), and in sleeping mice the spaces between brain cells widened by about 60%, letting waste drain better (**Science**, 2013).

### 💬 "Was my daily face lymphatic massage not just for my skin?"

I do face and neck **lymphatic massage** every morning and evening, along with deep breathing and neck stretches. Then I heard that Director Koh Gou Young's team at the Institute for Basic Science (IBS) Center for Vascular Research had uncovered, one after another, the routes brain waste drains through, and I thought, "So it wasn't just skin care."

This post originally summarized one piece of that news: the discovery of **arachnoid fenestrations**, which IBS introduced as a Cell paper. This time I checked the original papers for the same center's Nature studies too, and laid out how far research has answered the question "so does lymphatic massage help the brain?"

## Deep Dive: How does brain waste drain out?

### 1. What carries brain waste out?

Brain waste is carried by **cerebrospinal fluid**. Cerebrospinal fluid is the clear liquid around the brain and spinal cord, acting both as a cushion against shocks and as a route for removing waste. People make about 500 mL a day, but only about 150 mL is in the body at any time. As the original post explained, this means it's constantly draining somewhere as fast as it's made.

For a long time, the brain was thought to have no lymphatic vessels. But in 2015 a University of Virginia team discovered **meningeal lymphatic vessels** in the membranes around the brain (Louveau et al., **Nature**, 2015). Studies followed showing that cerebrospinal fluid drains through these vessels to lymph nodes in the neck.

### 2. What are arachnoid fenestrations?

According to IBS's announcement, the **arachnoid fenestrations** introduced in the original post are tiny holes about 4.5 µm across in the arachnoid membrane covering the brain, around the **olfactory bulb**, which handles smell. They're described as the first gateway through which cerebrospinal fluid passes the arachnoid toward the lymphatics. IBS's announcement said that in old mice these holes shrank 50–60% in size and number, and that delivering the **VEGF-C** gene into the nose increased nearby lymphatics two- to threefold and returned cerebrospinal fluid drainage to young-mouse levels.

However, I couldn't directly confirm the bibliographic details of the original paper for this study, so the numbers above are reported only as IBS's announcement. I also couldn't confirm the original post's phrase "a discovery 250 years in the making," so I removed it. Above all, these results all come from **mouse experiments**.

### 3. Does cerebrospinal fluid pass behind the nose and through the neck?

The same center has published several studies that can be confirmed from the original papers.

- In 2019, they showed in mice that meningeal lymphatics at the base of the skull are a main route for draining cerebrospinal fluid (Ahn et al., **Nature**, 2019).
- In 2024, they confirmed in mice and monkeys that cerebrospinal fluid gathers in the **nasopharyngeal lymphatic plexus** behind the nose before flowing to deep neck lymph nodes. With age, this plexus weakened (Yoon et al., **Nature**, 2024).

So the original post's explanation that "brain waste drains through the back of the nose and neck lymph nodes" fits the research.

### 4. Does stroking the face and neck really increase drainage?

This is the study closest to the original post. The same center focused on the shallow lymphatic vessels just beneath the skin of mice's face and neck (Jin et al., **Nature**, 2025).

- When mice's face and neck skin got a mechanical stimulus of **gentle, low-force stroking**, more cerebrospinal fluid drained to the neck lymph nodes.
- Even in **old mice**, whose cerebrospinal fluid drainage had declined, this stimulus increased drainage.

The original post's claim that "face and neck lymphatic massage isn't just skin care" now has evidence in mice. But there's **no evidence yet** that it reduces brain waste, protects memory or prevents dementia in people. Human skin thickness and lymphatic depth differ from mice, and trials in people haven't come out yet.

### 5. Does the brain's drainage route clog with age?

The original post's emphasis that "aging clogs the drain" showed up similarly across several studies. A University of Virginia team confirmed that in old mice, meningeal lymphatics shrink and less cerebrospinal fluid drains. Giving **VEGF-C**, which grows lymphatics, improved drainage and memory task performance, while mice with damaged lymphatics accumulated more **amyloid**, which is linked to Alzheimer's disease (Da Mesquita et al., **Nature**, 2018).

But this too was a mouse experiment, and VEGF-C gene therapy isn't used in people. As the original post put it, "hope, but it's too early for overconfidence."

### 6. What brain-cleaning habit has the most evidence in people?

**Sleep**.

- A University of Rochester team showed that when mice fall asleep, the spaces between brain cells widen by about **60%**, and cerebrospinal fluid flows through them, clearing waste like amyloid faster than when awake (Xie et al., **Science**, 2013).
- A Boston University team used MRI to show that when people fall into deep (non-REM) sleep, brain waves slow and cerebrospinal fluid surges into the brain in large, regular waves (Fultz et al., **Science**, 2019).

It wasn't a study measuring how much waste drains from the human brain, but the close link between cerebrospinal fluid flow and sleep has been confirmed in people too. ## Solution: 3 Steps to Support the Brain's Drainage Route

**Step 1. Sleep deeply for more than 7 hours every night**
This has the most evidence in people. Set your bedtime first, and cut screens and bright light in the hour before bed. Cerebrospinal fluid moved the most during deep sleep.

**Step 2. Stroke the face and neck gently, from top to bottom**
If you massage, rather than pressing hard, slowly stroke from in front of the ears and along the jawline down the side of the neck toward the collarbone, just lightly moving the skin. The mouse experiment also used a low-force stimulus. But keep in mind that the effect on the brain hasn't been confirmed in people. Follow the routine below.

## Conclusion

Animal research has made it fairly clear that brain waste is carried by cerebrospinal fluid out through lymphatics behind the nose and in the neck, and that this route weakens with age. The finding that gently stroking mice's face and neck increased drainage adds an intriguing meaning to the lymphatic massage I do every day. But we don't yet know whether the same holds for the human brain. Enjoy massage gently, but the surest habit for your brain is sleeping deeply enough tonight.

### 🙋 Frequently Asked Questions

#### Is lymphatic massage more effective the harder I press?

No. Shallow lymphatic vessels sit just beneath the skin, and the mouse experiment also used gentle, low-force stroking. Pressing hard can irritate or bruise the skin. Avoid pressing hard on the carotid artery area at the front of the neck.

#### Can lymphatic massage prevent dementia?

There's no evidence to say that yet. There's still a big gap between increased cerebrospinal fluid drainage in mice and preventing dementia in people. Lifestyle habits like exercise, blood pressure control and enough sleep have more evidence for dementia prevention.
