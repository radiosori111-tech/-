## 📌 Key takeaways

> **The short answer:** Five minutes a day works because, for the same amount of study, you remember longer when you **spread it over several days**. But cramming feels like better learning, so many people do the opposite. The key is a little every day, **recalling first** with the book closed, and keeping the chain unbroken.

- 📅 **Spreading it out sticks better.** In a meta-analysis of 317 experiments, even with the same study time, memories lasted longer when study was **spaced out** rather than crammed (**Psychological Bulletin**, 2006).
- 🤔 **But people felt the opposite.** Those who learned painters' styles spaced out did better on the test, but most participants said they **learned better** when it was crammed (**Psychological Science**, 2008).
- 🧠 **Recalling beats rereading.** People who **tried to recall** a passage remembered more a week later than people who reread it (**Psychological Science**, 2006).
- 🤹 **Stop practicing and the brain goes back.** Three months of juggling practice increased gray matter in part of the brain, and after three months off it shrank again (**Nature**, 2004).

## Deep Dive: why does a little every day work?

### 1. Is cramming really different from spreading it out?

In the first version of this post, I quoted an English-teaching expert who said "repeating a set amount every day is far more likely to move into long-term memory than cramming all at once." This matches one of the longest-standing and most often confirmed findings in psychology: the **spacing effect (distributed practice)**.

- A team at the University of California, San Diego analyzed 317 experiments (Cepeda et al., **Psychological Bulletin**, 2006). Even with the same study time, memories lasted longer when study was **spaced out** rather than crammed into one session.
- The same team ran an experiment with 1,354 people, varying the gap between study sessions and the time until the test (Cepeda et al., **Psychological Science**, 2008). The longer you wanted to remember something, the more effective it was to **widen** the review gaps.

### 2. So why do we cram?

Here's the surprising part. A team from Williams College and UCLA had participants learn the styles of 12 painters, comparing showing one painter's works in a block with mixing several painters and spreading them out (Kornell & Bjork, **Psychological Science**, 2008). The spread-out group did better at identifying the painter of new paintings. But most participants said they **learned better when the paintings were blocked**.

Cramming makes things feel smoothly familiar in the moment, so you get the feeling "I know this," but that feeling isn't the same as lasting memory. Spreading it out means each time you come back you have to recall something you've partly forgotten, so it feels like you learned less, yet that very effort is what makes memories stronger.

### 3. Why is recalling better than rereading?

What you do when you space it out matters too. A team at Washington University in St. Louis had students read a short passage, then had one group reread it and another close the book and write down what they remembered (Roediger & Karpicke, **Psychological Science**, 2006). Five minutes later the rereading group did slightly better, but **a week later** the recall group remembered much more. This is called **retrieval practice**.

The first version also quoted a professor who said that "sloppy notes actually weaken memory," and there's related research. A well-known study found that students who typed lectures verbatim on laptops did worse on conceptual questions than those who summarized by hand (Mueller & Oppenheimer, **Psychological Science**, 2014), but later replication studies didn't find a clear difference (Morehead et al., **Educational Psychology Review**, 2019). What seems to matter more than the tool is **whether you put it in your own words**. The "generation effect," in which information you produce yourself is remembered better, was confirmed across 86 studies (Bertsch et al., **Memory & Cognition**, 2007).

### 4. Do repeated habits really change brain structure?

The **neuroplasticity** idea in the first version, that "the brain keeps changing its circuit connections in response to repeated stimulation regardless of age," is true. A team at the University of Regensburg in Germany had half of 24 adults who had never juggled practice juggling for three months (Draganski et al., **Nature**, 2004). Those who practiced showed **increased gray matter** in brain regions involved in seeing motion.

Even more interesting is what came next. After three months without practice, the gray matter that had grown **shrank again**. This supports my original point that "a burst of effort for a day or two and then quitting makes it hard for change to take hold." But there's no study yet showing that exactly five minutes a day changes brain structure. What matters is less the length of time than **unbroken repetition**.

## The fix: three steps to make five minutes a day stick

**Step 1. Small enough not to fail, at the same time each day**
As the first version suggested, choose a small unit, such as 10 words, three lines of notes, or 10 pages a day, and do it at the same time and place every day. When the same situation repeats, the situation itself becomes the habit cue. The median time for a new habit to run on its own was 66 days (Lally et al., **European Journal of Social Psychology**, 2010).

**Step 2. Don't reread; close the book and recall first**
When you review, don't open the book first. Write down or say what you remember, then check. The moment you struggle to recall is what strengthens the memory.

**Step 3. Widen the gaps between reviews**
Recall what you learned today again tomorrow, in three days, in a week, in two weeks, and in a month. You can plan review dates for what you learned today right below.

## Conclusion

Five minutes a day works not because of willpower but because of how memory works. Even with the same amount of study, spreading it out lasts longer, but cramming feels like better learning, so we keep doing the opposite. Don't be fooled by that feeling: start small today, and tomorrow close the book and try to recall first. Keep the chain unbroken, and the brain changes you build through practice will stay put.

### 🙋 Frequently asked questions

#### What if a test is right around the corner and I have to cram?

Even when time is short, several short sessions beat one long one. Split it into morning, afternoon, and evening on the same day, and add recall practice with the book closed each time. If your focus keeps drifting while you study, try the 10-minute timer in [how to focus while reading](/en/blog/why-resolutions-keep-lasting-three-days-train-the-amcc-by-reading/).

#### Isn't five minutes too short?

Starting with long sessions makes it hard to get past day three. Five minutes is a size for *starting* the habit, and it grows naturally once it feels familiar. How consistency builds up in the brain, as with exercise, is also covered in [brain health and the compound effect of exercise habits](/en/blog/brain-health-plasticity-exercise-habits-compound-effect/).
