## 📌 Key takeaways

> **The short answer:** It isn't that the brain rejects new things. Sleepiness comes mainly from built-up sleep pressure and low arousal, and the reluctance comes from the brain treating mentally demanding work as a cost. Once repetition hands the task to habit circuits, it gets easier.

- 🔋 **The brain is built to save energy.** At about 2% of body weight, it uses about 20% of the body's energy (**PNAS**, 2002), so it tries to avoid costly work whenever it can.
- 🧩 **Mentally demanding work is meant to feel tiresome.** Given the same reward, people consistently chose the option that required less thinking (**Journal of Experimental Psychology: General**, 2010).
- 🔁 **Familiar actions are handed to habit circuits.** Repeated behaviors shift to habit circuits in the **basal ganglia** that need less deliberation, running on their own with less energy (**Annual Review of Neuroscience**, 2008).
- 🌱 **That's why the start is hardest.** New behaviors took anywhere from 18 to 254 days to become automatic (**European Journal of Social Psychology**, 2010). Feeling it's hard is less evidence of laziness than a sign that **your brain is changing**.

### 💬 "I open a book and get sleepy. Does that mean I'm not smart?"

I remember a day when I'd heard that "books are food for the mind" and that reading makes you bigger inside, so I opened a book with real resolve. A few pages in, my eyelids grew heavy. Then came the thoughts: "Maybe books just aren't for me," "They say effort is a talent too, and maybe I don't have it."

The first few months at a new job feel similar. The joy of being hired doesn't last long, and worries that begin with "Can I really do this?" lead from one to the next. Yet over time the unfamiliar people and rules become familiar, and one day the tension of being the new hire is gone. This post looks at what happens in the brain in between, and how to get through that uncomfortable stretch, based on research.

## Deep Dive: why new things feel especially hard

### 1. The brain is the body's most expensive organ

Marcus Raichle's team at Washington University in St. Louis summed it up: the brain is only about 2% of body weight but uses about **20%** of the body's energy (Raichle & Gusnard, **PNAS**, 2002). Because it is so costly, the brain works to save energy whenever it can. Thinking and choosing in familiar ways is not the brain being lazy; it is **a default setting that protects efficiency**.

### 2. Mentally demanding work feels like a "cost"

Matthew Botvinick's team at Princeton had people repeatedly choose between two tasks (Kool et al., **Journal of Experimental Psychology: General**, 2010). One switched rules often and took a lot of thinking; the other was simple. When the reward was the same, people **consistently chose the one that took less thinking**.

Researchers later pulled such results together, concluding that the brain counts mental effort as a kind of **cost** and spends it only when it seems worth it (Shenhav et al., **Annual Review of Neuroscience**, 2017). Feeling that something new is a chore means this cost accounting is at work.

### 3. With repetition, habit circuits take over

Ann Graybiel's team at MIT, drawing on animal and human studies, described how a repeated behavior is first handled by planning circuits and then gradually taken over by habit circuits in the **basal ganglia** (Graybiel, **Annual Review of Neuroscience**, 2008). When you first learn to drive you have to think about each mirror, signal and brake; once it's familiar, you can drive while holding a conversation.

Adapting to a new job works much the same way. At first you handle unfamiliar rules and people consciously, one by one; with repetition, the brain accepts that environment as **its new default**. That is what my original post meant by "**the brain has left its old environment and adapted to a new system**."

### 4. Why you get sleepy when you open a book: is the brain resisting?

My original post said "when you do something new, the brain gives you sleep as a gift." It's a relatable phrase, but research points to more concrete reasons for sleepiness.

- **Sleep pressure**: The longer you're awake, the more **adenosine** builds up in the brain, bringing on drowsiness (Porkka-Heiskanen et al., **Science**, 1997). If you're short on sleep, that pressure shows the moment you sit quietly and read.
- **Low arousal**: Sitting still and focusing on one thing tends to lower the body's level of alertness.
- **The cost of effort**: Unfamiliar text takes a lot of thinking, so the brain's cost accounting sends a "let's stop" signal.

Either way, **"I'm not smart enough" is not the answer.** Sleepiness is a sign either that you need sleep or that your brain is doing expensive work.

## The plan: 3 steps to help yourself adapt instead of blaming yourself

**Step 1. Figure out what's behind the sleepiness**
If you get sleepy every time you try something new, first check whether **you're getting enough sleep**. The American Academy of Sleep Medicine and the Sleep Research Society recommend **7 or more hours a night** for adults (Watson et al., **Sleep**, 2015). If you're sleeping enough and still drowsy, change how you're sitting, read aloud, or get up and move briefly every 20 to 30 minutes.

**Step 2. Make the cost of starting small**
The brain avoids costly work, so make the start **very small**: "two pages" rather than "a whole book," "putting on running shoes" rather than "an hour of exercise." And repeat it at **the same time and the same place**. The more consistent the context, the sooner habit circuits take over.

**Step 3. Start again with kindness instead of blame**
In experiments by Juliana Breines's team at UC Berkeley, people who were encouraged to be **kind to themselves** after recalling a mistake were more motivated to make amends and studied longer before a test than those who weren't (Breines & Chen, **Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin**, 2012). "My brain is changing" gets you to try again more than "What's wrong with me?"

## Conclusion

Feeling tired and sleepy when you start something new is not because you're lazy or untalented. It's because an energy-saving brain is doing **expensive work**, and the task hasn't yet been handed to habit circuits. So instead of blaming yourself, make the start small, repeat it at the same time, and speak kindly to yourself. Just as a new job eventually feels familiar, **a new system eventually becomes the brain's default.** Our brains are changing even now.

### 🙋 Frequently asked questions

#### Can the brain really change in adulthood?

Yes. Adults who had never juggled practiced for three months and gained gray matter in areas that track motion (Draganski et al., **Nature**, 2004). But it shrank back when they stopped, so keeping the change takes consistency. More research is gathered in [Do successful people have different brains?](/en/blog/the-secret-of-success/)

#### How long does it take to adapt to a new environment?

It varies widely by person and behavior. The time for a new daily behavior to become automatic ranged from 18 to 254 days, with a median of 66 days (Lally et al., **European Journal of Social Psychology**, 2010). Missing a day here and there made little difference to the process, so aim for **coming back** rather than perfection.
