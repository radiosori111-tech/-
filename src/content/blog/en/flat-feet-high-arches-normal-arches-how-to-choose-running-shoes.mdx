## 📌 Key takeaways

> **The short answer:** Having flat feet doesn't mean you must wear **stability shoes**. Choosing running shoes to match arch type left injury rates about the same. Use your arch as a guide when shopping, but the final test is to try on several pairs and pick **the most comfortable one**.

- 🦶 **Running puts about 2.8 times your body weight on your feet.** When runners struck the ground, peak force reached about 2.8 times body weight (**Journal of Biomechanics**, 1980).
- 👟 **Arch-matched shoes didn't reduce injuries.** Marine Corps recruits given shoes by sole shape had injury rates **similar** to recruits in the same shoe regardless (**American Journal of Sports Medicine**, 2010).
- ⚠️ **Very low or very high arches got hurt a bit more.** Among 449 special-forces trainees, both flat feet and high arches carried a higher risk of overuse injury (**AJSM**, 1999).
- 😌 **The standard is comfort.** Researchers proposed a "comfort filter": pick the most comfortable shoe out of several (**British Journal of Sports Medicine**, 2015).

### 💬 Do you look at brand and design first when buying running shoes?

Many people look at brand or design first when buying new running shoes. When I first wrote this post, I recommended checking your **foot arch** before anything else and said that choosing stability shoes for flat feet and maximally cushioned shoes for high arches could "significantly" reduce the strain on knees and ankles.

It's advice you often hear in running stores, so I took it at face value. But looking at the research this time, the evidence that choosing shoes by arch type alone reduces injuries was weak. So I kept the method for checking your arch but corrected the standard for choosing shoes.

## Deep Dive: what has research confirmed about arches and running shoes?

### 1. What does the foot arch do when you run?

The foot arch is the bow-shaped curve on the inside of the sole. When you land, it flattens slightly and springs back, spreading out the impact. Running repeats much larger forces than walking. In a study that measured the landing forces of 17 runners, the force at each foot strike peaked at about **2.8 times body weight** (Cavanagh & Lafortune, **Journal of Biomechanics**, 1980).

Arch shape isn't completely unrelated to injury. In a study of 449 US Navy special-forces trainees, those with **very low arches (flat feet)** and **very high arches** had a somewhat higher risk of overuse injury than those with normal arches (Kaufman et al., **American Journal of Sports Medicine**, 1999).

### 2. So do shoes matched to your arch reduce injuries?

This is where the research didn't match expectations.

- A US military team photographed the soles of Marine Corps recruits and gave motion-control shoes to flat-footed recruits, stability shoes to those with normal arches, and cushioned shoes to those with high arches. Compared with a group given stability shoes regardless of foot shape, **injury rates were similar** (Knapik et al., **American Journal of Sports Medicine**, 2010).
- In a study that gave 927 beginner runners the same neutral shoe and followed them for a year, people whose feet rolled inward, called **pronation (overpronation)**, did not have a higher risk of injury (Nielsen et al., **British Journal of Sports Medicine**, 2014).

> In my original post I wrote that "if flat-footed people wear shoes with nothing but cushioning, their feet wobble more and tire faster," and that "just choosing running shoes that suit your arch can significantly reduce the strain on knees and ankles." Since arch-matched shoes didn't change injury rates in the studies above, I corrected those statements. Stability shoes are fine if they feel comfortable, but they're not a must.

### 3. Then what should you choose shoes by?

Reviewing decades of research, Benno Nigg's team at the University of Calgary proposed two standards in place of the idea that shoes should "correct" foot motion (Nigg et al., **British Journal of Sports Medicine**, 2015). One is a shoe that doesn't get in the way of the path your body naturally wants to move along. The other is the "comfort filter": try on several pairs and pick **the one that feels most comfortable**, since a shoe that feels comfortable usually interferes less with your natural movement.

And in a study that followed 264 runners, those who **rotated several pairs** of running shoes had about a 39% lower risk of injury (Malisoux et al., **Scandinavian Journal of Medicine & Science in Sports**, 2015).

### 4. Should you replace running shoes every 300 to 500 km?

In my original post I wrote that "300 to 500 km is usually when it's time to replace them." It's true that midsole cushioning wears down with use, but I couldn't find research showing that replacing shoes at that distance reduces injuries. How fast shoes wear also varies with shoe weight, body weight, and running surface. So rather than a number, it's more practical to treat these as signs it's time: **the sole is badly worn on one side, the cushioning feels flatter than before, or pain starts at a distance that used to be fine**.

## The fix: three steps to choosing running shoes that fit your feet

**Step 1. Check your arch at home (as a rough guide)**
Wet the soles of your feet and stand naturally on dry paper. Pick the footprint below that looks similar, and you'll see what to check when trying on shoes. Also look at whether the soles of your old sneakers are unusually worn on one side.

<ArchPicker client:visible lang="en" />

**Step 2. Try on several pairs in a store**
Go in the **afternoon or evening**, when your feet swell slightly, wearing the socks you usually run in. Try on three or four pairs, including ones suited to your arch type, and walk or jog lightly in them. Check that your heel doesn't slip, the sides don't squeeze, and there's about a finger's width of space in front of your big toe.

**Step 3. The most comfortable pair, and ideally two**
Pick **the most comfortable pair** of the bunch. If you can, rotating between two pairs also helps. Think about replacing them when the sole is badly worn on one side or the cushioning feels flat.

## Conclusion

Knowing your arch is a good starting point for choosing running shoes, but the evidence for picking shoes by it alone was weak. Arch-matched shoes didn't change injury rates, and the standard researchers proposed was, in the end, **a shoe that feels comfortable on your feet**. Next time you buy running shoes, keep your arch type in mind as a guide, try on several pairs in the afternoon, and pick the most comfortable one.

### 🙋 Frequently asked questions

#### Do arch-support insoles help with flat feet?

Insoles can sometimes ease symptoms when you're in pain, but they don't change the shape of your arch. If you're running without pain, you don't need them. If you are in pain, it's safest to decide after a gait analysis by a professional. Check how fast you're increasing distance too; there's a self-check in [beginner runner knee pain](/en/blog/running-for-brain-health-beginner-knee-pain-and-the-wrong-shoes-for-your-arch/).

#### Is warming up before a run as important as shoes?

Spending the first five minutes walking or jogging very slowly helps your body get ready to run. I covered good moves for before and after running in [the 5-minute warm-up and cool-down](/en/blog/skip-the-5-minute-warm-up-and-cool-down-and-you-halve-runnings-brain-benefits/).
