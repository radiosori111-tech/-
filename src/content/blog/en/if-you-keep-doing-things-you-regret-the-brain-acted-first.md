> The thing you'll regret,
> *your brain had already done first.*

Why do we keep making the same choice in the moment even though we know we'll regret it later? We regret ordering a late-night snack, regret words blurted out in anger, procrastinate, pull an all-nighter before the deadline and regret it again. Curiously, neuroscientists explain that this pattern isn't a willpower problem but a problem of the order in which the brain works.

The recent brain-science book *The Betrayal of Thought* covers a similar idea: worrying ahead about what's to come, or straining to change a thought that's already surfaced, actually clouds your judgment. To prevent actions you'll regret, rather than squeezing out willpower, you need to change the very order in which the brain makes decisions.

Today I've summarized what actually happens in the brain, and the concrete methods you can use in the moment, so you stop repeating choices you'll regret. I'll focus on what you can apply right now rather than on difficult terminology.

> It wasn't willpower; it was a matter of order.

## Why can't you see the regret coming in the moment?

When the brain makes decisions, it uses two main pathways. One is the **amygdala** pathway, in charge of emotions and instincts; the other is the **prefrontal cortex** pathway, which reasons things through. The problem is that the two don't move at the same speed.

The amygdala reacts almost instantly, but it takes the prefrontal cortex much longer to analyze the situation and hit the brakes. So swiping your card in a fit of anger or sending a text while you're upset has actually already happened before the prefrontal cortex has a chance to step in.

The more intense the emotion, the wider this time gap grows. Psychology calls this a hot state, and it's exactly why decisions made when you're hungry, angry or sleepy are especially likely to end in regret.

![](/images/blog/3430ac5fca/02.webp)

## The real reason the amygdala moves first

The amygdala stepping forward first is actually a design for survival. If you met a predator and waited for the prefrontal cortex to finish analyzing the situation, it would already be too late. The problem is that most threats we face today aren't predators but KakaoTalk notifications and spending sprees in a fit of anger.

The instant reward of an impulsive choice, such as a late-night snack or an impulse buy, quickly releases **dopamine**. The benefits of resisting, on the other hand, are felt only hours or days later, so the brain easily leans toward the immediate reward.

Once you understand this structure, there's less reason to blame yourself for regrettable actions as a sign of weak willpower. Instead, checking first whether you're hungry, angry or tired becomes a far more practical response.

## What changes when you name your emotion

One method often mentioned in brain-science interviews is that <strong>the moment you recognize what this emotion is, the situation changes</strong>. Studies have found that simply naming it inwardly when you're angry, "I'm angry right now," calms the amygdala's excitement.

This is called <strong>affect labeling</strong>, and it's simple: just before doing something you might regret, say the emotion you're feeling in one word. <strong>Irritation, anxiety, hurt, for example. This brief moment buys the prefrontal cortex time to step in.</strong>

When you actually try it, it seems trivial but works surprisingly well. The moment you put an emotion into words, the brain starts treating it as something to analyze, delaying the impulsive reaction by a beat.

## Three concrete habits that prevent regret

The first is <strong>the 10-minute rule</strong>. When you want to impulsively buy, send or decide something, put it off for just 10 minutes. The excitement the amygdala creates usually subsides within a few minutes, so if you still want to do it after 10 minutes, it's a real need; if not, it was most likely an impulse.

The second is <strong>deciding in advance</strong>, so-called <strong>implementation intentions</strong> of the form "if this happens, I'll do that."

For example, <strong>if you pair situations and actions ahead of time, such as "When I'm angry, I'll go for a walk before sending any text,"</strong> then when the moment comes, your body moves automatically without having to deliberate anew.

The third is <strong>precommitment</strong>: <strong>setting up an environment in advance that makes the regrettable action hard to do in the first place</strong>. If late-night snacks are the problem, delete the delivery app; <strong>if impulse buying is the problem, go out with cash only instead of a card</strong>. Rather than spending willpower at the moment of decision, you cut down the options from the start.

## Why meditation calms the amygdala

Several recent interviews have mentioned that the harder people have been running without rest, the more their brains need a break as well. Brain scientists from Yale, Harvard and MIT who studied the brains of meditators reportedly concluded that the regions responsible for the brain's cravings and reactivity are far less active than usual.

You don't need to sit for an elaborate meditation every day. Taking even five minutes a day to close your eyes and focus only on your breathing is known to reduce how often the amygdala overreacts to minor stimuli. Reducing regrettable actions is, in the end, also a matter of how calm you can keep your amygdala.

At first meditation felt like grand spiritual training to me, so I kept putting it off. But when I tried turning off the lights for just five minutes before bed and focusing only on my breathing, I clearly felt my mood swings decrease the next morning. A short routine you can repeat every day lasts longer than a grand method.

## If you already regret something, try handling it this way

No matter how careful you are, you can't completely prevent actions you'll regret. If it's already happened, <strong>reinterpretation works better than self-blame</strong>. <strong>Simply writing down briefly why you made that choice and what your emotional state was at the time reportedly lowers the odds of repeating the same mistake</strong>.

Cognitive behavioral therapy calls this <strong>cognitive restructuring</strong>, and you can try it on your own without formal counseling. <strong>The key is not to stop at "Why did I do that?" but to go on to write "What signal could help me notice next time?"</strong>

<strong>Regret isn't an emotion to get rid of; it's closer to data that makes your next choice better</strong>. Simply building the habit of recording moments of regret noticeably raises the odds of making a different choice in the same situation.

In the end, the way to avoid regrettable actions wasn't squeezing out more willpower but <strong>understanding the order in which the brain reacts and stepping into that gap</strong>. I recommend putting even one of today's methods into practice this week.
