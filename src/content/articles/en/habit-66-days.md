"Stick with it for 21 days and it becomes a habit." It's a line you see often in self-help books and app ads. Three weeks seems like just the right length for a challenge, but if you still don't want to put on your running shoes on day 21, many people conclude, "I guess I just don't have the willpower." Before reaching that conclusion, it's worth checking where this number came from.

## Where the number 21 came from

The number is said to have spread from *Psycho-Cybernetics*, a 1960 book by Maxwell Maltz, a former plastic surgeon. He observed that patients seemed to need **a minimum of** about 21 days to get used to a new face or situation after surgery. It was an impression drawn from clinical experience rather than the result of a controlled experiment, and over time the word "minimum" disappeared.

## What the measurements showed: a median of 66 days, a range of 18 to 254

The best-known study that actually tracked how long habits take to form is a 2010 paper by a team at University College London (Lally et al., 2010). The researchers asked 96 participants to each choose one new behavior to do in the same situation every day, such as "drink a bottle of water with lunch" or "run for 15 minutes after breakfast," and to record every day for 12 weeks how **automatic** the behavior felt.

The results can be summed up in three points.

- **The curve was not a straight line.** Automaticity rose quickly in the first few weeks, then gradually leveled off as it neared its peak.
- **The median time to reach 95% of the peak was 66 days.** This figure was based on participants whose data fit the model well, and **the range was very wide, from 18 to 254 days.** Simple behaviors like drinking water were quick; effortful ones like exercise took much longer.
- **Missing a day didn't matter much.** An occasional slip had no meaningful effect on the habit-forming process.

## What happens in the brain

When you first do a new behavior, the **prefrontal cortex**, which handles planning and judgment, is heavily involved. That's why it feels tiring and effortful. As you repeat the same behavior in the same situation, control of the behavior gradually shifts to circuits centered on the **basal ganglia**, especially the **striatum**. These circuits store the link "in this situation, do this," and when the situational cue appears, they bring out the behavior without much deliberation. A habit feeling "automatic" means exactly this state.

The key word here is **situation**. A habit isn't stored as a behavior alone; it is bundled together with the place, the time, and the action that comes right before it. That is why a behavior tied to a fixed situation, like "after breakfast," becomes automatic faster than exercising at a different time each day.

## Today's practice

1. **Drop the time-based goal and choose a situation.** Instead of "for 21 days," attach the new behavior to something that happens every day without fail, like "right after I brew my morning coffee."
2. **Write it as an "if…, then…" sentence.** "If I come back to my desk after lunch, then I drink a glass of water." Many studies show that concrete plans like this make follow-through more likely than goals alone (Gollwitzer, 1999).
3. **Start almost laughably small.** What builds automaticity is not the size of the behavior but the number of repetitions. Two push-ups is fine.
4. **If you miss a day, come back the very next day.** A single slip doesn't break the curve. What breaks it is stopping for days because you think "I've already blown it."
5. **Judge after two months.** If it's still hard in week three, you haven't failed; you're simply on the early part of the curve.
