As phrases like "**dopamine** addiction" and "dopamine detox" have become common, dopamine has come to be known as the "pleasure chemical" released when we feel good. That isn't exactly wrong, but the picture brain research has drawn is far more interesting. What dopamine neurons respond to most clearly is not the reward itself, but **the difference between what we expected and what actually happened**.

## Monkeys and juice

In the 1990s, Wolfram Schultz's team recorded the activity of **dopamine** neurons in the midbrain of monkeys while repeating a simple experiment: a light turns on, and a moment later a drop of juice arrives (Schultz, Dayan & Montague, 1997).

1. **At first,** dopamine neurons fired strongly the moment the juice arrived, because it was an unexpected good thing.
2. **Once the monkeys learned that juice follows the light,** the neurons fired when the light came on rather than when the juice arrived. At the moment of receiving the juice, they barely responded.
3. **When the light came on but no juice followed,** activity actually dropped below normal at the moment the juice should have arrived.

This pattern is explained by the idea of **reward prediction error**. Subtract the prediction from the actual outcome: if the result is positive, activity rises; if it is zero, it stays the same; if it is negative, it falls. Dopamine is closer to a learning signal the brain sends itself, saying "predict more accurately next time."

## "Wanting" and "liking" are different

Kent Berridge's team proposed another distinction. **Wanting** something and actually **liking** it rely on different brain circuits, and **dopamine** is mainly tied to the first: the motivation that moves us toward a reward (Berridge & Robinson, 1998). The experience of endlessly scrolling a feed without really enjoying it fits this distinction well. Wanting is switched on strongly, while liking doesn't follow to the same degree.

## Why short videos and notifications are so powerful

From the perspective of **reward prediction error**, a reward that could arrive at any moment is hard to predict, so it produces a small "surprise" every time. Message notifications and feeds that change with every refresh are classic examples. Short videos repeat this pattern in the smallest possible unit. With every swipe, **you can't know in advance whether the next video will be good**, and that uncertainty creates a fresh "surprise" every few seconds.

Brain imaging adds a clue. A team at Zhejiang University in China scanned university students' brains while they watched **personalized videos** picked by a recommendation algorithm and videos that weren't. Watching the personalized videos, the <strong>ventral tegmental area (VTA)</strong>, where dopamine neurons cluster, and the **default mode network**, which becomes active when the mind wanders, were more active (Su et al., **NeuroImage**, 2021). This study looked at changes in blood flow in the brain, though, not dopamine release itself, and it does not prove that short videos cause addiction. On the other hand, when the same reward comes at the same time every day, the prediction becomes accurate and the response to the reward itself shrinks. Understanding this also makes it clear why "quitting **dopamine**" can't be the goal: dopamine is a signal we need in order to learn and to act. The goal is **choosing which signals to train yourself to respond to**.

## Today's practice

Instead of quitting dopamine, here are **3 steps to change the signals** it responds to.

1. **Make short videos and notifications predictable.** Switch off real-time notifications and autoplay, and set a few fixed times a day for watching short videos and checking messages. When a reward that could come at any moment becomes a set schedule, the prediction gets more accurate, and the pull of the "surprise" weakens with it.
2. **Write down "Did I want it?" and "Did I like it?" separately.** After watching short videos, rate how much you wanted to watch and how much you actually enjoyed it, each from 1 to 5. The moments with strong wanting but low satisfaction will stand out. Those are exactly the places to change.
3. **Put a cue for a good habit, and a small reward, in that spot.** Where you used to pick up your phone, place **a clear cue** such as a book or your workout clothes, and for the first few weeks occasionally add a small treat on days you follow through. When a cue repeats, the brain starts attaching motivation to the cue itself.
